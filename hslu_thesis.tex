\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

This thesis presents a novel game-theoretic framework for merging an autonomous vehicle into a platoon using Distributed Model Predictive Control (DMPC). The merging process is formulated as a non-cooperative Nash game between the autonomous system and the human driver, where cooperative behavior emerges through dynamic authority allocation based on driving risk assessment.

The key contributions of this work include: (1) a bidirectional longitudinal safety field that quantifies collision risk from both leading and following vehicles, (2) an ellipse-based lateral safety field for lane-change scenarios, (3) a Nash equilibrium solver that computes optimal control strategies for both players, and (4) a dynamic authority allocation mechanism that smoothly transitions control from the driver to the autonomous system as risk increases.

The simulation results demonstrate that the proposed approach enables safe and comfortable merging in multiple scenarios, with significant improvements in Time-to-Collision (TTC), jerk minimization, and platoon stability relative to baseline methods.

\textbf{Keywords:} Autonomous vehicles, platoon merging, game theory, Nash equilibrium, DMPC, shared control, safety field, authority allocation

\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Background}

Human-driven and autonomous vehicles will share public roads for decades before full autonomy arrives at scale. Vehicle platooning is a coordinated driving formation in which multiple vehicles travel in close succession under automated spacing control. It offers one of the clearest near-term benefits of this mixed-traffic environment: reduced aerodynamic drag for following vehicles and higher lane throughput per kilometer of road. The difficulty is entering the platoon. A human-driven vehicle that wishes to join an operating platoon must reconcile its driver's intentions with the platoon's automated spacing logic. This thesis develops a game-theoretic shared-control framework, grounded in Nash equilibrium theory, for exactly that problem.


\subsection{Vehicle Platooning}

Vehicle platooning is a driving formation in which multiple vehicles travel closely together at highway speeds, maintaining safe inter-vehicle distances through coordinated control and V2V communication \cite{treiberTrafficFlowDynamics2013}. Aerodynamic drag reduction from traveling in close formation (drafting) reduces fuel consumption by 10\% - 15\% for following vehicles, and smaller following distances allow more vehicles per kilometer of road. Coordinated braking reduces reaction times and collision risk.


Cooperative Adaptive Cruise Control (CACC) is the technology that enables coordinated platoon operation \cite{rajamaniVehicleDynamicsControl2005}. CACC systems exchange speed, acceleration, and braking intentions via Vehicle-to-Vehicle (V2V) links, allowing vehicles to respond faster than human reaction times permit. The desired inter-vehicle gap follows the Constant Time Gap (CTG) policy:
\begin{equation}
d_{\text{des}} = d_0 + h \cdot v
\label{eq:ctg_spacing_v2}
\end{equation}
where $d_0 = 2$\,m is the standstill distance, $h = 1.5$\,s is the time headway, and $v$ is the current vehicle speed. The gap therefore increases linearly with speed, providing appropriate safety margins under all operating conditions.

Most CACC systems assume that all platoon members are fully autonomous. This assumption fails in mixed traffic, where a human-driven vehicle must negotiate entry into an operating platoon. Safe merging requires explicit handling of both the human driver's intent and the platoon's automated spacing logic, a problem neither conventional CACC nor standard autonomous control addresses directly.


\subsection{Shared Control Systems}

Shared control divides driving authority between a human operator and an automation system \cite{liSharedControlNovel2019}. Unlike full autonomy or purely manual operation, shared control maintains driver engagement, provides safety assistance proportional to risk, and enables smooth transitions of authority. The core engineering challenge is computing an authority split that is safe when risk is high and minimally intrusive when risk is low.

Li et al.\ \cite{liSharedControlNovel2019} proposed mapping the instantaneous driving risk, quantified by a safety field potential, to an authority allocation ratio. This thesis adopts and extends that mapping to the platoon-merging problem, covering both longitudinal gap control and lateral lane-change execution.


\subsection{Game Theory for Vehicle Control}

Game theory provides a principled framework for systems with multiple decision-makers whose objectives are coupled but not identical \cite{basarDynamicNoncooperativeGame1999}. In the shared-control context, the human driver and the autonomous system are modeled as two players in a noncooperative differential game. Each player selects a control sequence that minimizes their own cost, given the other player's action. The Nash equilibrium is the joint strategy from which neither player benefits from deviating unilaterally.

For two players with quadratic cost functions over a finite horizon, the Nash equilibrium is computed via Distributed Model Predictive Control (DMPC). Player 1 (the autonomous system) minimizes:
\begin{equation}
J_1 = \sum_{k=0}^{N_p-1} \bigl\|\mathbf{z}[k] - \mathbf{r}_1[k]\bigr\|_{Q_1}^2 + \bigl\|u_1[k]\bigr\|_{R_1}^2
\label{eq:cost_j1_intro}
\end{equation}
Player 2 (the human driver) minimizes:
\begin{equation}
J_2 = \sum_{k=0}^{N_p-1} \bigl\|\mathbf{z}[k] - \mathbf{r}_2[k]\bigr\|_{Q_2(\lambda)}^2 + \bigl\|u_2[k]\bigr\|_{R_2}^2
\label{eq:cost_j2_intro}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{z}$ is the system output, $\mathbf{r}_i$ is each player's reference trajectory,
$Q_i$ weights output tracking errors, and $R_i$ weights each player's own control effort.
The authority ratio $\lambda$ enters via $Q_2(\lambda) = \alpha(\lambda)\,Q_1$,
where $\alpha = 1/(1+\lambda)$. As $\lambda$ increases, the human driver's tracking weight
is attenuated relative to Player~1, and the iterative best-response loop converges to a
solution in which $u_2^*$ approaches the system reference.

\subsection{Lane Change Trajectory Planning}

Platoon entry requires a lateral lane change coordinated with a longitudinal gap adjustment. The vehicle must move from its current lane into the platoon lane while avoiding collisions with platoon members.

A quintic (fifth-order) polynomial trajectory is used to generate smooth, real-time lane-change references \cite{guHumanlikeMotionPlanning2014}. The trajectory has zero velocity and acceleration at both endpoints and a continuous jerk profile. The normalized lateral progress is:
\begin{equation}
s(\tau) = 10\tau^3 - 15\tau^4 + 6\tau^5
\label{eq:quintic_polynomial}
\end{equation}
where $\tau = t / T_{lc}$ is the normalized time and $T_{lc}$ is the lane-change duration. The polynomial yields a closed-form solution that requires no iterations, making it suitable for real-time execution at 20\, Hz.

The lane-change duration $T_{lc}$ is computed dynamically from the heading angle constraint
$|\psi| \leq \psi_{\max}$. Since $\psi \approx \dot{y}/v_x$ for small angles, the minimum
permissible duration for the autonomous system's trajectory is:
\begin{equation}
T_{lc,\text{sys}} = \frac{1.875 \cdot |\Delta y|}{v_x \cdot \tan(\psi_{\max})}
\label{eq:T_lc_min}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta y = |y_{\text{platoon}} - y_{\text{ego}}(t_0)|$ is the lateral offset at the
moment the maneuver is initiated, $v_x$ is the longitudinal speed, and the factor 1.875
is derived from the maximum of the derivative of the quintic polynomial. A relaxed duration is assigned
to the human driver's trajectory:
\begin{equation}
T_{lc,\text{hum}} = \frac{1.5 \cdot |\Delta y|}{v_x \cdot \tan(\psi_{\max})}
\label{eq:T_lc_hum}
\end{equation}
The factor 1.5 accommodates the less constrained heading excursion typical of human
lane changes \cite{toledoModelingDurationLane2007, leeComprehensiveExaminationNaturalistic2004a}.
Both durations are subject to a minimum floor $T_{\min}$, computed as the time required to
bring the current lateral velocity, heading angle, and yaw rate to zero, with an absolute
lower bound of $4.0$\,s. By design, the system trajectory is longer than the human trajectory.
With $\psi_{\max} = 4^\circ$, $\Delta y = 3.5$\,m (full lane change from lane
center), and $v_x = 20$\,m/s, the constraints yield $T_{lc,\text{sys}} = 4.7$\,s and
$T_{lc,\text{hum}} = 3.75$\,s.

\subsection{Safety Field Theory}

The autonomous system requires a continuous quantitative measure of collision risk to
decide when intervention is warranted. Safety field theory, introduced by Wang \emph{et al.}
\cite{wangDrivingSafetyField2015, wangDrivingSafetyField2016} and extended to an elliptic form by
Li \emph{et al.} \cite{liSharedControlNovel2019}, provides such a measure by mapping each obstacle onto a scalar potential whose gradient defines a force on the ego vehicle.

The classical decomposition is retained:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{E}_s = \mathbf{E}_p + \mathbf{E}_v + \mathbf{E}_D
\label{eq:safety_field_intro}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{E}_p$ is the potential field from static obstacles and road
boundaries, $\mathbf{E}_v$ is the kinetic field from moving obstacles, and $\mathbf{E}_D$
is the behavior field reflecting the driver risk factor. All three components are
constructed in the elliptic form of Li \emph{et al.}, in which the isotropic distance of
the original Wang formulation is replaced by an elliptic radius whose tangential axis
stretches with the closing speed. This encodes the empirical observation that a driver
requires a larger longitudinal clearance than lateral clearance to complete an avoidance
maneuver.

Two safety fields are constructed in parallel. The \emph{lateral safety field} governs the
lane-change task and evaluates the full field force $\mathbf{F}^{\text{lat}}$ following
Li~\emph{et al.} Eq.~21, with an additional pair of $1/\mathrm{dist}$ repulsion terms at
the lane edges to keep the ego vehicle inside the roadway. The \emph{longitudinal safety
field} governs the gap-regulation task and reduces the same decomposition to the potential
component alone, expressed in the gap-error coordinate with distance and velocity weights
that subsume the kinetic and behavior contributions. Both fields use a signed convention
in which positive values are repulsive and negative values are attractive, and both are
smoothed by an exponential moving average filter that adapts the parameters continuously
to the current traffic state.

Two extensions are made to the classical single-obstacle formulation for the platoon-merging
problem. First, the longitudinal risk assessment is bidirectional: the total force combines
a leader term with a weighted follower term \cite{wangDrivingSafetyField2015}, so that both the
vehicle ahead and the vehicle behind contribute to the authority signal. The follower weight
is set to $0.5$, reflecting the asymmetric information flow characteristic of
predecessor-following topologies in cooperative adaptive cruise control. Second, a phase
machine with four discrete states (APPROACH, GAP SEARCH, MERGE, and FOLLOWING) gates
the safety response so that aggressive gap closing during MERGE gives way to a comfort-oriented
soft transition during FOLLOWING. The follower term is never faded, since rear-end safety
must remain unconditional at all phases.

\section{Problem Statement}

This thesis addresses the safe merging of a human-driven vehicle into an autonomous
platoon. The merging vehicle travels in an adjacent lane and must enter the platoon at
a designated gap without colliding with the leader or the follower. The problem is
decomposed into two subproblems, each solved within the same Nash/DMPC framework.


\subsection{Longitudinal Control Problem}

Longitudinal control regulates the vehicle's forward motion to close the target gap,
reach the correct inter-vehicle distance, and maintain it in steady state. The controller
tracks a constant-time-gap reference with standstill distance $d_0 = 2$~m and time
headway $h = 1.5$~s, respects the follower constraint arising from the vehicle behind,
limits jerk to preserve passenger comfort, and stays within the physical acceleration
envelope of the vehicle. Execution is required at $10$~Hz to close the outer control
loop of the platoon in real time.

The merging scenario places a large initial gap error under a moving follower, so the
controller must close the gap without triggering an emergency response in the trailing
vehicle. Entry before, within, or after the platoon each presents a different risk
profile and a different sign of the follower force.

\subsection{Lateral Control Problem}

Lateral control guides the vehicle from its own lane into the platoon lane along a
smooth quintic reference. Contact with platoon members is forbidden throughout the
maneuver; lateral acceleration and heading angle are kept within comfort bounds, and
the lane-change timing is coordinated with the longitudinal controller. Execution is
required at $20$~Hz.

Lateral dynamics is governed by the single-track bicycle model, whose response depends
on vehicle speed and tire properties. Because the linearization is only accurate near
the operating point, the lateral Nash solver rebuilds the state-space matrices whenever
the longitudinal speed drifts by more than $2$~m/s from the last operating point.

\subsection{Authority Allocation Problem}

Authority allocation determines how the Nash-optimal system control $u_1^{*}$ and the
Nash-optimal human control $u_2^{*}$ are combined into the command sent to the vehicle.
The shared output is
\begin{equation}
u_{\text{shared}} = u_1^{*} + u_2^{*}
\label{eq:shared_control_intro}
\end{equation}
where $u_1^{*}$ is the first-step optimal control of the autonomous system and $u_2^{*}$
is the first-step optimal control of the human player at the current Nash step.
The two controls are summed directly rather than blended by a convex combination.
Authority balance is encoded inside the Nash cost function rather than applied as an
outer weighting rule \cite{pustilnikGeneralizedNashEquilibrium2025}.

The authority balance is governed by the scalar weight
\begin{equation}
\alpha = \frac{1}{1 + \lambda}
\label{eq:alpha_lambda}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha \in (0,1]$ is the authority weight derived from the safety field force,
and $\lambda \geq 0$ is the authority ratio. The weight $\alpha$ enters the Nash
quadratic program by scaling the tracking-cost Hessian of Player~2:
\begin{equation}
P_2 = 2\bigl(\alpha\, H^{\top}Q_1 H + R_2 I\bigr)
\label{eq:P2_alpha}
\end{equation}
where $H$ is the input-to-output prediction matrix, $Q_1$ is the shared tracking weight
matrix, $R_2$ is the human player's control-effort weight, and $I$ is the identity matrix.
A reduction in $\alpha$ attenuates the tracking Hessian $H^{\top}Q_1 H$ relative to the
effort term $R_2 I$. Consequently, $u_2^*$ is driven toward zero and $u_1^*$ is allowed
to dominate $u_{\text{shared}}$. Under nominal risk ($\lambda = 0$, $\alpha = 1$) the human
player's cost is unmodified and full authority is retained. Under maximal risk
($\lambda \to \infty$, $\alpha \to 0$) the tracking Hessian vanishes and
$u_2^* \to 0$, so $u_{\text{shared}} \approx u_1^*$. The value of $\lambda$ is
determined at each control step from the safety field force computed in the preceding
step.

\section{Research Objectives}

This thesis pursues the following objectives.

The first objective is to implement a real-time Nash equilibrium solver. Two
independent solvers are constructed, one for longitudinal control and one for
lateral control, each formulated as a separate two-player differential game with no
coupling between them. Both solvers are based on an Iterative Best Response (IBR)
algorithm with a per-player OSQP backend, and each pre-factorizes its cost matrices
for a discrete set of authority-ratio levels (eleven levels for the longitudinal
solver and seven for the lateral solver) so that the closest level can be selected
without rebuilding the quadratic program. Warm-starting from the shifted horizon
of the previous solution is applied at every control step. The target execution
rates are $10$\, Hz for longitudinal control and $20$\, Hz for lateral control.

The second objective is to implement the $\alpha$-scaling authority mechanism of
Pustilnik and Borrelli \cite{pustilnikGeneralizedNashEquilibrium2025}. The authority ratio $\lambda$ is mapped to a scalar weight $\alpha = 1/(1+\lambda)$ that multiplies the tracking cost
of Player~2 in the Nash quadratic program, so that the safety-field-driven
authority balance is encoded inside the Nash cost function rather than applied as
an outer blending rule on the shared control.

The third objective is to develop phase-aware safety field models. A four-state
phase machine (APPROACH, GAP SEARCH, MERGE, and FOLLOWING) is introduced, with
transition conditions derived from gap error, relative velocity, and lateral
alignment. The safety response is aggressive during MERGE to close the gap, and
attenuated during FOLLOWING to prioritize passenger comfort. A dwell-time
requirement on entry into FOLLOWING prevents chattering at phase boundaries.


The fourth objective is to develop a bidirectional longitudinal safety field. The
field quantifies risk from both the preceding vehicle and the following vehicle,
with asymmetric weighting that reflects the forward-dominant information flow of
predecessor-following topologies.

The fifth objective is to design an elliptic lateral safety field. A lateral field
based on the Li \emph{et al.} \cite{liSharedControlNovel2019} elliptic \emph{driving safety
field} is derived, with velocity-corrected kinetic terms, road-boundary repulsion,
and a $\tanh$ sign function that avoids force discontinuities at lateral alignment.

The sixth objective is to develop an online driver-calibration mechanism. The human
driver's time headway $T$ is estimated online: during gap-closing by inverting the
Intelligent Driver Model (IDM), and during following by direct gap measurement.
Estimates are smoothed over a twenty-sample circular buffer (approximately $2$\,s
at the $10$\,Hz Nash rate) and clipped to the physiological range
$T \in [0.5, 3.0]$\,s, with a population-mean fallback of $T = 1.5$\,s until the
buffer is full.

The seventh objective is to implement a common architecture for the two independent
controllers. The longitudinal and lateral solvers share the same Nash/IBR structure,
the same phase-detection logic, and the same authority-allocation mechanism, and
both are implemented in Unity with a C\# wrapper for the OSQP solver.

The eighth objective is to validate the system through simulation. Three merging
scenarios are tested (joining before, within, and after the platoon) with
metrics covering safety (gap error, time-to-collision (TTC)), comfort (longitudinal
jerk, lateral acceleration), and shared-control behavior (authority distribution
over time).

\section{Thesis Contributions}

This thesis makes the following contributions.

The first contribution is an IBR Nash equilibrium solver with $\lambda$-cached OSQP
instances. The Nash game is solved by iterative best response, with a separate OSQP
quadratic program per player. Cost matrices are pre-factorized for eleven
longitudinal $\lambda$ levels and seven lateral $\lambda$ levels, and the closest
level is selected at each control step by minimizing $|\ln\hat{\lambda} - \ln\lambda_k|$. Horizon-shift warm-starting reduces the OSQP iteration count, meeting the real-time budgets of the $10$\, Hz and $20$\, Hz control loops. The longitudinal and lateral solvers are constructed independently, with no cross-coupling between their state or control variables.

The second contribution is the direct-sum shared-control formulation with
$\alpha$-scaled Nash costs. The shared control is computed as $u_{\text{shared}} = u_1^{*} + u_2^{*}$ without an outer convex combination, since the authority balance is absorbed into the Player~2 cost through the $\alpha$-scaling of Pustilnik and Borrelli \cite{pustilnikGeneralizedNashEquilibrium2025}, where $\alpha = 1/(1+\lambda)$ multiplies the tracking-weight term of Player~2. The resulting equilibrium is shown to converge to a cooperative solution across the full range $\lambda \in [0, \infty)$, without the oscillatory instabilities that arise when authority is applied as an outer weighted sum on independent single-player controllers.

The third contribution is a phase-detection mechanism with dwell-time locking. Entry
into FOLLOWING requires the normalized gap error $|\Delta g| / d_{\text{des}}$ to
fall below $0.10$, the velocity error $|v_{\text{ego}} - v_{\text{leader}}|$ to fall
below $0.5$\,m/s, and the gap-error rate $|\dot{(\Delta g)}|$ to fall below
$0.3$\,m/s, with all conditions sustained for $5$\,s. A quadratic soft-transition
factor further attenuates the safety field force as the gap error decreases within
the FOLLOWING-band threshold ($0.15\, d_{\text{des}}$), preventing abrupt changes in
control demand at the phase boundary.

The fourth contribution of this thesis is a bidirectional longitudinal safety field that
combines an elliptic leader force \cite{liSharedControlNovel2019} with a
time-to-collision (TTC) force floor and a follower-weighted rear force.
The TTC floor is defined as
\begin{equation}
F_{\text{TTC}} = \text{ttc}_{\text{frac}} \cdot F_{\max}, \qquad
\text{ttc}_{\text{frac}} = 1 - \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\tfrac{\mathrm{TTC} - \mathrm{TTC}_{\text{crit}}}{\mathrm{TTC}_{\text{warn}} - \mathrm{TTC}_{\text{crit}}}, 0, 1\right)
\label{eq:ttc_floor}
\end{equation}
where $F_{\max} = 800$\,N is the saturation force, $\mathrm{TTC}_{\text{warn}} = 5.5$\,s
is the TTC threshold at which the floor begins to rise (Urgency~2 upper boundary,
Lee~\cite{hsAnalysesRearEndCrashes2007}), and $\mathrm{TTC}_{\text{crit}} = 3.0$\,s is the TTC at
which the floor reaches saturation (Urgency~3 ``forced'' threshold,
Lee~\cite{hsAnalysesRearEndCrashes2007}).
The leader force is saturated whenever the bumper-to-bumper gap falls below the minimum
safe distance, defined as
\begin{equation}
d_{\min}(v_x) = \max\!\bigl(d_{\min}^{\text{floor}},\; \tau_{\min} \cdot v_x\bigr)
\label{eq:d_min}
\end{equation}
where $d_{\min}^{\text{floor}} = 5$\,m is the low-speed floor, $\tau_{\min} = 1.0$\,s is
the critical time headway as defined by Wang~\emph{et al.}~\cite{wangDrivingSafetyField2016},
$v_x$ is the ego longitudinal speed, and $w_f = 0.5$ is the weighting coefficient applied
to the rear-vehicle contribution at steady state.
A linear interpolation region of width $1$\,m above $d_{\min}(v_x)$ is introduced, over
which the force rises from zero to $F_{\max}$ as the gap closes.
At $v_x = 20$\,m/s this yields $d_{\min} = 20$\,m and an emergency boundary at $21$\,m.

The fifth contribution is an elliptic lateral safety field. The field is derived
from the Li \emph{et al.} \cite{liSharedControlNovel2019} elliptic \emph{driving safety
field}, with a Wang \emph{et al.}\ \cite{wangDrivingSafetyField2015, wangDrivingSafetyField2016}
speed-dependent virtual mass, velocity-corrected kinetic terms with
$K_1 = K_2 = 0.005$, and a $\tanh$ smoothing function with $\sigma = 0.5$\,m for the
lateral force direction. Road-boundary repulsion is added via a
$1/\mathrm{dist}$ potential active within $3$\,m of the lane edge.

The sixth contribution is an online IDM driver-calibration method. During
gap-closing, the human's time headway is estimated by inverting the IDM formula
given the observed gap, velocity, and acceleration. During steady-state following,
it is estimated directly as $T = (d - s_0) / v_x$, where $d$ is the measured gap,
$s_0$ is the standstill distance, and $v_x$ is the ego longitudinal velocity. A
twenty-sample circular buffer smooths the estimate, which converges within
approximately $2$\,s to a driver-specific value used to personalize both the human
reference trajectory and the $R_2$ weight schedule.

The seventh contribution is a Unity-based simulation framework. The complete system
is implemented in Unity with the Universal Render Pipeline and a C\#
wrapper for the OSQP solver. The vehicle plant is a nonlinear six-degree-of-freedom
model \cite{belousovDevelopmentVehicleSimulation} integrated by fourth-order Runge--Kutta at $100$\, Hz, with a Rajamani parabolic tire model calibrated to Audi TT Coup\'{e} parameters.
Per-tick telemetry is logged to CSV files and analyzed by a Python post-processing
pipeline.

\section{Thesis Organization}

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows.

\textbf{Chapter~2: Mathematical Modeling} presents the vehicle-dynamics models used
throughout the thesis. A distinction is drawn between the plant used for simulation, 
a nonlinear six-degree-of-freedom vehicle model integrated by fourth-order
Runge--Kutta at $100$\, Hz  and the reduced models seen by the controllers: a
double-integrator for the longitudinal Nash solver and a two-degree-of-freedom

\textbf{Chapter~3: Safety Field Formulations} describes the risk-quantification

\chapter{Mathematical Modeling}

\section{Introduction}

This chapter describes the vehicle-dynamics models used throughout the thesis. A distinction is drawn between the \emph{plant model}, which governs the simulation, and the \emph{controller models}, which the Nash solvers use for prediction.

The plant is a nonlinear six-degree-of-freedom model derived from Belousov
\cite{belousovDevelopmentVehicleSimulation}. It integrates the coupled longitudinal, lateral, and yaw dynamics by fourth-order Runge Kutta at \SI{100}{Hz}, with a
parabolic tire force law (Rajamani \cite{rajamaniVehicleDynamicsControl2005},
Eq.~13.45) calibrated to the Audi TT Coup\'{e} 2.0~TFSI. This model constitutes the
ground truth against which all control outputs are evaluated.

The Nash solvers operate on reduced linear models that are rebuit at each control step
from the current operating point. The longitudinal solver uses a double-integrator
state-space model with state $[x,\, v_x]^\top$, discretized at \SI{10}{Hz}. The
lateral solver uses a two-degree-of-freedom bicycle model with state
$[y,\, \dot{y},\, \psi,\, \dot{\psi}]^\top$, discretized at \SI{20}{Hz}. Both
controller models are derived by linearizing the plant around the current speed and
applying a Zero-Order Hold (ZOH) discretization.

Section~\ref{sec:plant} presents the plant model. Sections~\ref{sec:long_model}
and~\ref{sec:lat_model} derive the two controller models. Section~\ref{sec:multirate}
describes the multi-rate timing architecture.

\section{Plant Model}
\label{sec:plant}

The simulation plant is a nonlinear six-degree-of-freedom (6-DOF) vehicle model
derived from Belousov \cite{belousovDevelopmentVehicleSimulation}. The state vector
is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{s} = \bigl[x,\; v_x,\; y,\; v_y,\; \psi,\; \dot\psi\bigr]^\top
\label{eq:plant_state}
\end{equation}
where $x$ is the longitudinal position, $v_x$ the longitudinal speed, $y$ the
lateral position, $v_y$ the body-frame lateral velocity, $\psi$ the yaw angle, and
$\dot\psi$ the yaw rate. All quantities are expressed in the vehicle body frame.

\subsection{Equations of Motion}

The equations of motion follow Belousov Eqs.~3.11a--c:
\begin{align}
m_{\text{eff}}\,\dot v_x &= F_{x,c}\cos\delta - F_{yf}\sin\delta - F_{x,r}
\label{eq:plant_long} \\
m\,\dot v_y              &= F_{yf}\cos\delta + F_{yr} + F_{xf,c}\sin\delta
                            - f_v\,v_x\dot\psi
\label{eq:plant_lat} \\
I_z\,\ddot\psi           &= l_f\bigl(F_{yf}\cos\delta + F_{xf,c}\sin\delta\bigr)
                            - l_r F_{yr}
\label{eq:plant_yaw}
\end{align}
where $F_{x,c}$ is the net contact-patch longitudinal force, $F_{x,r}$ collects
body-level resistances (aerodynamic drag, rolling resistance, road grade), $F_{xf,c}$
is the front-axle contact force, $\delta$ is the front-wheel steer angle, $m$ is the
vehicle mass, and $m_{\text{eff}} = m + I_w/r^2$ (Belousov Eq.~3.8) augments the
inertia with the rotational inertia of the driven wheels referred to the vehicle body.
The factor $f_v = \tfrac{1}{2}(\tanh(10|v_x|-8)+1)$ gates the centripetal coupling
terms so that they decay smoothly to zero near standstill, where the body-frame
velocity states lose physical meaning.

The world-frame lateral position and its rate are recovered by the kinematic relations
\begin{equation}
\dot y = -(v_x\sin\psi + v_y\cos\psi), \qquad
y = x_0 - x
\label{eq:plant_world_y}
\end{equation}
where $x_0$ is the lateral spawn coordinate.

\subsection{Tire Model}

Lateral tire forces are computed from the parabolic pressure-distribution model of
Rajamani \cite{rajamaniVehicleDynamicsControl2005}, Eq.~13.45:
\begin{equation}
F_y(\alpha) =
\begin{cases}
\mu F_z\!\left(3\theta\tan\alpha - 3\theta^2\tan^2\!\alpha + \theta^3\tan^3\!\alpha\right)
  & |\tan\alpha| \leq 1/\theta \\
\operatorname{sgn}(\alpha)\,\mu F_z & |\tan\alpha| > 1/\theta
\end{cases}
\label{eq:tire_parabolic}
\end{equation}
where $\theta = C_\alpha/(3\mu F_z)$, $C_\alpha$ is the per-wheel cornering
stiffness, $\mu = 0.8$ is the road friction coefficient, $F_z$ is the wheel normal
load, and $\alpha$ is the tire slip angle. The model recovers a linear
force law $F_y \approx C_\alpha\alpha$ for small $\alpha$ and saturates at
$\mu F_z$ under full sliding.

The Audi TT Coup\'{e} 2.0~TFSI is front-wheel-drive with a 62/38 front-to-rear mass
distribution. Applying the Pacejka rule ($C_\alpha \approx 9.2\,F_{z,\text{wheel}}$)
to the nominal axle loads gives asymmetric per-wheel cornering stiffnesses:
\begin{equation}
C_{af} = 36\,500\ \text{N/rad}, \qquad C_{ar} = 22\,300\ \text{N/rad}
\label{eq:tire_stiffness}
\end{equation}
These values are consistent with the compact FWD range reported in Rajamani
\cite{rajamaniVehicleDynamicsControl2005}, Ch.~2.

\subsection{Numerical Integration}

The 6-DOF state is advanced by fourth-order Runge Kutta (RK4) at \SI{100}{Hz}
($\Delta t_{\text{sim}} = 0.01$~s). RK4 is chosen over Euler integration because the
stiff tire-yaw coupling in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:plant_lat}--\eqref{eq:plant_yaw} causes
first-order methods to diverge at this step size for the vehicle parameters used.

\subsection{Linearization for Nash Solvers}

At each Nash control step, the plant provides linearized Jacobians that the solvers
use to build their prediction models. The longitudinal Jacobian (method
\texttt{GetLongJacobian}) linearizes Eq.~\eqref{eq:plant_long} around the current
operating point ($\delta \approx 0$, $\dot\psi \approx 0$) with feedforward
cancellation of drag and rolling resistance, yielding the double-integrator structure
described in Section~\ref{sec:long_model}. The lateral Jacobian (method
\texttt{GetLatJacobian}) linearizes Eqs.~\eqref{eq:plant_lat}--\eqref{eq:plant_yaw}
around straight driving, yielding the bicycle-model structure described in
Section~\ref{sec:lat_model}. Both Jacobians are recomputed at every solver invocation
to track changes in $v_x$.

\section{Longitudinal Controller Model}
\label{sec:long_model}

The longitudinal Nash solver operates on a linearized double-integrator model
obtained from \texttt{GetLongJacobian()} at each control step. The state vector is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_{\text{long}} = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ v_x \end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:long_state}
\end{equation}
where $x$ is the longitudinal position and $v_x$ is the longitudinal speed.

\subsection{Continuous-Time Representation}

Linearizing Eq.~\eqref{eq:plant_long} around straight driving ($\delta \approx 0$,
$\dot\psi \approx 0$) with feedforward cancellation of drag and rolling resistance
yields
\begin{equation}
\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{long}} = A_c\,\mathbf{x}_{\text{long}} + B_c\,u
\label{eq:long_continuous}
\end{equation}
where the control input $u = a_{\text{des}}$ is the desired longitudinal acceleration
in m/s² and
\begin{equation}
A_c = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -d_{\text{lin}} \end{bmatrix}, \quad
B_c = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:long_matrices_continuous}
\end{equation}
The term $d_{\text{lin}} = \rho C_d A_f |v_x| / m_{\text{eff}}$ is the
linearized aerodynamic drag coefficient at the current speed. At highway speeds
($v_x = 20$~m/s) this evaluates to $d_{\text{lin}} \approx 0.02$~s$^{-1}$, which is
small relative to the closed-loop bandwidth; the model therefore reduces to the
standard double integrator $A_c = \bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$
to good approximation.

The output equation is:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{y} = C\,\mathbf{x}_{\text{long}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\mathbf{x}_{\text{long}}
\label{eq:long_output}
\end{equation}
so both position and velocity are available as outputs for Nash cost evaluation.

\subsection{Discrete-Time Representation}

Zero-Order Hold (ZOH) discretization at $\Delta t = 0.1$~s yields
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_{\text{long}}[k+1] = A_d\,\mathbf{x}_{\text{long}}[k] + B_d\,u[k]
\label{eq:long_discrete}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
A_d = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0.1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
B_d = \begin{bmatrix} 0.005 \\ 0.1 \end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:Ad_Bd_10Hz}
\end{equation}
These matrices correspond to the kinematic updates
\begin{align}
z[k+1]   &= z[k] + v_x[k]\,\Delta t + \tfrac{1}{2}\,u[k]\,\Delta t^2
\label{eq:pos_update} \\
v_x[k+1] &= v_x[k] + u[k]\,\Delta t
\label{eq:vel_update}
\end{align}

\subsection{Control Constraints}
\label{sec:long_constraints}

Two levels of constraints are enforced by the Nash solver. At the first level, wide solver
ceilings are imposed as box constraints on the OSQP quadratic program:
\begin{equation}
u \in [-20,\; 10]\ \text{m/s}^2, \qquad
|\Delta u| \leq 15\ \text{m/s}^2\text{/step}
\label{eq:long_solver_box}
\end{equation}
where $u$ is the commanded longitudinal acceleration, and $\Delta u = u[k] - u[k-1]$ is
the inter-step increment. These bounds serve as numerical guardrails rather than comfort
limits; physical saturation is addressed by the second level.

At the second level, physical bounds are computed at each control step from the
current vehicle state via \texttt{UpdatePhysicalBounds()}, which clips the commanded
acceleration to the envelope that the powertrain and brake system can actually deliver.

For the human player, the inter-step rate is bounded by
\begin{equation}
|\Delta u_2| \leq 4.0\ \text{m/s}^2\text{/step}
\quad \longrightarrow \quad
0.4\ \text{m/s}^2\text{/s at } 10\,\text{Hz}
\label{eq:long_human_jerk}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta u_2 = u_2[k] - u_2[k-1]$ is the inter-step change in the human player's
optimal control. Inter-step oscillations in $u_2^*$ are thereby suppressed while the
Nash game's freedom to assign a nonzero human control is preserved. Comfort shaping beyond
this hard cap is handled through the $R_2$ weight schedule described in
Chapter~\ref{ch:nash_framework}.

\section{Lateral Vehicle Model}
\label{sec:lat_vehicle_model}

\subsection{The Bicycle Model}

The lateral dynamics of the ego vehicle are described by the standard single-track bicycle
model \cite{rajamaniVehicleDynamicsControl2005}. The equations of motion follow from Newton's second law
for lateral translation and yaw rotation:
\begin{align}
m \ddot{y}_{\text{b}} &= F_{yf} + F_{yr} \label{eq:lat_force} \\
I_z \ddot{\psi}        &= l_f F_{yf} - l_r F_{yr} \label{eq:yaw_moment}
\end{align}
where $m$ is the vehicle mass, $I_z$ is the yaw moment of inertia, $F_{yf}$ and $F_{yr}$
are the lateral tire forces at the front and rear axles, $l_f$ is the distance from the
centre of gravity to the front axle, and $l_r$ is the distance from the center of gravity
to the rear axle.

The plant model integrates these equations using a load-dependent nonlinear tire force
function. For the Nash solver, which requires a linear state-space model for QP
construction, the equations of motion are linearized at the current longitudinal
velocity $v_x$.

\subsection{State Variables}

The state vector is defined in the global frame as
$\mathbf{x} = [y,\; \dot{y}_{\text{b}},\; \psi,\; \dot{\psi}]^{\top}$,
where $y$ is the global lateral position, $\dot{y}_{\text{b}}$ is the body-frame lateral
velocity, $\psi$ is the heading angle, and $\dot{\psi}$ is the yaw rate. The global lateral
position is related to the body-frame lateral velocity by
\begin{equation}
\dot{y} \approx \dot{y}_{\text{b}} + v_x \psi
\label{eq:global_y}
\end{equation}
where the small-angle approximation $\sin\psi \approx \psi$ holds for the heading angles
encountered during controlled lane changes.

\subsection{Global-Frame Bicycle Model}

Under the small-angle assumption, the axle tire forces are expressed in terms of
per-wheel cornering stiffness $C_{af}$ (front) and $C_{ar}$ (rear):
\begin{align}
F_{yf} &= -2 C_{af} \alpha_f \label{eq:front_tire} \\
F_{yr} &= -2 C_{ar} \alpha_r \label{eq:rear_tire}
\end{align}
where the factor of two converts per-wheel stiffness to the full-axle value, and the
slip angles are
\begin{align}
\alpha_f &= \frac{\dot{y}_{\text{b}} + l_f \dot{\psi}}{v_x} - \delta \label{eq:front_slip} \\
\alpha_r &= \frac{\dot{y}_{\text{b}} - l_r \dot{\psi}}{v_x} \label{eq:rear_slip}
\end{align}
where $\delta$ is the front wheel steering angle. These expressions are substituted into
the equations of motion, and the kinematic relation $\dot{y} \approx \dot{y}_{\text{b}} + v_x\psi$
from Eq.~\eqref{eq:global_y} is applied, yielding the continuous-time state-space
representation
\begin{equation}
\dot{\mathbf{x}} = A_c(v_x)\, \mathbf{x} + B_c\, \delta
\label{eq:lat_ss_continuous}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{x}$ is the state vector and $\delta$ is the steering input. The
velocity-dependent state matrix is
\begin{equation}
A_c = \begin{bmatrix}
0 & 1 & v_x & 0 \\[6pt]
0 & -\dfrac{2(C_{af}+C_{ar})}{m v_x} & 0 &
    \dfrac{2(l_r C_{ar} - l_f C_{af})}{m v_x} - v_x \\[6pt]
0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\[6pt]
0 & \dfrac{2(l_r C_{ar} - l_f C_{af})}{I_z v_x} & 0 &
   -\dfrac{2(l_f^2 C_{af} + l_r^2 C_{ar})}{I_z v_x}
\end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:Ac_lat}
\end{equation}
and the input matrix is
\begin{equation}
B_c = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\[4pt] \dfrac{2 C_{af}}{m} \\[4pt] 0 \\[4pt]
\dfrac{2 l_f C_{af}}{I_z} \end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:Bc_lat}
\end{equation}
where $m$ is the vehicle mass, $I_z$ is the yaw moment of inertia, $l_f$ and $l_r$
are the distances from the center of gravity to the front and rear axles, respectively,
and $v_x$ is the longitudinal speed at the current linearization point. The
$(1,3)$ entry of $A_c$ equals $v_x$, not zero. This term arises directly from the
kinematic identity $\dot{y} = \dot{y}_{\text{b}} + v_x\psi$: differentiating the global
lateral position $y$ introduces a coupling between the heading angle $\psi$ and the
global lateral velocity $\dot{y}$ that is proportional to $v_x$. Because $A_c$ depends
on $v_x$, the model constitutes a linear parameter-varying (LPV) system. The matrices
are recomputed, and the OSQP prediction matrices rebuilt, whenever the longitudinal
speed deviates by more than $2\;\mathrm{m/s}$ from the last linearization point.

\subsection{Discrete-Time Representation}

The continuous-time matrices are converted to discrete time by zero-order hold (ZOH)
discretisation at the Nash control period $\Delta t_{\mathrm{lat}} = 0.05\;\mathrm{s}$
(20~Hz), with the matrix exponential evaluated numerically. Between Nash updates, the
most recent steering command is held constant.

\subsection{Vehicle Parameters}

\cite{rajamaniVehicleDynamicsControl2005}. The parameters are listed in
Table~\ref{tab:vehicle_params_ch2}.

every fifth simulation step. At the nominal velocity of 20~m/s, the dominant yaw time
constant of the bicycle model is approximately $0.15$--$0.3\;\mathrm{s}$, and the 20~Hz
rate provides three to six control updates per time constant, which is sufficient for
accurate trajectory tracking.

\section{Human Driver Models}
\label{sec:human_driver}

\subsection{Longitudinal: Online IDM Calibration}

The human driver's longitudinal reference trajectory is generated by the Intelligent
Driver Model (IDM) \cite{treiberCongestedTrafficStates2000}, with parameters calibrated online to
the observed driver behavior. The IDM acceleration is:
\begin{equation}
a_{\text{IDM}} = a_{\max} \left[ 1 - \left( \frac{v}{v_0} \right)^\delta
- \left( \frac{s^*(v, \Delta v)}{s} \right)^2 \right]
\label{eq:idm}
\end{equation}
where $a_{\max}$ is the maximum acceleration, $v$ is the current velocity, $v_0$ is the
desired velocity, $s$ is the current gap, $\delta = 4$ is the acceleration exponent, and
$s^*$ is the desired gap:
\begin{equation}
s^*(v, \Delta v) = s_0 + v T + \frac{v \Delta v}{2\sqrt{a_{\max} b}}
\label{eq:idm_desired_gap}
\end{equation}
where $s_0$ is the minimum standstill gap, $T$ is the desired time headway, $\Delta v$ is
the velocity difference to the lead vehicle, and $b$ is the comfortable deceleration.

The time headway parameter $T$ is estimated online from observed following behaviour using
the method of Zhang and Sun \cite{zhangBayesianCalibrationIntelligent2024}. A circular buffer of 20 samples is
maintained; at each longitudinal Nash tick (10~Hz), a new sample is admitted when the
ego vehicle is following a visible leader with $|\Delta v| < 2\;\mathrm{m/s}$ and a
positive headway. The headway regression yields $\hat{T}$, which is clamped to
$[0.5,\;3.0]\;\mathrm{s}$ and applied with a fallback of $T = 1.5\;\mathrm{s}$ when
fewer than ten samples have been collected. The remaining IDM parameters ($a_{\max}$,
$s_0$, $b$) are fixed at values calibrated from the HighD naturalistic dataset
\cite{krajewskiHighDDatasetDrone2018}: $a_{\max} = 0.553\;\mathrm{m/s^2}$,
$s_0 = 3.416\;\mathrm{m}$, $b = 1.649\;\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

\subsection{Lateral: Neuromuscular Steering Model}

The human driver's lateral reference trajectory is generated by a quintic polynomial
lane-change planner with a neuromuscular steering model following Swain and Rath
\cite{swainCooperativeGameTheory2023}. The neuromuscular filter models the biomechanical lag between
the driver's intended steering input and the actual wheel angle:
\begin{equation}
J\ddot{\delta} + B\dot{\delta} + K\delta = K\delta_{\text{cmd}}
\label{eq:neuromuscular}
\end{equation}
where $J = 3.7 \times 10^{-3}\;\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2}$ is the steering column inertia,
$B = 0.1363\;\mathrm{N\cdot m\cdot s/rad}$ is the damping coefficient, $K = 1.1742\;\mathrm{N\cdot m/rad}$
is the stiffness, and $\delta_{\text{cmd}}$ is the commanded steering angle from the
quintic planner. The lane-change duration for the human trajectory is computed from the
relaxed heading constraint (Section~\ref{sec:lat_traj_planning}). Steering output is
saturated at the vehicle's physical limit and passed through an EMA filter
($\alpha = 0.2$) to suppress high-frequency noise.


\section{Summary}

This chapter presented the mathematical models underlying the vehicle dynamics simulation.
The longitudinal model is a double integrator with position and velocity as state variables,
discretized at 10~Hz by zero-order hold. The lateral model is a four-state bicycle model
with state vector $\mathbf{x} = [y,\;\dot{y}_{\text{b}},\;\psi,\;\dot{\psi}]^{\top}$,
linearized at the current longitudinal velocity and discretized at 20~Hz. The cornering
stiffness values ($C_{af} = 36\,500\;\mathrm{N/rad}$, $C_{ar} = 22\,300\;\mathrm{N/rad}$)
reflect the asymmetric FWD weight distribution of the Audi TT platform.

Vehicle dynamics are integrated at 100~Hz. The lateral Nash solver operates at 20~Hz,
exclusively during the MERGE and FOLLOWING phases, while the longitudinal Nash solver
operates at 10~Hz throughout the merging sequence.

The human driver's longitudinal reference is generated by the IDM with online time-headway
calibration (Zhang and Sun \cite{zhangBayesianCalibrationIntelligent2024}), and the lateral reference by a quintic
polynomial planner with a neuromuscular steering filter (Swain and Rath \cite{swainCooperativeGameTheory2023}).
Both models provide the reference trajectories consumed by the Nash solver described in
Chapter~4.

\chapter{Safety Field Formulations}

This chapter develops the mathematical framework for quantifying collision risk in both the longitudinal and lateral directions. Both directions rest on a shared theoretical foundation: the \textit{driving safety field} (DSF) introduced by Wang et al.~\cite{wangDrivingSafetyField2015, wangDrivingSafetyField2016} and extended to elliptic obstacle representations by Li et al.~\cite{liSharedControlNovel2019}. The longitudinal field applies an elliptic potential to regulate the gap between the ego vehicle and its platoon neighbours; the lateral field applies the full Wang--Li formulation to all platoon members visible in the adjacent lane, generating repulsive forces that prevent lateral collisions during lane change. Both fields supply a common authority allocation mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:authority}) that determines the degree to which the automated system overrides the human driver within the Nash equilibrium framework. The implementation also introduces a dynamic phase detection system that adapts both fields to the current operating context, distinguishing active merging from steady-state following in the longitudinal direction and a five-phase lane-change sequence in the lateral direction.

Section~\ref{sec:dsf_theory} introduces the theoretical foundation of driving safety field theory. Section~\ref{sec:longitudinal_field} describes the bidirectional longitudinal safety field with phase detection. Section~\ref{sec:lateral_field} presents the Wang--Li lateral safety field. Section~\ref{sec:authority} details integration with the Nash control framework.


\section{Driving Safety Field Theory}
\label{sec:dsf_theory}

The driving safety field (DSF) framework, introduced by Wang et al.~\cite{wangDrivingSafetyField2015, wangDrivingSafetyField2016}, extends classical potential field methods from robotics to multi-vehicle traffic environments by representing collision risk as a continuous scalar field over the road plane. Following Li et al.~\cite{liSharedControlNovel2019}, the total field decomposes into three additive components:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{E}_s = \mathbf{E}_p + \mathbf{E}_k + \mathbf{E}_b
\label{eq:total_safety_field}
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{E}_p$ captures static obstacles through a position-dependent potential, $\mathbf{E}_k$ represents the kinetic interaction between moving vehicles, and $\mathbf{E}_b$ encodes driver behavioural characteristics. In platoon-merging scenarios the kinetic component $\mathbf{E}_k$ dominates: all vehicles travel at highway speed, so collision risk arises primarily from closing velocities rather than from stationary obstacles.

The field strength at a given position produces a longitudinal or lateral control force according to~\cite{wangDrivingSafetyField2016}:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{F}_{fi} = E_{si} \, M_i \, R_i \, \exp\!\left(-k_2 \, v_i \cos\theta_i\right)\left(1 + DR_i\right)
\label{eq:field_force}
\end{equation}

where $M_i$ is the virtual mass of vehicle $i$, $R_i$ is a road-condition factor, $v_i$ is its speed, $\theta_i$ is the angle between the vehicle's velocity vector and the field gradient direction, and $DR_i$ is a driver-risk factor that scales the overall force with the assessed riskiness of the driver's behaviour. The virtual mass depends non-linearly on vehicle speed~\cite{wangDrivingSafetyField2016}:

\begin{equation}
M_i = m_0 \left( c_s \, |v_i|^{c_e} + c_o \right)
\label{eq:virtual_mass}
\end{equation}

where $m_0 = 1304\,\mathrm{kg}$ is the physical vehicle mass and the coefficients $c_s = 1.566 \times 10^{-14}$, $c_e = 6.687$, and $c_o = 0.3345$ were calibrated from accident statistics~\cite{wangDrivingSafetyField2016}.

Li et al.~\cite{liSharedControlNovel2019} extended the Wang framework to non-circular obstacle representations by replacing the Euclidean distance with an elliptic distance measure that accounts for the asymmetry between the longitudinal and lateral extents of the safety envelope. The field strength around a single obstacle becomes:

\begin{equation}
E = \frac{G \, R_{\mathrm{obs}} \, M_{\mathrm{obs}}}{r^*} \, \exp\!\left(k_1 \, v_{\mathrm{obs}} \cos\theta_v\right)
\label{eq:li_field_strength}
\end{equation}

where $G = 6 \times 10^{-3}$ is a scaling constant, $R_{\mathrm{obs}}$ is the road-condition factor of the obstacle vehicle, and the kinetic correction term $\exp(k_1 v_{\mathrm{obs}} \cos\theta_v)$ amplifies the field strength when the obstacle moves toward the ego vehicle. The elliptic distance $r^*$ is defined as:

\begin{equation}
r^* = \sqrt{\left(\frac{d_x}{a}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{d_y}{b}\right)^2}
\label{eq:elliptic_distance}
\end{equation}

where $d_x$ and $d_y$ are the longitudinal and lateral separations between the ego and obstacle vehicles, and the semi-axes $a = \max(|\Delta v| \cdot T_s,\, a_{\min})$ and $b = \tau$ adapt to relative velocity, preventing the field from collapsing to zero when the vehicles share the same longitudinal position. The parameters $T_s = 2.0\,\mathrm{s}$, $a_{\min} = 2.0\,\mathrm{m}$, and $\tau = 2.0\,\mathrm{m}$ are fixed. The resultant force on the ego vehicle is~\cite{liSharedControlNovel2019}:

\begin{equation}
F_r = E \, M_{\mathrm{ego}} \, R_{\mathrm{ego}} \, \exp\!\left(-k_2 \, v_{\mathrm{ego}} \cos\theta_i\right)\left(1 + DR\right)
\label{eq:li_force}
\end{equation}

The Wang speed-dependent virtual mass and the Li elliptic distance are combined throughout the implementation. The longitudinal safety field applies the elliptic potential to quantify risk from the leader and follower vehicles; the lateral safety field applies the full Wang--Li formulation to all platoon members in the adjacent lane. Sections~\ref{sec:longitudinal_field} and~\ref{sec:lateral_field} detail each direction separately.

\subsection{Design Philosophy: Bidirectional Forces with Hard Safety Zones}

One design choice differentiates this implementation from standard potential field methods: the longitudinal field is \textit{bidirectional}, generating a repulsive force when the ego vehicle is too close to the leader and an attractive force when it has fallen too far behind. A separate gap-closing controller is therefore unnecessary; the Nash equilibrium solver tracks a single combined reference determined entirely by the safety field.

The bidirectional force is bounded by two hard safety zones derived from a time-headway rule~\cite{wangDrivingSafetyField2016}:

\begin{align}
d_{\min}(v) &= \max\!\left(d_{\mathrm{floor}},\, h_{\min} \cdot v\right) \label{eq:min_safe} \\
d_{\mathrm{emg}}(v) &= d_{\min}(v) + \Delta_{\mathrm{emg}} \label{eq:emg_dist}
\end{align}

where $d_{\mathrm{floor}} = 5.0\,\mathrm{m}$ is a standstill floor, $h_{\min} = 1.0\,\mathrm{s}$ is the minimum time headway, and $\Delta_{\mathrm{emg}} = 1.0\,\mathrm{m}$ is the emergency offset. When the bumper-to-bumper gap falls below $d_{\min}$, the force saturates to its maximum value $F_{\max} = 800\,\mathrm{N}$. Between $d_{\min}$ and $d_{\mathrm{emg}}$, the force ramps linearly from $F_{\max}$ to the elliptic potential value, ensuring a continuous transition into the danger zone. Under these conditions the authority allocator (Section~\ref{sec:authority}) receives a saturated signal, driving $\lambda \to \lambda_{\max}$ and transferring full longitudinal control to the automated system.

\section{Bidirectional Longitudinal Safety Field}
\label{sec:longitudinal_field}

\subsection{Overview and Architecture}

The longitudinal safety field quantifies collision risk from two directions simultaneously: the leader vehicle ahead and the follower vehicle behind. During platoon merging, the ego vehicle must close a large gap to the platoon leader while avoiding a collision with a following vehicle that may be decelerating into the same space. Both constraints must be satisfied concurrently, and the bidirectional formulation addresses this directly.

The field is evaluated over the full merge trajectory and yields a signed scalar force $F_{\mathrm{total}}$ that is passed directly to the authority allocator. The sign convention is: $F > 0$ is repulsive (ego is too close — decelerate); $F < 0$ is attractive (ego has fallen too far behind — accelerate). The desired bumper-to-bumper gap follows a constant time-gap policy~\cite{rajamaniVehicleDynamicsControl2005}:

\begin{equation}
d_{\mathrm{des}}(v) = d_s + h \cdot v
\label{eq:desired_gap}
\end{equation}

where $d_s = 2.0\,\mathrm{m}$ is the standstill distance, $h = 1.5\,\mathrm{s}$ is the time gap, and $v$ is the ego vehicle speed. The gap error with respect to the leader is:

\begin{equation}
e_{\mathrm{gap}} = d_{\mathrm{des}}(v) - d_{\mathrm{actual}}
\label{eq:gap_error}
\end{equation}

so that $e_{\mathrm{gap}} > 0$ indicates the ego is too close (danger) and $e_{\mathrm{gap}} < 0$ indicates the ego has more space than required (safe, attractive regime).

\subsection{Force Computation Model}
\label{sec:long_force}

The leader force is computed through four regimes, ordered by proximity severity.

\subsubsection{Hard Safety Zones}

When the gap falls below the velocity-dependent minimum safe distance $d_{\min}(v)$ defined in Equation~\ref{eq:min_safe}, the force is saturated immediately:

\begin{equation}
F_{\mathrm{leader}} = \begin{cases}
F_{\max} & d < d_{\min}(v) \\[4pt]
\left(1 - \dfrac{d - d_{\min}(v)}{d_{\mathrm{emg}}(v) - d_{\min}(v)}\right) F_{\max} & d_{\min}(v) \leq d < d_{\mathrm{emg}}(v)
\end{cases}
\label{eq:hard_zones}
\end{equation}

where $F_{\max} = 800\,\mathrm{N}$. The linear ramp between $d_{\min}$ and $d_{\mathrm{emg}}$ keeps the transition into full saturation continuous. The EMA filter that normally smooths the force output is bypassed in the emergency zone, so that filter lag cannot attenuate the signal on the first timestep of a dangerous approach.

\subsubsection{Elliptic Potential (Li et al.)}

When the gap exceeds $d_{\mathrm{emg}}$ and the gap error is positive ($e_{\mathrm{gap}} > 0$, ego too close relative to desired), the force is computed from the elliptic \textit{driving safety field} potential~\cite{liSharedControlNovel2019}:

\begin{equation}
F_{\mathrm{elliptic}} = \frac{M_o \cdot R_i}{\left(r_e + \varepsilon\right)^2} \cdot w_d \cdot w_v \cdot \left(1 + DR\right)
\label{eq:elliptic_force}
\end{equation}

where the elliptic ratio is:
\begin{equation}
r_e = \frac{e_{\mathrm{gap}}}{s + \varepsilon}
\label{eq:elliptic_ratio}
\end{equation}

with $\varepsilon = 0.1\,\mathrm{m}$ preventing singularities when $e_{\mathrm{gap}} \to 0$. The distance decay weight is:
\begin{equation}
w_d = \exp\!\left(-\frac{e_{\mathrm{gap}}}{\tau_d}\right), \quad \tau_d = 15\,\mathrm{m}
\label{eq:dist_decay}
\end{equation}

and the closing-velocity weight is:
\begin{equation}
w_v = \exp\!\left(\frac{\max(0,\, v_{\mathrm{rel}})}{5.0}\right), \quad v_{\mathrm{rel}} = v_{\mathrm{ego}} - v_{\mathrm{leader}}
\label{eq:vel_weight}
\end{equation}

so that the force is amplified when the ego approaches the leader ($v_{\mathrm{rel}} > 0$) and remains unchanged when the gap is growing.

\subsubsection{TTC Floor}

A time-to-collision (TTC) floor ensures the elliptic potential does not underestimate risk at mid-range gaps where $r_e$ is small but the closing speed is high. When $v_{\mathrm{rel}} > 0.1\,\mathrm{m/s}$:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{TTC} = \frac{d}{v_{\mathrm{rel}}}
\label{eq:ttc}
\end{equation}

The TTC floor force ramps linearly from zero at $\mathrm{TTC} = T_{\mathrm{warn}} = 5.5\,\mathrm{s}$ to $F_{\max}$ at $\mathrm{TTC} = T_{\mathrm{crit}} = 3.0\,\mathrm{s}$:

\begin{equation}
F_{\mathrm{TTC}} = \left(1 - \frac{\mathrm{TTC} - T_{\mathrm{crit}}}{T_{\mathrm{warn}} - T_{\mathrm{crit}}}\right)_{\!+} F_{\max}
\label{eq:ttc_floor}
\end{equation}

where $(\cdot)_+$ denotes clamping to $[0, 1]$. The final repulsive force takes the larger of the two signals, capped at $F_{\max}$:

\begin{equation}
F_{\mathrm{leader}}^{+} = \min\!\left(\max\!\left(F_{\mathrm{elliptic}},\, F_{\mathrm{TTC}}\right),\, F_{\max}\right)
\label{eq:leader_repulsive}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Attractive Regime}

When $e_{\mathrm{gap}} \leq 0$ (gap exceeds the desired value), a linear attractive force pulls the ego vehicle forward:

\begin{equation}
F_{\mathrm{leader}}^{-} = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\frac{e_{\mathrm{gap}}}{d_{\mathrm{des}}} \cdot F_{\max},\; -0.25\,F_{\max},\; 0\right)
\label{eq:attractive_force}
\end{equation}

The cap at $-0.25\,F_{\max} = -200\,\mathrm{N}$ prevents excessive acceleration when the ego is far behind the platoon; in that regime, the Nash reference generator serves as the primary catch-up mechanism.


\subsection{Dynamic Parameter Computation}

All safety field parameters are adapted to the current driving context via low-pass-filtered updates.

\subsubsection{Dynamic Safety Radius}

The safety radius expands or contracts based on vehicle position in the platoon and current velocity:

\begin{equation}
s = s_{\text{base}} \cdot m_{\text{pos}} \cdot \left(1 + \alpha_v \cdot \frac{v_{\text{ego}}}{v_{\text{ref}}} - \alpha_v\right)
\label{eq:dynamic_radius_impl}
\end{equation}

where $s_{\text{base}} = 10$ m is the base safety radius, $\alpha_v = 0.8$ is the velocity scaling factor, and $v_{\text{ref}} = 120$ km/h ($\approx 33.3$ m/s) is the reference velocity corresponding to the platoon cruise speed. The position multiplier $m_{\text{pos}}$ takes values of 0.8 when the ego vehicle is at the front of the platoon in the leader position, 1.2 when the ego is in the middle of the platoon, and 1.0 when the ego is at the back in the follower position:

\begin{equation}
m_{\text{pos}} = \begin{cases}
0.8 & \text{ego at front of platoon (leader position)} \\
1.2 & \text{ego in middle of platoon} \\
1.0 & \text{ego at back of platoon (follower position)}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:position_multiplier}
\end{equation}

The velocity scaling ensures that higher speeds yield larger safety zones, consistent with the intuition that faster vehicles require greater reaction distance.

\subsubsection{Dynamic Obstacle Mass}

The virtual obstacle mass follows a similar pattern:
\begin{equation}
M_o = M_{\text{base}} \cdot m_{\text{pos}}
\label{eq:dynamic_mass}
\end{equation}

where $M_{\text{base}} = 400$ kg equivalent.

\subsubsection{Low-Pass Filtering}

To prevent abrupt parameter changes that could cause control discontinuities, all dynamic parameters are filtered:

\begin{equation}
\theta_{\text{filtered}}[k] = \alpha_f \cdot \theta_{\text{target}}[k] + (1 - \alpha_f) \cdot \theta_{\text{filtered}}[k-1]
\label{eq:lowpass_filter}
\end{equation}

where $\alpha_f = 0.2$ is the filter coefficient, providing smooth transitions while remaining responsive to changing conditions.

\subsection{Bidirectional Force Combination}

The total longitudinal safety force combines leader and follower contributions:

\begin{equation}
F_{\text{total}} = F_{\text{leader}} + w_f \cdot F_{\text{follower}}
\label{eq:bidirectional_force}
\end{equation}

The follower weight $w_f$ reflects the asymmetric information flow in Cooperative Adaptive Cruise Control (CACC) systems, where information primarily flows forward from leader to follower. The weight takes the value 0.5 during normal operation, 0.2 when the follower is joining and is far behind, and 0.4 when the follower is decelerating:

\begin{equation}
w_f = \begin{cases}
0.5 & \text{normal operation} \\
0.2 & \text{follower is joining (far behind)} \\
0.4 & \text{follower is decelerating}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:follower_weight_impl}
\end{equation}

This forward-dominant weighting ensures string stability by preventing the ego vehicle from over-reacting to follower dynamics.

\subsection{Phase Detection System}

A key innovation in this work is the \textbf{dynamic phase detection system} that distinguishes between operating modes based on actual system state rather than fixed timers. This prevents the safety field from generating unnecessary corrective forces once the vehicle has reached steady-state following.

\subsubsection{Control Phases}

The system operates in two primary phases. The first is the \textbf{MERGING Phase}, which involves active gap closing with full safety field response, where the vehicle is actively approaching its target position in the platoon. The second is the \textbf{FOLLOWING Phase}, which represents steady-state operation with soft-transition response, where the vehicle has reached its target position and maintains formation.

\subsubsection{Phase Transition Conditions}

\textbf{Entry to FOLLOWING Phase} requires all conditions to be satisfied simultaneously for a sustained period:

\begin{align}
|e_{\text{gap}}| &< \gamma_{\text{gap}} \cdot d_{\text{desired}} \label{eq:following_gap} \\
|v_{\text{rel}}| &< \gamma_v \cdot v_{\text{target}} \label{eq:following_vel} \\
|a_{\text{ego}}| &< a_{\text{threshold}} \label{eq:following_acc}
\end{align}

where $\gamma_{\text{gap}} = 0.15$ represents 15\% of the desired gap, $\gamma_v = 0.05$ represents 5\% of target velocity, $a_{\text{threshold}} = 0.5$ m/s², and all conditions must hold for $T_{\text{stable}} = 5$ seconds.

\textbf{Exit from FOLLOWING Phase} occurs immediately if any threshold is violated:

\begin{align}
|e_{\text{gap}}| &> \gamma_{\text{gap,exit}} \cdot d_{\text{desired}} \quad \text{OR} \label{eq:merging_gap} \\
|v_{\text{rel}}| &> \gamma_{v,\text{exit}} \cdot v_{\text{target}} \label{eq:merging_vel}
\end{align}

where $\gamma_{\text{gap,exit}} = 0.25$ provides 25\% hysteresis and $\gamma_{v,\text{exit}} = 0.10$ provides 10\% hysteresis.

The hysteresis between entry and exit thresholds (15\% vs 25\% for gap, 5\% vs 10\% for velocity) prevents oscillation between phases.

\subsubsection{Soft Transition Function}

In the FOLLOWING phase, forces are scaled by a quadratic soft transition function:

\begin{equation}
F_{\text{following}} = F_{\text{raw}} \cdot \min\left(1.0, \left(\frac{|e_{\text{gap}}|}{\gamma_{\text{gap}} \cdot d_{\text{desired}}}\right)^2\right)
\label{eq:soft_transition}
\end{equation}

This creates a smooth reduction of force as the gap error decreases. At the threshold where $|e_{\text{gap}}| = \gamma_{\text{gap}} \cdot d_{\text{desired}}$, the force is at 100\%. At 50\% of the threshold, the force drops to 25\%. Near zero error, the force becomes negligible.

The quadratic relationship ensures smooth, jerk-free transitions while maintaining safety responsiveness for larger errors.

\subsection{Target Position Locking}

After the merge is triggered at time $t_{\text{merge}}$, the safety field locks onto specific leader and follower vehicles by their identifiers rather than using dynamic closest-vehicle detection. This prevents target switching during the merge maneuver:

\begin{equation}
\text{target}_{\text{leader}} = \begin{cases}
\text{closest vehicle ahead} & t < t_{\text{merge}} \\
\text{locked ID} & t \geq t_{\text{merge}}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:target_locking}
\end{equation}

This mechanism is essential for stable merging behavior, especially in scenarios where the ego vehicle may temporarily come closer to a different platoon member than its intended target.

\subsection{Force Saturation and Output}

The final force output is saturated to prevent excessive control commands:

\begin{equation}
F_{\text{output}} = \text{clip}(F_{\text{total}}, -500, 800) \text{ N}
\label{eq:force_saturation}
\end{equation}

The asymmetric limits reflect that stronger acceleration intervention may be needed (positive force for braking when too close to the leader) compared to negative force (accelerating when the follower is too close).

\subsection{Implementation Algorithm}

Algorithm \ref{alg:longitudinal_field_impl} summarizes the complete longitudinal safety field computation.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Bidirectional Longitudinal Safety Field with Phase Detection}
\label{alg:longitudinal_field_impl}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Input:} Ego state $(x_e, v_e, a_e)$, Leader $(x_l, v_l)$, Follower $(x_f, v_f)$, $d_{\text{desired}}$, $t$
\State \textbf{Output:} Total force $F_{\text{total}}$

\State // Update phase based on conditions
\State $e_{\text{gap}} \gets (x_l - x_e) - d_{\text{desired}}$
\State $v_{\text{rel}} \gets v_e - v_l$
\State Call \textsc{UpdatePhase}($e_{\text{gap}}$, $v_{\text{rel}}$, $a_e$, $d_{\text{desired}}$, $v_{\text{target}}$)

\State // Compute leader force (only if gap < desired)
\If{$e_{\text{gap}} < 0$}
    \State $s \gets$ \textsc{ComputeDynamicRadius}(context)
    \State $M_o \gets$ \textsc{ComputeDynamicMass}(context)
    \State $r_e \gets |e_{\text{gap}}| / (s + \epsilon)$
    \State $w_d \gets \exp(-|e_{\text{gap}}| / \tau_d)$
    \State $w_v \gets \exp(\max(0, v_{\text{rel}}) / 5.0)$
    \State $F_{\text{leader}} \gets M_o \cdot R_i / (r_e + \epsilon)^2 \cdot w_d \cdot w_v \cdot (1 + DR_i)$
\Else
    \State $F_{\text{leader}} \gets 0$ \Comment{Safe - no force needed}
\EndIf

\State // Compute follower force with adaptive weight
\If{follower exists AND not ignoring follower}
    \State $w_f \gets$ \textsc{GetFollowerWeight}(context)
    \State $F_{\text{follower}} \gets w_f \cdot$ \textsc{ComputeForce}(follower)
\Else
    \State $F_{\text{follower}} \gets 0$
\EndIf

\State // Apply soft transition in FOLLOWING phase
\If{phase = FOLLOWING}
    \State $F_{\text{leader}} \gets$ \textsc{ApplySoftTransition}($F_{\text{leader}}$, $e_{\text{gap}}$, $d_{\text{desired}}$)
    \State $F_{\text{follower}} \gets$ \textsc{ApplySoftTransition}($F_{\text{follower}}$, $e_{\text{gap,f}}$, $d_{\text{desired}}$)
\EndIf

\State $F_{\text{total}} \gets F_{\text{leader}} + F_{\text{follower}}$
\State $F_{\text{total}} \gets \text{clip}(F_{\text{total}}, -500, 800)$
\State \Return $F_{\text{total}}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Elliptic Lateral Safety Field}
\label{sec:lateral_field}

\subsection{Design Philosophy}

The lateral safety field quantifies collision risk from neighboring platoon vehicles during a lane-change maneuver. A fundamental distinction separates this field from its longitudinal counterpart. In longitudinal control, reducing the gap to the leader is \textit{dangerous}; in lateral control, closing the gap to the target lane is \textit{desired behavior}. The lateral field therefore generates repulsive force only from genuine proximity to other vehicles, not from progress toward the target lane. This separation allows the Nash equilibrium to govern the lane-change trajectory without safety-field interference, with repulsive forces activated only when a real collision threat arises.

The formulation follows Wang and Li~\cite{wangDrivingSafetyField2015,wangDrivingSafetyField2016,liSharedControlNovel2019}, computing a continuous potential field over the two-dimensional plane. The virtual-mass and elliptic-distance framework from the longitudinal field is retained here, with parameters tuned for lateral dynamics.

\subsection{Control Phases for Lateral Motion}
\label{sec:lat_phases}

The lateral controller advances through four phases that map directly to the \texttt{NashCoordinator} state machine: \textsc{Approach}, \textsc{GapSearch}, \textsc{Merge}, and \textsc{Following}.

During \textsc{Approach}, the ego vehicle remains in its current lane while the coordinator waits for a merge command. In \textsc{GapSearch}, available gaps in the target lane are evaluated, and an insertion point is selected. During \textsc{Merge}, the active lane change is executed under Nash shared control. \textsc{Following} is the steady-state phase, entered once the vehicle has settled in the target lane.

\textbf{Entry to \textsc{Following}.} The transition from \textsc{Merge} to \textsc{Following} requires all five conditions in Equations~\ref{eq:lat_following_y}--\ref{eq:lat_conv_cond} to hold simultaneously for a continuous period $T_{\mathrm{stable}} = 5.0\,\mathrm{s}$:

\begin{align}
|y_{\mathrm{ego}} - y_{\mathrm{platoon}}| &< 0.3\,\mathrm{m} \label{eq:lat_following_y} \\
|\psi| &< 0.05\,\mathrm{rad} \label{eq:lat_following_psi} \\
\frac{|e_{\mathrm{gap}}|}{d_{\mathrm{des}}} &< \gamma_{\mathrm{gap}} = 0.10 \label{eq:lat_following_gap} \\
|v_{\mathrm{ego}} - v_{\mathrm{leader}}| &< \delta_v = 0.5\,\mathrm{m/s} \label{eq:lat_following_vel} \\
\left|\dot{e}_{\mathrm{gap}}\right| &< \dot{e}_{\mathrm{max}} = 0.3\,\mathrm{m/s} \label{eq:lat_conv_cond}
\end{align}

where Equation~\ref{eq:lat_following_y} confirms lateral alignment with the platoon lane, Equation~\ref{eq:lat_following_psi} confirms heading alignment, Equations~\ref{eq:lat_following_gap}--\ref{eq:lat_following_vel} confirm longitudinal convergence and velocity synchronization, and Equation~\ref{eq:lat_conv_cond} confirms the gap is no longer drifting.

\textbf{Exit from \textsc{Following}.} The system returns to \textsc{Merge} immediately upon a lateral exceedance:
\begin{equation}
|y_{\mathrm{ego}} - y_{\mathrm{platoon}}| > \tfrac{1}{4}\,W_{\mathrm{lane}}
\label{eq:lat_exit_y}
\end{equation}

where $y_{\mathrm{ego}}$ is the lateral position of the ego vehicle, $y_{\mathrm{platoon}}$ is the lateral center of the platoon lane, and $W_{\mathrm{lane}}$ is the lane width. A sustained heading deviation also triggers the return:
\begin{equation}
|\psi| > 0.10\,\mathrm{rad}
\label{eq:lat_exit_psi}
\end{equation}

where $\psi$ is the yaw angle of the ego vehicle relative to the road centreline.

\subsection{Virtual Mass Formulation}
\label{sec:lat_virtual_mass}

The virtual mass of each vehicle follows Wang~\cite{wangDrivingSafetyField2016} Eq.\,17 and depends on longitudinal speed:

\begin{equation}
M_i(v_i) = m_0 \bigl(c_s\,|v_i|^{c_e} + c_o\bigr)
\label{eq:lat_virtual_mass}
\end{equation}

where $m_0 = 1304\,\mathrm{kg}$ is the vehicle physical mass, $c_s = 1.566 \times 10^{-14}$ and $c_e = 6.687$ are the speed-scaling coefficients, and $c_o = 0.3345$ is the offset ensuring a non-zero mass at rest. The same formula applies to both the ego vehicle ($M_{\mathrm{ego}}$) and each platoon obstacle ($M_{\mathrm{obs}}$). At highway cruising speed the virtual mass exceeds the physical mass, amplifying the perceived inertia and tightening the repulsive field.

\subsection{Elliptic Field Strength}
\label{sec:lat_elliptic}

The two-dimensional separation between the ego vehicle and each obstacle is decomposed into a longitudinal component $d_x$ and a lateral component $d_y$:
\begin{equation}
d_x = x_{\mathrm{ego}} - x_{\mathrm{obs}}, \qquad d_y = y_{\mathrm{ego}} - y_{\mathrm{obs}}
\label{eq:lat_separation}
\end{equation}

where $x_{\mathrm{ego}}$ and $y_{\mathrm{ego}}$ are the longitudinal and lateral positions of the ego vehicle, $x_{\mathrm{obs}}$ and $y_{\mathrm{obs}}$ are the corresponding positions of the obstacle, and $d_y > 0$ indicates that the ego is to the left of the obstacle. An elliptic metric weights the two components according to the velocity difference and a fixed lateral scale~\cite{liSharedControlNovel2019} (Eqs.\,12--13):

\begin{equation}
r^{*} = \sqrt{\left(\frac{d_x}{a}\right)^{\!2} + \left(\frac{d_y}{b}\right)^{\!2}}
\label{eq:lat_elliptic_dist}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
a = \max\!\bigl(|\Delta v|\,T_s,\; a_{\min}\bigr), \qquad b = \tau
\label{eq:lat_elliptic_axes}
\end{equation}

where $\Delta v = v_{\mathrm{ego}} - v_{\mathrm{obs}}$ is the relative longitudinal speed, $T_s = 2.0\,\mathrm{s}$ is the reaction-time scale, $a_{\min} = 2.0\,\mathrm{m}$ prevents the longitudinal axis from collapsing when both vehicles travel at the same speed, and $b = \tau = 2.0\,\mathrm{m}$ is the fixed lateral axis. At speed differentials below $1.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$, both axes equal $2.0\,\mathrm{m}$, making the field locally isotropic.

The scalar field strength~\cite{liSharedControlNovel2019} (Eq.\,11) is:

\begin{equation}
E = G \cdot R_{\mathrm{obs}} \cdot \frac{M_{\mathrm{obs}}}{r^{*}}
\label{eq:lat_field_strength}
\end{equation}

where $G = 6 \times 10^{-3}$ is the gravitational-analogue constant and $R_{\mathrm{obs}}$ is the road-condition factor for the obstacle vehicle.

\subsection{Kinetic Corrections}
\label{sec:lat_kinetic}

Two kinetic corrections account for the velocities of the obstacle and the ego vehicle relative to the field gradient direction~\cite{liSharedControlNovel2019}.

\subsubsection{Obstacle Kinetic Correction (Li Eq.~18)}

The obstacle's longitudinal velocity is projected onto the elliptic gradient unit vector $\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{r^*}$. For a vehicle moving in the positive-$x$ direction, the unit velocity vector is $[1, 0]^{\top}$, so the projection reduces to the $x$-component of $\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{r^*}$:

\begin{equation}
\cos\theta_v = \hat{u}_{r^*,x} = \frac{d_x / a}{r^{*}}
\label{eq:lat_cos_theta_v}
\end{equation}

where $\hat{u}_{r^*,x}$ is the $x$-component of the normalized gradient vector and $\theta_v$ is the angle between the obstacle velocity and the gradient direction.

\begin{equation}
E \;\leftarrow\; E \cdot \exp\!\bigl(K_1\,|v_{\mathrm{obs}}|\,\cos\theta_v\bigr), \qquad K_1 = 0.005
\label{eq:lat_obs_kinetic}
\end{equation}

where $K_1$ is the obstacle kinetic coefficient. This update amplifies the field when the obstacle moves toward the ego along the gradient and attenuates it when the obstacle moves away.

\subsubsection{Ego Kinetic Correction (Li Eq.~21)}

The ego vehicle's velocity is projected onto the unnormalized elliptic gradient $\nabla(r^{*2})$ to form $\cos\theta_i$:

\begin{equation}
\cos\theta_i = \frac{d_x / a^2}{\left\|(d_x / a^2,\; d_y / b^2)\right\|}
\label{eq:lat_cos_theta_i}
\end{equation}

where $\theta_i$ is the angle between the ego velocity and the elliptic gradient, and the denominator is the Euclidean norm of the unnormalized gradient vector. The repulsive force magnitude is then:

\begin{equation}
F_r = E \cdot M_{\mathrm{ego}} \cdot R_{\mathrm{ego}} \cdot \exp\!\bigl(-K_2\,|v_{\mathrm{ego}}|\,\cos\theta_i\bigr) \cdot (1 + DR)
\label{eq:lat_force_mag}
\end{equation}

where $K_2 = 0.005$ is the ego kinetic coefficient, $R_{\mathrm{ego}}$ is the road-condition factor for the ego vehicle, and $DR = 0.5$ is the dynamic risk addend that sustains a non-zero floor even when kinetic corrections are near unity.

\subsection{Lateral Direction Convention}
\label{sec:lat_direction}

Equation~\ref{eq:lat_force_mag} gives only the magnitude of the repulsive force. The lateral direction is assigned through a smooth hyperbolic tangent of the lateral separation:

\begin{equation}
F_{\mathrm{lat}} = F_r \cdot \tanh\!\left(\frac{d_y}{\sigma}\right), \qquad \sigma = 0.5\,\mathrm{m}
\label{eq:lat_direction}
\end{equation}

where $\sigma$ is the lateral sharpness parameter governing the transition width around $d_y = 0$. When $d_y = 0$ (ego directly ahead of or behind the obstacle), the force is exactly zero: no lateral bias is introduced. When $|d_y| \gg \sigma$, the $\tanh$ saturates to $\pm 1$ and the full magnitude $F_r$ is applied. This continuously differentiable sign function avoids the discontinuity that a hard $\mathrm{sign}(\cdot)$ would introduce at $d_y = 0$.

\subsection{Road Boundary Forces}
\label{sec:lat_boundaries}

Lane-boundary forces prevent the ego vehicle from leaving the road. These act alongside the vehicle-to-vehicle repulsion described above. The lateral position $y_{\mathrm{ego}}$ defines distances to the right and left edges:

\begin{equation}
d_{\mathrm{right}} = W_{\mathrm{road}}/2 - y_{\mathrm{ego}}, \qquad d_{\mathrm{left}} = W_{\mathrm{road}}/2 + y_{\mathrm{ego}}
\label{eq:lat_boundary_dists}
\end{equation}

where $W_{\mathrm{road}}$ is the total road width and $y_{\mathrm{ego}}$ is measured from the road centreline. A repulsive potential is activated when either distance falls below the proximity threshold $d_{\mathrm{prox}} = 3.0\,\mathrm{m}$:

\begin{equation}
F_{\mathrm{boundary},k} = \pm\, K_b \cdot \tanh\!\left(\frac{1}{\max(d_k,\,\varepsilon_b)\,K_{b,s}}\right)
\label{eq:lat_boundary_force}
\end{equation}

where $K_b = 150\,\mathrm{N}$ is the boundary gain, $K_{b,s} = 2.0$ is the saturation scale, $\varepsilon_b = 0.1\,\mathrm{m}$ is a lower-bound clamp on $d_k$ that prevents division by zero as the vehicle approaches the road edge, and $k \in \{\mathrm{right}, \mathrm{left}\}$ indexes the two boundaries. The right boundary contributes a negative (leftward) force and the left boundary a positive (rightward) force. The $\tanh$ saturation prevents the force from diverging as $d_k \to 0$.

\subsection{Force Saturation and Output}
\label{sec:lat_output}

The total lateral force sums over all platoon vehicles and both boundary contributions:

\begin{equation}
F_{\mathrm{total}} = \sum_{j} F_{\mathrm{lat},j} + F_{\mathrm{boundary,right}} + F_{\mathrm{boundary,left}}
\label{eq:lat_total}
\end{equation}

where the index $j$ runs over all platoon vehicles in the scene. The output is saturated to a symmetric bound and filtered through a first-order exponential moving average (EMA):

\begin{equation}
F_{\mathrm{clip}} = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(F_{\mathrm{total}},\; -F_{\mathrm{lat,max}},\; F_{\mathrm{lat,max}}\right), \qquad F_{\mathrm{lat,max}} = 2000\,\mathrm{N}
\label{eq:lat_saturation}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
F_{\mathrm{out}}[k] = \alpha_f \cdot F_{\mathrm{clip}}[k] + (1 - \alpha_f)\cdot F_{\mathrm{out}}[k-1], \qquad \alpha_f = 0.3
\label{eq:lat_ema}
\end{equation}

where $\alpha_f$ is the EMA smoothing coefficient and $k$ is the discrete time index. The saturation bound of $\pm 2000\,\mathrm{N}$ is four times the longitudinal bound, reflecting the greater lateral inertia during a lane change. The coefficient $\alpha_f = 0.3$ is higher than the longitudinal value of $0.2$, yielding a faster response to sudden lateral threats while still attenuating high-frequency noise.

\section{Integration with Nash Equilibrium Control}

\subsection{Authority Allocation Mechanism}

The safety field force directly influences the Nash equilibrium through the authority allocation mechanism. The authority ratio $\lambda$ determines the relative weighting between the system controller and the human driver in the Nash cost functions.

\subsubsection{Safety-Based Authority (Sigmoid)}

The baseline authority is computed from the safety field force magnitude using a sigmoid function:
\begin{equation}
\lambda_{\text{safety}} = \lambda_{\min} + (\lambda_{\max} - \lambda_{\min}) \cdot \sigma(|F|)
\label{eq:authority_sigmoid}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma(|F|) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-k(|F| - F_0)}}$ is the sigmoid function with steepness $k = 0.015$ N$^{-1}$ and midpoint $F_0 = 400$ N. The authority bounds are $\lambda_{\min} = 0.1$ (human dominant when safe) and $\lambda_{\max} = 100$ (system dominant during emergencies).

\subsubsection{Performance-Based Authority with Hysteresis}

In addition to safety-based authority, a performance component responds to large gap errors that do not yet generate safety field forces (e.g., the vehicle is far from its target but not near any obstacle). To prevent oscillation at mode boundaries, hysteresis logic is employed:
\begin{equation}
\text{Performance mode} = \begin{cases}
\texttt{ON} & \text{if previously OFF and } |e_{\text{gap}}| > e_{\text{enter}} \\
\texttt{OFF} & \text{if previously ON and } |e_{\text{gap}}| < e_{\text{exit}} \\
\text{unchanged} & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:hysteresis}
\end{equation}
where $e_{\text{enter}} = 3.0$ m and $e_{\text{exit}} = 1.0$ m. When performance mode is active, $\lambda_{\text{performance}}$ increases progressively with gap error, capped at 50.

\subsubsection{Authority Fusion and Smoothing}

The final authority ratio is the maximum of the two components, followed by exponential smoothing:
\begin{align}
\lambda_{\text{target}} &= \max(\lambda_{\text{safety}}, \lambda_{\text{performance}}) \label{eq:authority_fusion} \\
\lambda[k] &= \alpha \cdot \lambda_{\text{target}} + (1 - \alpha) \cdot \lambda[k-1] \label{eq:authority_smoothing}
\end{align}
where $\alpha \in [\alpha_{\text{base}}, \alpha_{\text{fast}}] = [0.05, 0.12]$ adapts based on the gap error magnitude: faster smoothing for large errors (quick response) and slower smoothing for small errors (comfort).

The system authority weight is computed from $\lambda$:
\begin{equation}
\alpha_{\text{sys}} = \frac{\lambda}{1 + \lambda}
\label{eq:authority_alpha}
\end{equation}

This creates the control loop:
\begin{equation}
\text{Safety Field} \xrightarrow{F} \text{Authority Allocator} \xrightarrow{\lambda} \text{Nash Weights} \xrightarrow{u_1, u_2} \text{Shared Control} \xrightarrow{u_{\text{shared}}} \text{Vehicle}
\label{eq:control_loop}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Separation of Concerns}

The architecture maintains a clear separation between the three components. The \textbf{Nash Equilibrium} enables optimal trajectory tracking via cost-function optimization, in which both longitudinal (gap and velocity tracking) and lateral (position and heading tracking) controllers share the same Nash framework within DMPC. The \textbf{Safety Field} handles collision avoidance via repulsive forces, generating force only when collision risk exists, thereby enabling Nash to operate without interference when the vehicle is safe. The \textbf{Phase Detection} handles context awareness, distinguishing active merging from steady-state following and enabling different control strategies for different situations.

This separation ensures that normal operation relies on Nash for smooth, optimal control, that safety intervention occurs only when genuinely needed, and that transitions between modes are smooth and predictable.

\section{Computational Considerations}

\subsection{Complexity Analysis}

The \textbf{Longitudinal Safety Field} has time complexity $O(1)$ since a constant number of vehicles are considered. The update rate is 10 Hz, matching the longitudinal controller, and the typical computation time is under 1 ms.

The \textbf{Lateral Safety Field} has time complexity $O(N_{\text{obs}})$ where $N_{\text{obs}}$ is the number of obstacles. The update rate is 20 Hz, matching the lateral Nash MPC controller, with typical computation time under 0.5 ms for 3--5 obstacles.

\subsection{Numerical Stability}

Several measures ensure numerical stability. Epsilon regularization adds $\epsilon = 0.1$ m to all distance denominators. Force saturation through defined limits prevents unbounded outputs. Low-pass filtering smooths parameter and force transitions. Tanh saturation provides natural, smooth limiting for high forces.

\section{Summary}

This chapter presented the safety field formulations for both longitudinal and lateral control. Six key contributions emerge from this work.

First, the \textbf{Pure Repulsive Design} ensures that forces are generated only when collision risk exists, specifically when $e_{\text{gap}} < 0$ for longitudinal control and $d_y < d_{\text{safe}}$ for lateral control. This enables Nash to control trajectories without interference when the vehicle is safe.

Second, the \textbf{Dynamic Phase Detection} uses state-based rather than timer-based phase transitions with hysteresis to prevent oscillations and enable context-appropriate control responses.

Third, the \textbf{Soft Transition Function} provides quadratic scaling in the FOLLOWING phase, ensuring smooth force reduction as errors decrease.

Fourth, the \textbf{Bidirectional Longitudinal Field} employs asymmetric leader/follower weighting with $w_f \in [0.2, 0.5]$ to reflect CACC information flow characteristics.

Fifth, the \textbf{Collision-Based Lateral Field} employs a two-threshold system that distinguishes ``safe, aware,'' and ``danger'' zones and elicits appropriate force responses.

Sixth, \textbf{Target Locking} ensures that merge targets are locked at trigger time to prevent target switching during maneuvers.

\section{Nash Game Formulation}
\label{sec:nash_formulation}
% ===================================================================

\subsection{Two-Player Non-Cooperative Game}

The shared control problem is formulated as a non-cooperative differential game between two players, following the framework of Li et al.~\cite{li_shared_2019}. Player~1 is the autonomous system, and Player~2 is the human driver. Both players operate on the same vehicle, applying their respective control inputs(longitudinal acceleration or steering angle) to a shared dynamic system.

Although the formulation is non-cooperative in the game-theoretic sense, cooperative behavior emerges through two mechanisms. The first is the cross-coupling cost terms $S_1$ and $S_2$, which penalize each player for the other player's control effort. The second is the dynamic authority allocation parameter $\lambda(k)$, which scales the human driver's cost function based on the current risk level assessed through the driving safety field~\cite{wang_driving_2015, wang_driving_2016}.

A central design principle is that each player tracks its own reference trajectory independently. The autonomous system tracks the safety-optimal reference $\mathbf{r}_1$, while the human driver tracks their comfort-oriented reference $\mathbf{r}_2$. No blending of references occurs within the Nash game; coordination between players arises solely from the cost function structure and the post-optimization authority blending of the resulting control inputs:
\begin{equation}
u_{\text{shared}}[k] = \alpha[k] \cdot u_1^*[k] + (1 - \alpha[k]) \cdot u_2^*[k]
\label{eq:shared_control_ch4}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha[k] = \lambda[k] / (1 + \lambda[k])$ converts the authority ratio to a blending weight~\cite{li_shared_2019}.

\subsection{Nash Equilibrium Definition}

A strategy pair $(D_1^*, D_2^*)$ constitutes a Nash equilibrium if neither player can unilaterally improve their cost by deviating~\cite{jond_connected_2023}:
\begin{align}
J_1(D_1^*, D_2^*) &\leq J_1(D_1, D_2^*) \quad \forall D_1 \label{eq:nash_def_1}\\
J_2(D_1^*, D_2^*) &\leq J_2(D_1^*, D_2) \quad \forall D_2 \label{eq:nash_def_2}
\end{align}

Unlike centralized optimization, which minimizes a single combined objective, the Nash equilibrium preserves individual objectives while achieving coordination through the dynamic coupling via $\lambda[k]$ and the cross-coupling weights $S_1$, $S_2$~\cite{li_shared_2019}.

\subsection{Game Structure per Control Cycle}

At each control cycle $k$, the game proceeds as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Both players observe the current state $\mathbf{x}[k]$.
\item The safety field computes the risk force $F_{\text{total}}[k]$ (Chapter~3, based on~\cite{wang_driving_2015, wang_driving_2016, song_vehicle_2013}).
\item The authority allocator maps the force to an authority ratio $\lambda[k]$.
\item Both players' optimization problems are solved simultaneously as a single stacked QP.
\item The shared control is computed via Eq.~\eqref{eq:shared_control_ch4}.
\end{enumerate}

This procedure applies identically to both the longitudinal and lateral controllers, differing only in the state-space model, weight matrices, and constraint values.

\section{Distributed Model Predictive Control Framework}
\label{sec:dmpc}
% ===================================================================

\subsection{Longitudinal Dynamics}
\label{sec:longitudinal_dynamics}

The longitudinal dynamics are described by a double-integrator model with state $\mathbf{x} = [p, v]^T$ (position and velocity) and control input $u$ (acceleration). The discrete-time matrices are:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A}_{\text{long}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & T_s \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
\mathbf{B}_{\text{long}} = \begin{bmatrix} T_s^2/2 \\ T_s \end{bmatrix}, \quad
\mathbf{C}_{\text{long}} = \mathbf{I}_2
\label{eq:long_ss}
\end{equation}

Both players apply acceleration to the same vehicle, so $\mathbf{B}_1 = \mathbf{B}_2 = \mathbf{B}_{\text{long}}$. The output is $\mathbf{z} = [p, v]^T$ with $n_x = 2$ states and $n_z = 2$ outputs.

\subsection{Lateral Dynamics: Bicycle Model}
\label{sec:lateral_dynamics}

The lateral dynamics use the global-frame bicycle model (Section~\ref{sec:global_frame_model}) with state $\mathbf{x} = [y, \dot{y}, \psi, \dot{\psi}]^T$ (lateral position, lateral velocity, heading angle, yaw rate) and control input $\delta$ (front-wheel steering angle). The continuous-time state-space matrices are:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A}_c = \begin{bmatrix}
0 & 1 & v_x & 0 \\[4pt]
0 & -\dfrac{2(C_f + C_r)}{m v_x} & 0 & -v_x - \dfrac{2(L_f C_f - L_r C_r)}{m v_x} \\[4pt]
0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\[4pt]
0 & -\dfrac{2(L_f C_f - L_r C_r)}{I_z v_x} & 0 & -\dfrac{2(L_f^2 C_f + L_r^2 C_r)}{I_z v_x}
\end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:Ac_bicycle}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{B}_c = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 2C_f / m \\ 0 \\ 2L_f C_f / I_z \end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:Bc_bicycle}
\end{equation}

where $C_f$ and $C_r$ are the per-tire cornering stiffness coefficients (multiplied by 2 for the axle total), $L_f$ and $L_r$ are the distances from the center of gravity to the front and rear axles, $m$ is the vehicle mass, $I_z$ is the yaw moment of inertia, and $v_x$ is the longitudinal velocity (assumed constant within each prediction horizon)~\cite{rajamani_vehicle_2005, fernandez_vehicle_nodate}. The $A_c(1,3) = v_x$ term represents the kinematic coupling between heading and global lateral position, as described in Chapter~2.

As described in Section~\ref{sec:dual_model}, the implementation uses a dual-model architecture in which the Nash solver receives state feedback from the error model (Equation~\eqref{eq:Ac_error}), while the plant propagates using the body-frame model (Equation~\eqref{eq:Ac_body}). The matrices shown in Equations~\eqref{eq:Ac_bicycle}--\eqref{eq:Bc_bicycle} represent the global-frame formulation used for the theoretical derivation; the controller model replaces the kinematic coupling terms with force-coupling terms as detailed in Section~\ref{sec:dual_model}.

Discretization is performed using the matrix exponential method~\cite{rajamani_vehicle_2005}:
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{A}_{\text{lat}} & \mathbf{B}_{\text{lat}} \\ \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{I} \end{bmatrix}
= \exp\!\left( \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{A}_c & \mathbf{B}_c \\ \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix} T_s \right)
\label{eq:zoh_discretization}
\end{equation}

The output matrix selects the lateral position and heading angle:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{C}_{\text{lat}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:C_lateral}
\end{equation}

yielding $\mathbf{z} = [y, \psi]^T$ with $n_x = 4$ states and $n_z = 2$ outputs. As in the longitudinal case, both players apply steering to the same vehicle: $\mathbf{B}_1 = \mathbf{B}_2 = \mathbf{B}_{\text{lat}}$.

\subsection{Multi-Step Prediction and Compact Form}

For both domains, the system output over the prediction horizon $N_p$ is written in compact form as:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{Z}[k] = \mathbf{U} \mathbf{x}[k] + \mathbf{H} D_1 + \mathbf{H} D_2
\label{eq:compact_prediction}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{U} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_z N_p \times n_x}$ is the free response matrix and $\mathbf{H} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_z N_p \times N_u}$ is the forced response matrix. Since $\mathbf{B}_1 = \mathbf{B}_2$ in both domains, the forced response matrices are identical: $\mathbf{H}_1 = \mathbf{H}_2 \equiv \mathbf{H}$.

The free response matrix is:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{U} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{C} \mathbf{A} \\ \mathbf{C} \mathbf{A}^2 \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{C} \mathbf{A}^{N_p} \end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:U_matrix}
\end{equation}

The forced response matrix $\mathbf{H}$ has a lower-triangular Toeplitz structure:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{H} = \begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{C} \mathbf{B} & \mathbf{0} & \cdots & \mathbf{0} \\
\mathbf{C} \mathbf{A} \mathbf{B} & \mathbf{C} \mathbf{B} & \cdots & \mathbf{0} \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
\mathbf{C} \mathbf{A}^{N_p-1} \mathbf{B} & \mathbf{C} \mathbf{A}^{N_p-2} \mathbf{B} & \cdots & \mathbf{C} \mathbf{A}^{N_p-N_u} \mathbf{B}
\end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:H_matrix}
\end{equation}

The dimensions of $\mathbf{U}$ and $\mathbf{H}$ differ between the two domains due to different state and output dimensions (longitudinal: $n_x = n_z = 2$; lateral: $n_x = 4$, $n_z = 2$), but the algebraic structure is identical.

\section{Cost Function Formulation}
\label{sec:cost_functions}
% ===================================================================

\subsection{General Quadratic Cost Structure with Cross-Coupling}

Following Li et al.~\cite{li_shared_2019}, each player minimizes a quadratic cost over the prediction horizon that includes cross-coupling terms penalizing the other player's control effort:
\begin{align}
J_1 &= \sum_{j=0}^{N_p-1} \| \mathbf{e}_1[k+j] \|_{Q_1}^2 + \sum_{j=0}^{N_u-1} \left( R_1 \, u_1[k+j]^2 + S_1 \, u_2[k+j]^2 \right) \label{eq:cost_player1}\\
J_2 &= \sum_{j=0}^{N_p-1} \| \mathbf{e}_2[k+j] \|_{Q_2(\lambda)}^2 + \sum_{j=0}^{N_u-1} \left( R_2 \, u_2[k+j]^2 + S_2 \, u_1[k+j]^2 \right) \label{eq:cost_player2}
\end{align}
where $\mathbf{e}_i[k+j] = \mathbf{r}_i[k+j] - \mathbf{z}[k+j]$ is the tracking error for player $i$, $Q_i$ is the output tracking weight matrix, $R_i$ is the own control effort weight, and $S_i$ is the cross-coupling weight penalizing the other player's control effort.

The authority ratio $\lambda$ enters through Player~2's tracking weight:
\begin{equation}
Q_2(\lambda[k]) = \lambda[k] \cdot Q_1
\label{eq:Q2_coupling}
\end{equation}

When $\lambda$ is low (safe conditions), Player~2's tracking penalty is small, giving the human more freedom. When $\lambda$ is high (dangerous conditions), Player~2 is strongly penalized for deviating from their reference~\cite{li_shared_2019}.

\subsection{Longitudinal Cost Function Weights}

For the longitudinal controller, the output weight matrix tracks gap position and velocity:
\begin{equation}
Q_{1}^{\text{long}} = \begin{bmatrix} Q_{1,p} & 0 \\ 0 & Q_{1,v} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2500 & 0 \\ 0 & 50 \end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:Q1_long}
\end{equation}

The position-tracking weight $Q_{1,p} = 2500$ is substantially higher than the velocity-tracking weight $Q_{1,v} = 50$, thereby implementing a position-dominant tuning strategy. In platoon merging, the primary objective is to close the gap to the correct position, and velocity matching follows naturally. The control effort weights are:
\begin{equation}
R_1^{\text{long}} = R_2^{\text{long}} = S_1^{\text{long}} = S_2^{\text{long}} = 800
\label{eq:RS_long}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Lateral Cost Function Weights}

For the lateral controller, the output weight matrix tracks lateral position and heading angle:
\begin{equation}
Q_{1}^{\text{lat}} = \begin{bmatrix} Q_{1,y} & 0 \\ 0 & Q_{1,\psi} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 & 0 \\ 0 & 10{,}000 \end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:Q1_lat}
\end{equation}

The heading weight $Q_{1,\psi} = 10{,}000$ is three orders of magnitude larger than the lateral position weight $Q_{1,y} = 10$. This heading-dominant tuning reflects a fundamental principle: the vehicle must first stabilize its heading angle before correcting its lateral position. An unstable heading during a lane change results in dangerous oscillations, whereas a slightly longer lateral correction is acceptable.

The control effort weights for the lateral controller are:
\begin{equation}
R_1^{\text{lat}} = R_2^{\text{lat}} = S_1^{\text{lat}} = S_2^{\text{lat}} = 50{,}000
\label{eq:RS_lat}
\end{equation}

These weights are substantially larger than the longitudinal weights ($R^{\text{long}} = 800$) because the control input is a steering angle (radians, typically $|\delta| \sim 0.01$~rad) rather than acceleration (m/s$^2$, typically $|u| \sim 1$--$2$~m/s$^2$). The $R/Q_y$ ratio of $5{,}000$ ensures realistic, small steering corrections that satisfy passenger comfort constraints~\cite{rajamani_vehicle_2005}. Notably, the $S = R$ cooperation principle applies identically to the lateral domain, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:cooperation_principle}.

\subsection{Scale Separation Between Domains}

The difference in weight magnitudes between the longitudinal and lateral controllers arises from the physical scales of the respective control inputs. For the longitudinal domain, typical accelerations are $\sim 2$~m/s$^2$, while for the lateral domain, typical steering angles are $\sim 0.01$~rad ($\approx \SI{0.6}{\degree}$). The lateral control effort weight $R^{\text{lat}} = 50{,}000$ compared to $R^{\text{long}} = 800$ yields a ratio of approximately 62.5. This ratio, combined with the heading-dominant $Q$ matrix ($Q_\psi = 10{,}000$), ensures that the lateral steering corrections remain physically realistic despite the different input scales between the two domains.

Direct copying of weight values between domains without this scaling would produce either negligible control effort (if longitudinal weights were used for lateral) or excessive, saturating control (if lateral weights were used for longitudinal).

\subsection{The \texorpdfstring{$S = R$}{S = R} Cooperation Principle}
\label{sec:cooperation_principle}

A fundamental insight of this research is that the cross-coupling weights $S_i$ determine the nature of the Nash equilibrium. In the stacked QP formulation (Section~\ref{sec:solution_algorithm}), the cross-coupling weights appear in the diagonal blocks of the Hessian:
\begin{align}
\mathbf{P}_{11} &= \mathbf{H}^T \bar{Q}_1 \mathbf{H} + (R_1 + S_2) \mathbf{I}_{N_u} \label{eq:P11}\\
\mathbf{P}_{22} &= \lambda \, \mathbf{H}^T \bar{Q}_1 \mathbf{H} + (R_2 + S_1) \mathbf{I}_{N_u} \label{eq:P22}
\end{align}

Setting $S_i = R_i$ produces a symmetric penalty structure where each player equally penalizes both its own and the other's control effort:
\begin{itemize}
\item When $S < R$: each player weighs its own control cost more heavily, leading to \textit{competitive} behavior where both controllers fight each other.
\item When $S = R$: both costs are equally weighted, yielding a \textit{cooperative} equilibrium where both controllers converge smoothly.
\item When $S > R$: each player over-prioritizes the other's cost, leading to overly deferential behavior.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Longitudinal implementation.} The $S = R$ principle is applied directly: $R_1 = R_2 = S_1 = S_2 = 800$.

\paragraph{Lateral implementation.} The $S = R$ principle is applied directly: $R_1 = R_2 = S_1 = S_2 = 50{,}000$. Early experiments with $S = 0.2 R$ produced insufficient cross-coupling, causing the two controllers to compete rather than cooperate during lane changes. Setting $S = R$ resolved the oscillations once the heading-dominant tuning ($Q_\psi = 10{,}000 \gg Q_y = 10$) was established. The heading-dominant weighting ensures that the vehicle first stabilizes its heading before correcting its lateral position, which provides the stability margin needed for the $S = R$ ratio to produce smooth convergence. This confirms that the $S = R$ cooperation principle is \textbf{universal}: it applies to both the double-integrator longitudinal model and the bicycle-model lateral dynamics without modification.

\subsection{Driver-Type Modifiers (Lateral Controller)}
\label{sec:driver_type}

Following Li et al.~\cite{li_shared_2019}, the lateral controller supports three driver types by modifying Player~2's cost function weights. The modifiers are applied to the base values $R_2^{\text{lat}}$, $S_2^{\text{lat}}$, and $Q_{1,y}^{\text{lat}}$:

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{|l|c|c|c|}
\hline
\textbf{Driver Type} & \textbf{$R_2$ Factor} & \textbf{$S_2$ Factor} & \textbf{$Q_y$ Factor} \\
\hline
Cautious   & 1.5 & 1.5 & 0.8 \\
Normal     & 1.0 & 1.0 & 1.0 \\
Aggressive & 0.6 & 0.6 & 1.3 \\

\caption{Driver-type modifiers for lateral Nash game weights}
\label{tab:driver_types}
\end{table}

A cautious driver has a higher $R_2$ (prefers gentle control), a higher $S_2$ (is more cooperative with the system), and a lower $Q_y$ (exhibits less aggressive position tracking). An aggressive driver has a lower $R_2$ (willingness to use large steering inputs), a lower $S_2$ (cooperativeness), and a higher $Q_y$ (demanding faster lane changes). These modifiers are not applied in the longitudinal controller, where driver behavior is modeled differently through the human reference trajectory.

\subsection{Compact Matrix Representation}

Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:compact_prediction}, the compact cost functions become:
\begin{align}
J_1 &= \mathbf{E}_1^T \bar{Q}_1 \mathbf{E}_1 + D_1^T \bar{R}_1 D_1 + D_2^T \bar{S}_1 D_2 \label{eq:cost_matrix_1}\\
J_2 &= \mathbf{E}_2^T \bar{Q}_2 \mathbf{E}_2 + D_2^T \bar{R}_2 D_2 + D_1^T \bar{S}_2 D_1 \label{eq:cost_matrix_2}
\end{align}
where $\bar{Q}_1 = \text{diag}(Q_1, \ldots, Q_1)$, $\bar{Q}_2 = \lambda \bar{Q}_1$, $\bar{R}_i = R_i \mathbf{I}_{N_u}$, $\bar{S}_i = S_i \mathbf{I}_{N_u}$, and $\mathbf{E}_i = \mathbf{R}_i - \mathbf{U}\mathbf{x}[k] - \mathbf{H}D_1 - \mathbf{H}D_2$.

\section{Dynamic Authority Allocation}
\label{sec:authority_allocation}
% ===================================================================

The authority ratio $\lambda[k]$ dynamically adjusts the balance between system and human control based on the assessed risk level. The longitudinal and lateral controllers use different authority allocation strategies reflecting their distinct operational requirements.

\subsection{Longitudinal Authority Allocation: Dual-Source with Hysteresis}
\label{sec:long_authority}

The longitudinal authority is computed from two independent sources, following the safety field framework of Wang et al.~\cite{wang_driving_2015, wang_driving_2016} as adapted by Li et al.~\cite{li_shared_2019}.

\subsubsection{Safety Authority via Sigmoid Mapping}

The safety field force $F_{\text{total}}[k]$ is mapped to an authority ratio through a sigmoid function:
\begin{equation}
\lambda_{\text{safety}}[k] = \lambda_{\min} + (\lambda_{\max} - \lambda_{\min}) \cdot \sigma\!\left(k_s \cdot (|F_{\text{total}}[k]| - F_{\text{mid}})\right)
\label{eq:sigmoid_authority_long}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-x})$, $\lambda_{\min} = 0.1$, $\lambda_{\max} = 100$, $k_s = 0.015$, and $F_{\text{mid}} = 400$~N. This sigmoid mapping yields a continuously differentiable authority transition, unlike the discrete lookup table used by Li et al.~\cite{li_shared_2019}.

\subsubsection{Performance Authority with Hysteresis}

A performance-based authority addresses situations in which the gap error is large but the safety force is small (e.g., during initial gap closure). A hysteresis mechanism prevents oscillations at the mode boundary:
\begin{equation}
\text{Enter performance mode:} \quad |e_{\text{gap}}[k]| > d_{\text{enter}} = 3.0 \text{ m}
\label{eq:hysteresis_enter}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\text{Exit performance mode:} \quad |e_{\text{gap}}[k]| < d_{\text{exit}} = 1.0 \text{ m}
\label{eq:hysteresis_exit}
\end{equation}

When in performance mode, the authority is computed as:
\begin{equation}
\lambda_{\text{perf}}[k] = 1.0 + 0.3 \bar{e} + 0.25 (|e_{\text{gap}}| - d_{\text{exit}})
\label{eq:perf_authority}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{e} = \max(0, (|e_{\text{gap}}| - d_{\text{exit}}) / (d_{\text{enter}} - d_{\text{exit}}))$. An asymmetry factor of $1.3$ is applied when $e_{\text{gap}} > 0$ (catching up is more urgent). The performance authority is bounded by $\lambda_{\text{perf}} \leq 50$.

\subsubsection{Fusion and Adaptive Smoothing}

The target authority is the maximum of the two sources:
\begin{equation}
\lambda_{\text{target}}[k] = \max\!\left(\lambda_{\text{safety}}[k], \, \lambda_{\text{perf}}[k]\right)
\label{eq:lambda_fusion}
\end{equation}

Smoothing uses an adaptive exponential moving average (EMA):
\begin{equation}
\lambda[k] = \alpha_\lambda \cdot \lambda_{\text{target}}[k] + (1 - \alpha_\lambda) \cdot \lambda[k-1]
\label{eq:lambda_smooth_long}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_\lambda$ adapts based on the gap error:
\begin{equation}
\alpha_\lambda = \begin{cases}
0.12 & |e_{\text{gap}}| > 5.0 \text{ m} \\
0.05 + 0.07 \cdot \frac{|e_{\text{gap}}| - 2.0}{3.0} & 2.0 < |e_{\text{gap}}| \leq 5.0 \\
0.05 & |e_{\text{gap}}| \leq 2.0
\end{cases}
\label{eq:adaptive_alpha_long}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Lateral Authority Allocation: Sigmoid with Rate Limiting}
\label{sec:lat_authority}

The lateral authority allocator uses a simpler single-source design, as the lateral safety field force alone provides sufficient risk assessment during lane changes.

\subsubsection{Sigmoid Mapping}

The lateral safety force is mapped similarly to the longitudinal case, but with different parameters reflecting the lower force magnitudes typical of lateral interactions~\cite{wang_driving_2016, song_vehicle_2013}:
\begin{equation}
\lambda_{\text{lat}}[k] = \lambda_{\min} + (\lambda_{\max} - \lambda_{\min}) \cdot \sigma\!\left(k_s \cdot (|F_{\text{lat}}[k]| - F_{\text{mid}})\right)
\label{eq:sigmoid_authority_lat}
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_{\min} = 0.1$, $\lambda_{\max} = 10$, $k_s = 0.02$, and $F_{\text{mid}} = 150$~N.

The lateral authority range ($\lambda \in [0.1, 10]$) is narrower than the longitudinal range ($\lambda \in [0.1, 100]$) because excessive system authority during lane changes can produce abrupt steering corrections that compromise passenger comfort.

\subsubsection{Smoothing and Rate Limiting}

The lateral authority uses a first-order low-pass filter followed by rate limiting:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\lambda}[k] = \alpha_\lambda \cdot \lambda_{\text{target}}[k] + (1 - \alpha_\lambda) \cdot \lambda[k-1]
\label{eq:lateral_lpf}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha_\lambda = 0.02$ (very slow smoothing for comfort). The rate is then limited:
\begin{equation}
\lambda[k] = \lambda[k-1] + \text{clip}\!\left(\hat{\lambda}[k] - \lambda[k-1], \; -\Delta\lambda_{\text{down}}, \; \Delta\lambda_{\text{up}}\right)
\label{eq:lateral_rate_limit}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta\lambda_{\text{up}} = 0.02$ per step and $\Delta\lambda_{\text{down}} = 0.01$ per step. The asymmetric rate-limiting (slower release than engagement) ensures that the system does not relinquish authority prematurely following a risk event. At $T_s = 0.01$~s (lateral simulation rate), reaching maximum authority from minimum requires approximately 5 seconds.

\subsection{Conversion to Control Authority}

For both controllers, the shared control uses:
\begin{equation}
\alpha[k] = \frac{\lambda[k]}{1 + \lambda[k]}, \quad
u_{\text{shared}}[k] = \alpha[k] \cdot u_1^*[k] + (1 - \alpha[k]) \cdot u_2^*[k]
\label{eq:alpha_shared}
\end{equation}

\section{Nash Equilibrium Solution via Stacked QP}
\label{sec:solution_algorithm}
% ===================================================================

\subsection{From Best-Response to Stacked QP}

The Nash equilibrium can be derived through the best-response approach. Taking the gradient of $J_1$ with respect to $D_1$ and setting to zero:
\begin{equation}
(\mathbf{H}^T \bar{Q}_1 \mathbf{H} + \bar{R}_1) D_1 = \mathbf{H}^T \bar{Q}_1 (\mathbf{e}_1^{\text{free}} - \mathbf{H} D_2)
\label{eq:best_response_1}
\end{equation}

Similarly for Player~2:
\begin{equation}
(\mathbf{H}^T \bar{Q}_2 \mathbf{H} + \bar{R}_2) D_2 = \mathbf{H}^T \bar{Q}_2 (\mathbf{e}_2^{\text{free}} - \mathbf{H} D_1)
\label{eq:best_response_2}
\end{equation}

These coupled equations could be solved iteratively through alternating optimization~\cite{li_shared_2019}. However, to achieve DPP compliance for real-time performance, the equilibrium is reformulated as a single stacked QP over $\mathbf{u} = [D_1^T, D_2^T]^T \in \mathbb{R}^{2N_u}$:
\begin{equation}
\min_{\mathbf{u}} \; \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{u}^T \mathbf{P} \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{q}^T \mathbf{u}
\label{eq:stacked_qp}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Stacked Hessian Structure}

The Hessian $\mathbf{P} \in \mathbb{R}^{2N_u \times 2N_u}$ has the block structure:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{P} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{P}_{11} & \mathbf{P}_{12} \\ \mathbf{P}_{12}^T & \mathbf{P}_{22} \end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:P_block}
\end{equation}
with blocks given by Eqs.~\eqref{eq:P11}--\eqref{eq:P22} and the off-diagonal cross-coupling:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{P}_{12} = c(\lambda) \cdot \mathbf{H}^T \bar{Q}_1 \mathbf{H}
\label{eq:P12_full}
\end{equation}
where $c(\lambda) = \sqrt{\min(\lambda, 1)}$ ensures positive definiteness for small $\lambda$. Without this scaling, the Schur complement condition $\mathbf{P}_{22} - \mathbf{P}_{12}^T \mathbf{P}_{11}^{-1} \mathbf{P}_{12} \succ 0$ may fail when the weakened $\mathbf{P}_{22}$ (at small $\lambda$) cannot dominate the cross-coupling.

The linear term $\mathbf{q}$ updates at each time step:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{q} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \, \mathbf{H}^T \bar{Q}_1 \, \mathbf{e}_1^{\text{free}} \\ -2\lambda \, \mathbf{H}^T \bar{Q}_1 \, \mathbf{e}_2^{\text{free}} \end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:q_vector}
\end{equation}

\subsection{DPP Compliance through Pre-computed Lambda Levels}

Since $\mathbf{P}$ depends on $\lambda$, DPP compliance requires pre-computing $\mathbf{P}$ for discrete $\lambda$ levels. At runtime, the closest level is selected in log-space:
\begin{equation}
\lambda_{\text{used}} = \arg\min_{\hat{\lambda} \in \Lambda} \left| \ln \hat{\lambda} - \ln \lambda[k] \right|
\label{eq:lambda_selection}
\end{equation}

For each $\hat{\lambda} \in \Lambda$, CVXPY caches the KKT factorization, and subsequent solves only require updating $\mathbf{q}$.

\paragraph{Longitudinal levels.} $\Lambda_{\text{long}} = \{0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0, 10.0, 50.0\}$ (8 levels), covering the wide authority range $[0.1, 100]$.

\paragraph{Lateral levels.} $\Lambda_{\text{lat}} = \{0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0, 10.0\}$ (7 levels), reflecting the narrower authority range $[0.1, 10]$.

\subsection{Positive Definiteness Guarantee}

After constructing $\mathbf{P}$ for each level, the eigenvalues are verified. If $\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P}) < 10^{-6}$, regularization is applied:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{P}_{\text{reg}} = \mathbf{P} + \max(10^{-4} - \lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P}), \; \epsilon_{\text{reg}}) \cdot \mathbf{I}_{2N_u}
\label{eq:regularization}
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon_{\text{reg}} = 10^{-5}$.

\subsection{Constraints}

\subsubsection{Longitudinal Constraints}

Input bounds enforce physical acceleration limits:
\begin{equation}
-3.5 \leq u_1 \leq 2.5 \text{ m/s}^2, \quad -4.0 \leq u_2 \leq 3.0 \text{ m/s}^2
\label{eq:long_bounds}
\end{equation}

Rate constraints limit the change per time step to ensure comfort~\cite{rajamani_vehicle_2005}:
\begin{equation}
|u_i[k+j] - u_i[k+j-1]| \leq \Delta u_{i,\max} \cdot T_s
\label{eq:long_rate}
\end{equation}
with $\Delta u_{1,\max} = 1.5$~m/s$^3$ and $\Delta u_{2,\max} = 2.0$~m/s$^3$.

\subsubsection{Lateral Constraints}

Steering angle bounds~\cite{rajamani_vehicle_2005}:
\begin{equation}
-0.436 \leq \delta_i \leq 0.436 \text{ rad} \quad (\approx \pm 25^\circ)
\label{eq:lat_bounds}
\end{equation}

Steering rate constraint:
\begin{equation}
|\delta_i[k+j] - \delta_i[k+j-1]| \leq \dot{\delta}_{\max} \cdot T_s
\label{eq:lat_rate}
\end{equation}
with $\dot{\delta}_{\max} = 0.262$~rad/s ($15^\circ$/ss). This value corresponds to the Audi TT steering system specification and ensures smooth steering transitions consistent with passenger comfort expectations.

\subsection{Complete Algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:stacked_qp} presents the solution procedure, which is identical for both controllers, modulo the state-space model and constraint values.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{DPP-Compliant Nash Equilibrium via Stacked QP}
\label{alg:stacked_qp}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Initialization (once):}
\State Build prediction matrices $\mathbf{U}$, $\mathbf{H}$ from $(\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C})$
\State Compute base Hessian $\mathbf{H}^T \bar{Q}_1 \mathbf{H}$
\For{each $\hat{\lambda} \in \Lambda$}
    \State Build $\mathbf{P}(\hat{\lambda})$ using Eqs.~\eqref{eq:P11}--\eqref{eq:P12_full}
    \State Verify $\mathbf{P} \succ 0$; regularize if needed (Eq.~\eqref{eq:regularization})
    \State Create CVXPY problem with constant $\mathbf{P}$, parametric $\mathbf{q}$
\EndFor
\State
\State \textbf{Runtime (each control cycle $k$):}
\State \textbf{Input:} $\mathbf{x}[k]$, $\mathbf{R}_1$, $\mathbf{R}_2$, $\lambda[k]$
\State Select $\lambda_{\text{used}} \gets \arg\min_{\hat{\lambda} \in \Lambda} |\ln \hat{\lambda} - \ln \lambda[k]|$
\State Compute free errors: $\mathbf{e}_i^{\text{free}} = \mathbf{R}_i - \mathbf{U} \mathbf{x}[k]$
\State Update $\mathbf{q}$ via Eq.~\eqref{eq:q_vector}
\State Solve cached QP for $\lambda_{\text{used}}$ with warm start
\State Extract first controls: $u_1^*[k]$, $u_2^*[k]$
\State \Return $(u_1^*[k], u_2^*[k])$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Reference Trajectory Generation}
\label{sec:reference_generation}
% ===================================================================

\subsection{Longitudinal References}

\subsubsection{System Reference (Player 1)}

The system reference maintains a safe following distance using the Constant Time Gap (CTG) spacing policy~\cite{rajamani_vehicle_2005}:
\begin{equation}
d_{\text{des}}[k] = d_0 + h \cdot v_{\text{ego}}[k]
\label{eq:ctg_spacing_v1}
\end{equation}
where $d_0 = 5$~m is the standstill distance and $h = 1.5$~s is the time headway. The reference over the prediction horizon is:
\begin{align}
p_{\text{ref},1}[k+j] &= p_{\text{leader}}[k+j] - d_{\text{des}}[k+j] \label{eq:long_ref_pos}\\
v_{\text{ref},1}[k+j] &= v_{\text{leader}}[k+j] \label{eq:long_ref_vel}
\end{align}

The leader's future trajectory is predicted assuming constant velocity, consistent with the IDM car-following model assumptions~\cite{treiber_congested_2000, treiberTrafficFlowDynamics2013}.

\subsubsection{Human Reference (Player 2)}

The human driver's longitudinal reference is generated by simulating the vehicle's behavior under platoon control for $N_p$ steps using a deep-copy forward simulation at the faster internal rate. This approach generates a realistic prediction of human intent from current conditions, consistent with human-driver modeling practices.

\subsection{Lateral References}
\label{sec:lateral_references}

The lateral reference trajectories define smooth paths for the lane change maneuver. Both references are planned once at the start of the lane change and then tracked based on elapsed time, preventing oscillations caused by replanning from the current position.

\subsubsection{System Reference: Quintic Polynomial}

The system generates a quintic (5th-order) polynomial trajectory for maximum smoothness~\cite{gu_toward_2014}:
\begin{equation}
y_{\text{ref},1}(\tau) = y_0 + \Delta y \cdot \left(10\tau^3 - 15\tau^4 + 6\tau^5\right)
\label{eq:quintic_traj}
\end{equation}
where $\tau = t / T_{\text{lc}} \in [0, 1]$ is the normalized time, $y_0$ is the starting lateral position, $\Delta y$ is the lane change distance, and $T_{\text{lc}}$ is the lane change duration.

The quintic polynomial has zero velocity and acceleration at both endpoints ($\dot{y}(0) = \dot{y}(T_{\text{lc}}) = 0$, $\ddot{y}(0) = \ddot{y}(T_{\text{lc}}) = 0$), ensuring smooth entry and exit from the lane change maneuver.

\paragraph{Heading constraint on $T_{\text{lc}}$} A critical requirement is that the heading angle remains below a threshold throughout the maneuver. Since $\psi \approx \dot{y} / v_x$ for small angles~\cite{rajamani_vehicle_2005}, the maximum heading occurs at $\tau = 0.5$:
\begin{equation}
\dot{y}_{\max} = \frac{1.875 \, |\Delta y|}{T_{\text{lc}}}
\label{eq:ydot_max_quintic}
\end{equation}

For $\psi < \psi_{\max}$, the minimum lane change duration must satisfy:
\begin{equation}
T_{\text{lc}} \geq \frac{1.875 \, |\Delta y|}{v_x \tan(\psi_{\max})}
\label{eq:Tlc_constraint}
\end{equation}

With $\psi_{\max} \approx \SI{4}{\degree}$ ($0.070$~rad) for a normal driver, $|\Delta y| = 3.5$~m (one lane width), and $v_x = 20$~m/s, the quintic polynomial constraint yields $T_{\text{lc}} \geq 1.875 \times 3.5 / (20 \times \tan(0.070)) = 4.7$~s. The system reference duration is set to 1.5 times the corresponding human base duration, yielding 9.0~s (cautious), 6.75~s (normal), and 4.5~s (aggressive). These durations are dynamically adjusted if the heading constraint requires a longer maneuver, but in practice the personality-specific $\psi_{\max}$ values are calibrated to ensure compatibility with the base durations. These values are consistent with empirical lane change duration data: Toledo and Zohar~\cite{toledoModelingDurationLane2007} report average durations of 5--6~s for passenger cars, while Lee et al.~\cite{leeComprehensiveExaminationNaturalistic2004a} observe durations from 3~s (urgent) to over 8~s (cautious) in naturalistic driving.

The heading reference is set to zero throughout: $\psi_{\text{ref},1} = 0$.

\subsubsection{Human Reference: Cubic Polynomial}

The human driver's reference uses a cubic (3rd-order) polynomial, reflecting the tendency of human drivers to execute lane changes more directly with less emphasis on smoothness at the endpoints~\cite{gu_toward_2014, hoffmannAutonomousAutomobileTrajectory2007}:
\begin{equation}
y_{\text{ref},2}(\tau) = y_0 + \Delta y \cdot \left(3\tau^2 - 2\tau^3\right)
\label{eq:cubic_traj}
\end{equation}

The cubic polynomial has zero velocity at the endpoints but non-zero acceleration, producing a sharper transition. The heading constraint is similarly enforced:
\begin{equation}
T_{\text{lc}} \geq \frac{1.5 \, |\Delta y|}{v_x \tan(\psi_{\max})}
\label{eq:Tlc_constraint_cubic}
\end{equation}

The cubic coefficient (1.5 vs.\ 1.875 for quintic) means the human reference allows shorter lane change durations for the same heading constraint. Base durations depend on driver type: 6.0~s (cautious), 4.5~s (normal), 3.0~s (aggressive). Each driver type has a corresponding maximum heading angle calibrated from empirical lane change data~\cite{toledoModelingDurationLane2007, leeComprehensiveExaminationNaturalistic2004a}: cautious drivers maintain $\psi_{\max} \approx \SI{3}{\degree}$, normal drivers allow $\psi_{\max} \approx \SI{4}{\degree}$, and aggressive drivers tolerate $\psi_{\max} \approx \SI{6}{\degree}$.

\subsection{Phase-Dependent Reference Selection}

Both lateral reference generators operate in five phases synchronized with the safety field:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{CRUISE}: Reference at current lane position.
\item \textbf{GAP\_SEARCH}: Gradual reference shift begins.
\item \textbf{LANE\_CHANGE}: Full polynomial trajectory tracking.
\item \textbf{LANE\_KEEPING}: Reference at target lane.
\item \textbf{FOLLOWING}: Reference at target lane (longitudinal control dominant).
\end{enumerate}

Phase transitions are detected using the criteria from the MOBIL lane change model~\cite{kesting_general_2007, kesting_mobil_nodate} and the lateral safety field (Chapter~3).

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:ch4_summary}
% ===================================================================

This chapter presented the complete game-theoretic framework for shared control during platoon merging in both the longitudinal and lateral domains. The Nash game is formulated with cross-coupling cost terms ($S_1$, $S_2$) that promote cooperative equilibria. The authority ratio $\lambda$ dynamically scales the human's cost based on risk assessed through the driving safety field~\cite{wang_driving_2015, wang_driving_2016}. The key methodological contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A DPP-compliant stacked QP formulation that solves the Nash equilibrium in approximately 2--3~ms, a 20- to 30-fold improvement over iterative best-response methods~\cite{li_shared_2019}.
\item The $S = R$ cooperation principle, which applies universally to both the longitudinal and lateral domains. Setting $S_i = R_i$ for both players transforms the competitive Nash equilibrium into a cooperative one, enabling smooth convergence without domain-specific tuning of the cross-coupling ratio.
\item Dual-source authority allocation combining sigmoid-based safety authority~\cite{wang_driving_2016} with hysteresis-based performance authority for the longitudinal controller.
\item Heading-constrained reference trajectories that analytically guarantee the lane change satisfies $|\psi| < \psi_{\max}$ throughout the maneuver.
\item Driver-type modifiers in the lateral controller that adjust the Nash game weights to model cautious, normal, and aggressive driving styles~\cite{li_shared_2019}.
\end{enumerate}
