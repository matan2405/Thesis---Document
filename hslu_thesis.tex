\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

This thesis presents a novel game-theoretic framework for merging an autonomous vehicle into a platoon using Distributed Model Predictive Control (DMPC). The merging process is formulated as a non-cooperative Nash game between the autonomous system and the human driver, where cooperative behavior emerges through dynamic authority allocation based on driving risk assessment.

The key contributions of this work include: (1) a bidirectional longitudinal safety field that quantifies collision risk from both leading and following vehicles, (2) an ellipse-based lateral safety field for lane-change scenarios, (3) a Nash equilibrium solver that computes optimal control strategies for both players, and (4) a dynamic authority allocation mechanism that smoothly transitions control from the driver to the autonomous system as risk increases.

The simulation results demonstrate that the proposed approach enables safe and comfortable merging in multiple scenarios, with significant improvements in Time-to-Collision (TTC), jerk minimization, and platoon stability relative to baseline methods.

\textbf{Keywords:} Autonomous vehicles, platoon merging, game theory, Nash equilibrium, DMPC, shared control, safety field, authority allocation

\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Background}

Human-driven and autonomous vehicles will share public roads for decades before full autonomy arrives at scale. Vehicle platooning is a coordinated driving formation in which multiple vehicles travel in close succession under automated spacing control. It offers one of the clearest near-term benefits of this mixed-traffic environment: reduced aerodynamic drag for following vehicles and higher lane throughput per kilometer of road. The difficulty is entering the platoon. A human-driven vehicle that wishes to join an operating platoon must reconcile its driver's intentions with the platoon's automated spacing logic. This thesis develops a game-theoretic shared-control framework, grounded in Nash equilibrium theory, for exactly that problem.


\subsection{Vehicle Platooning}

Vehicle platooning is a driving formation in which multiple vehicles travel closely together at highway speeds, maintaining safe inter-vehicle distances through coordinated control and V2V communication \cite{treiberTrafficFlowDynamics2013}. Aerodynamic drag reduction from traveling in close formation (drafting) reduces fuel consumption by 10\% - 15\% for following vehicles, and smaller following distances allow more vehicles per kilometer of road. Coordinated braking reduces reaction times and collision risk.


Cooperative Adaptive Cruise Control (CACC) is the technology that enables coordinated platoon operation \cite{rajamaniVehicleDynamicsControl2005}. CACC systems exchange speed, acceleration, and braking intentions via Vehicle-to-Vehicle (V2V) links, allowing vehicles to respond faster than human reaction times permit. The desired inter-vehicle gap follows the Constant Time Gap (CTG) policy:
\begin{equation}
d_{\text{des}} = d_0 + h \cdot v
\label{eq:ctg_spacing_v2}
\end{equation}
where $d_0 = 2$\,m is the standstill distance, $h = 1.5$\,s is the time headway, and $v$ is the current vehicle speed. The gap therefore increases linearly with speed, providing appropriate safety margins under all operating conditions.

Most CACC systems assume that all platoon members are fully autonomous. This assumption fails in mixed traffic, where a human-driven vehicle must negotiate entry into an operating platoon. Safe merging requires explicit handling of both the human driver's intent and the platoon's automated spacing logic, a problem neither conventional CACC nor standard autonomous control addresses directly.


\subsection{Shared Control Systems}

Shared control divides driving authority between a human operator and an automation system \cite{liSharedControlNovel2019}. Unlike full autonomy or purely manual operation, shared control maintains driver engagement, provides safety assistance proportional to risk, and enables smooth transitions of authority. The core engineering challenge is computing an authority split that is safe when risk is high and minimally intrusive when risk is low.

Li et al.\ \cite{liSharedControlNovel2019} proposed mapping the instantaneous driving risk, quantified by a safety field potential, to an authority allocation ratio. This thesis adopts and extends that mapping to the platoon-merging problem, covering both longitudinal gap control and lateral lane-change execution.


\subsection{Game Theory for Vehicle Control}

Game theory provides a principled framework for systems with multiple decision-makers whose objectives are coupled but not identical \cite{basarDynamicNoncooperativeGame1999}. In the shared-control context, the human driver and the autonomous system are modeled as two players in a noncooperative differential game. Each player selects a control sequence that minimizes their own cost, given the other player's action. The Nash equilibrium is the joint strategy from which neither player benefits from deviating unilaterally.

For two players with quadratic cost functions over a finite horizon, the Nash equilibrium is computed via Distributed Model Predictive Control (DMPC). Player 1 (the autonomous system) minimizes:
\begin{equation}
J_1 = \sum_{k=0}^{N_p-1} \bigl\|\mathbf{z}[k] - \mathbf{r}_1[k]\bigr\|_{Q_1}^2 + \bigl\|u_1[k]\bigr\|_{R_1}^2
\label{eq:cost_j1_intro}
\end{equation}
Player 2 (the human driver) minimizes:
\begin{equation}
J_2 = \sum_{k=0}^{N_p-1} \bigl\|\mathbf{z}[k] - \mathbf{r}_2[k]\bigr\|_{Q_2(\lambda)}^2 + \bigl\|u_2[k]\bigr\|_{R_2}^2
\label{eq:cost_j2_intro}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{z}$ is the system output, $\mathbf{r}_i$ is each player's reference trajectory,
$Q_i$ weights output tracking errors, and $R_i$ weights each player's own control effort.
The authority ratio $\lambda$ enters via $Q_2(\lambda) = \alpha(\lambda)\,Q_1$,
where $\alpha = 1/(1+\lambda)$. As $\lambda$ increases, the human driver's tracking weight
is attenuated relative to Player~1, and the iterative best-response loop converges to a
solution in which $u_2^*$ approaches the system reference.

\subsection{Lane Change Trajectory Planning}

Platoon entry requires a lateral lane change coordinated with a longitudinal gap adjustment. The vehicle must move from its current lane into the platoon lane while avoiding collisions with platoon members.

A quintic (fifth-order) polynomial trajectory is used to generate smooth, real-time lane-change references \cite{guHumanlikeMotionPlanning2014}. The trajectory has zero velocity and acceleration at both endpoints and a continuous jerk profile. The normalized lateral progress is:
\begin{equation}
s(\tau) = 10\tau^3 - 15\tau^4 + 6\tau^5
\label{eq:quintic_polynomial}
\end{equation}
where $\tau = t / T_{lc}$ is the normalized time and $T_{lc}$ is the lane-change duration. The polynomial yields a closed-form solution that requires no iterations, making it suitable for real-time execution at 20\, Hz.

The lane-change duration $T_{lc}$ is computed dynamically from the heading angle constraint
$|\psi| \leq \psi_{\max}$. Since $\psi \approx \dot{y}/v_x$ for small angles, the minimum
permissible duration for the autonomous system's trajectory is:
\begin{equation}
T_{lc,\text{sys}} = \frac{1.875 \cdot |\Delta y|}{v_x \cdot \tan(\psi_{\max})}
\label{eq:T_lc_min}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta y = |y_{\text{platoon}} - y_{\text{ego}}(t_0)|$ is the lateral offset at the
moment the maneuver is initiated, $v_x$ is the longitudinal speed, and the factor 1.875
is derived from the maximum of the derivative of the quintic polynomial. A relaxed duration is assigned
to the human driver's trajectory:
\begin{equation}
T_{lc,\text{hum}} = \frac{1.5 \cdot |\Delta y|}{v_x \cdot \tan(\psi_{\max})}
\label{eq:T_lc_hum}
\end{equation}
The factor 1.5 accommodates the less constrained heading excursion typical of human
lane changes \cite{toledoModelingDurationLane2007, leeComprehensiveExaminationNaturalistic2004a}.
Both durations are subject to a minimum floor $T_{\min}$, computed as the time required to
bring the current lateral velocity, heading angle, and yaw rate to zero, with an absolute
lower bound of $4.0$\,s. By design, the system trajectory is longer than the human trajectory.
With $\psi_{\max} = 4^\circ$, $\Delta y = 3.5$\,m (full lane change from lane
center), and $v_x = 20$\,m/s, the constraints yield $T_{lc,\text{sys}} = 4.7$\,s and
$T_{lc,\text{hum}} = 3.75$\,s.

\subsection{Safety Field Theory}

The autonomous system requires a continuous quantitative measure of collision risk to
decide when intervention is warranted. Safety field theory, introduced by Wang \emph{et al.}
\cite{wangDrivingSafetyField2015, wangDrivingSafetyField2016} and extended to an elliptic form by
Li \emph{et al.} \cite{liSharedControlNovel2019}, provides such a measure by mapping each obstacle onto a scalar potential whose gradient defines a force on the ego vehicle.

The classical decomposition is retained:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{E}_s = \mathbf{E}_p + \mathbf{E}_v + \mathbf{E}_D
\label{eq:safety_field_intro}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{E}_p$ is the potential field from static obstacles and road
boundaries, $\mathbf{E}_v$ is the kinetic field from moving obstacles, and $\mathbf{E}_D$
is the behavior field reflecting the driver risk factor. All three components are
constructed in the elliptic form of Li \emph{et al.}, in which the isotropic distance of
the original Wang formulation is replaced by an elliptic radius whose tangential axis
stretches with the closing speed. This encodes the empirical observation that a driver
requires a larger longitudinal clearance than lateral clearance to complete an avoidance
maneuver.

Two safety fields are constructed in parallel. The \emph{lateral safety field} governs the
lane-change task and evaluates the full field force $\mathbf{F}^{\text{lat}}$ following
Li~\emph{et al.} Eq.~21, with an additional pair of $1/\mathrm{dist}$ repulsion terms at
the lane edges to keep the ego vehicle inside the roadway. The \emph{longitudinal safety
field} governs the gap-regulation task and reduces the same decomposition to the potential
component alone, expressed in the gap-error coordinate with distance and velocity weights
that subsume the kinetic and behavior contributions. Both fields use a signed convention
in which positive values are repulsive and negative values are attractive, and both are
smoothed by an exponential moving average filter that adapts the parameters continuously
to the current traffic state.

Two extensions are made to the classical single-obstacle formulation for the platoon-merging
problem. First, the longitudinal risk assessment is bidirectional: the total force combines
a leader term with a weighted follower term \cite{wangDrivingSafetyField2015}, so that both the
vehicle ahead and the vehicle behind contribute to the authority signal. The follower weight
is set to $0.5$, reflecting the asymmetric information flow characteristic of
predecessor-following topologies in cooperative adaptive cruise control. Second, a phase
machine with four discrete states (APPROACH, GAP SEARCH, MERGE, and FOLLOWING) gates
the safety response so that aggressive gap closing during MERGE gives way to a comfort-oriented
soft transition during FOLLOWING. The follower term is never faded, since rear-end safety
must remain unconditional at all phases.

\section{Problem Statement}

This thesis addresses the safe merging of a human-driven vehicle into an autonomous
platoon. The merging vehicle travels in an adjacent lane and must enter the platoon at
a designated gap without colliding with the leader or the follower. The problem is
decomposed into two subproblems, each solved within the same Nash/DMPC framework.


\subsection{Longitudinal Control Problem}

Longitudinal control regulates the vehicle's forward motion to close the target gap,
reach the correct inter-vehicle distance, and maintain it in steady state. The controller
tracks a constant-time-gap reference with standstill distance $d_0 = 2$~m and time
headway $h = 1.5$~s, respects the follower constraint arising from the vehicle behind,
limits jerk to preserve passenger comfort, and stays within the physical acceleration
envelope of the vehicle. Execution is required at $10$~Hz to close the outer control
loop of the platoon in real time.

The merging scenario places a large initial gap error under a moving follower, so the
controller must close the gap without triggering an emergency response in the trailing
vehicle. Entry before, within, or after the platoon each presents a different risk
profile and a different sign of the follower force.

\subsection{Lateral Control Problem}

Lateral control guides the vehicle from its own lane into the platoon lane along a
smooth quintic reference. Contact with platoon members is forbidden throughout the
maneuver; lateral acceleration and heading angle are kept within comfort bounds, and
the lane-change timing is coordinated with the longitudinal controller. Execution is
required at $20$~Hz.

Lateral dynamics is governed by the single-track bicycle model, whose response depends
on vehicle speed and tire properties. Because the linearization is only accurate near
the operating point, the lateral Nash solver rebuilds the state-space matrices whenever
the longitudinal speed drifts by more than $2$~m/s from the last operating point.

\subsection{Authority Allocation Problem}

Authority allocation determines how the Nash-optimal system control $u_1^{*}$ and the
Nash-optimal human control $u_2^{*}$ are combined into the command sent to the vehicle.
The shared output is
\begin{equation}
u_{\text{shared}} = u_1^{*} + u_2^{*}
\label{eq:shared_control_intro}
\end{equation}
where $u_1^{*}$ is the first-step optimal control of the autonomous system and $u_2^{*}$
is the first-step optimal control of the human player at the current Nash step.
The two controls are summed directly rather than blended by a convex combination.
Authority balance is encoded inside the Nash cost function rather than applied as an
outer weighting rule \cite{pustilnikGeneralizedNashEquilibrium2025}.

The authority balance is governed by the scalar weight
\begin{equation}
\alpha = \frac{1}{1 + \lambda}
\label{eq:alpha_lambda}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha \in (0,1]$ is the authority weight derived from the safety field force,
and $\lambda \geq 0$ is the authority ratio. The weight $\alpha$ enters the Nash
quadratic program by scaling the tracking-cost Hessian of Player~2:
\begin{equation}
P_2 = 2\bigl(\alpha\, H^{\top}Q_1 H + R_2 I\bigr)
\label{eq:P2_alpha}
\end{equation}
where $H$ is the input-to-output prediction matrix, $Q_1$ is the shared tracking weight
matrix, $R_2$ is the human player's control-effort weight, and $I$ is the identity matrix.
A reduction in $\alpha$ attenuates the tracking Hessian $H^{\top}Q_1 H$ relative to the
effort term $R_2 I$. Consequently, $u_2^*$ is driven toward zero and $u_1^*$ is allowed
to dominate $u_{\text{shared}}$. Under nominal risk ($\lambda = 0$, $\alpha = 1$) the human
player's cost is unmodified and full authority is retained. Under maximal risk
($\lambda \to \infty$, $\alpha \to 0$) the tracking Hessian vanishes and
$u_2^* \to 0$, so $u_{\text{shared}} \approx u_1^*$. The value of $\lambda$ is
determined at each control step from the safety field force computed in the preceding
step.

\section{Research Objectives}

This thesis pursues the following objectives.

The first objective is to implement a real-time Nash equilibrium solver. Two
independent solvers are constructed, one for longitudinal control and one for
lateral control, each formulated as a separate two-player differential game with no
coupling between them. Both solvers are based on an Iterative Best Response (IBR)
algorithm with a per-player OSQP backend, and each pre-factorizes its cost matrices
for a discrete set of authority-ratio levels (eleven levels for the longitudinal
solver and seven for the lateral solver) so that the closest level can be selected
without rebuilding the quadratic program. Warm-starting from the shifted horizon
of the previous solution is applied at every control step. The target execution
rates are $10$\, Hz for longitudinal control and $20$\, Hz for lateral control.

The second objective is to implement the $\alpha$-scaling authority mechanism of
Pustilnik and Borrelli \cite{pustilnikGeneralizedNashEquilibrium2025}. The authority ratio $\lambda$ is mapped to a scalar weight $\alpha = 1/(1+\lambda)$ that multiplies the tracking cost
of Player~2 in the Nash quadratic program, so that the safety-field-driven
authority balance is encoded inside the Nash cost function rather than applied as
an outer blending rule on the shared control.

The third objective is to develop phase-aware safety field models. A four-state
phase machine (APPROACH, GAP SEARCH, MERGE, and FOLLOWING) is introduced, with
transition conditions derived from gap error, relative velocity, and lateral
alignment. The safety response is aggressive during MERGE to close the gap, and
attenuated during FOLLOWING to prioritize passenger comfort. A dwell-time
requirement on entry into FOLLOWING prevents chattering at phase boundaries.


The fourth objective is to develop a bidirectional longitudinal safety field. The
field quantifies risk from both the preceding vehicle and the following vehicle,
with asymmetric weighting that reflects the forward-dominant information flow of
predecessor-following topologies.

The fifth objective is to design an elliptic lateral safety field. A lateral field
based on the Li \emph{et al.} \cite{liSharedControlNovel2019} elliptic \emph{driving safety
field} is derived, with velocity-corrected kinetic terms, road-boundary repulsion,
and a $\tanh$ sign function that avoids force discontinuities at lateral alignment.

The sixth objective is to develop an online driver-calibration mechanism. The human
driver's time headway $T$ is estimated online: during gap-closing by inverting the
Intelligent Driver Model (IDM), and during following by direct gap measurement.
Estimates are smoothed over a twenty-sample circular buffer (approximately $2$\,s
at the $10$\,Hz Nash rate) and clipped to the physiological range
$T \in [0.5, 3.0]$\,s, with a population-mean fallback of $T = 1.5$\,s until the
buffer is full.

The seventh objective is to implement a common architecture for the two independent
controllers. The longitudinal and lateral solvers share the same Nash/IBR structure,
the same phase-detection logic, and the same authority-allocation mechanism, and
both are implemented in Unity with a C\# wrapper for the OSQP solver.

The eighth objective is to validate the system through simulation. Three merging
scenarios are tested (joining before, within, and after the platoon) with
metrics covering safety (gap error, time-to-collision (TTC)), comfort (longitudinal
jerk, lateral acceleration), and shared-control behavior (authority distribution
over time).

\section{Thesis Contributions}

This thesis makes the following contributions.

The first contribution is an IBR Nash equilibrium solver with $\lambda$-cached OSQP
instances. The Nash game is solved by iterative best response, with a separate OSQP
quadratic program per player. Cost matrices are pre-factorized for eleven
longitudinal $\lambda$ levels and seven lateral $\lambda$ levels, and the closest
level is selected at each control step by minimizing $|\ln\hat{\lambda} - \ln\lambda_k|$. Horizon-shift warm-starting reduces the OSQP iteration count, meeting the real-time budgets of the $10$\, Hz and $20$\, Hz control loops. The longitudinal and lateral solvers are constructed independently, with no cross-coupling between their state or control variables.

The second contribution is the direct-sum shared-control formulation with
$\alpha$-scaled Nash costs. The shared control is computed as $u_{\text{shared}} = u_1^{*} + u_2^{*}$ without an outer convex combination, since the authority balance is absorbed into the Player~2 cost through the $\alpha$-scaling of Pustilnik and Borrelli \cite{pustilnikGeneralizedNashEquilibrium2025}, where $\alpha = 1/(1+\lambda)$ multiplies the tracking-weight term of Player~2. The resulting equilibrium is shown to converge to a cooperative solution across the full range $\lambda \in [0, \infty)$, without the oscillatory instabilities that arise when authority is applied as an outer weighted sum on independent single-player controllers.

The third contribution is a phase-detection mechanism with dwell-time locking. Entry
into FOLLOWING requires the normalized gap error $|\Delta g| / d_{\text{des}}$ to
fall below $0.10$, the velocity error $|v_{\text{ego}} - v_{\text{leader}}|$ to fall
below $0.5$\,m/s, and the gap-error rate $|\dot{(\Delta g)}|$ to fall below
$0.3$\,m/s, with all conditions sustained for $5$\,s. A quadratic soft-transition
factor further attenuates the safety field force as the gap error decreases within
the FOLLOWING-band threshold ($0.15\, d_{\text{des}}$), preventing abrupt changes in
control demand at the phase boundary.

The fourth contribution of this thesis is a bidirectional longitudinal safety field that
combines an elliptic leader force \cite{liSharedControlNovel2019} with a
time-to-collision (TTC) force floor and a follower-weighted rear force.
The TTC floor is defined as
\begin{equation}
F_{\text{TTC}} = \text{ttc}_{\text{frac}} \cdot F_{\max}, \qquad
\text{ttc}_{\text{frac}} = 1 - \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\tfrac{\mathrm{TTC} - \mathrm{TTC}_{\text{crit}}}{\mathrm{TTC}_{\text{warn}} - \mathrm{TTC}_{\text{crit}}}, 0, 1\right)
\label{eq:ttc_floor}
\end{equation}
where $F_{\max} = 800$\,N is the saturation force, $\mathrm{TTC}_{\text{warn}} = 5.5$\,s
is the TTC threshold at which the floor begins to rise (Urgency~2 upper boundary,
Lee~\cite{hsAnalysesRearEndCrashes2007}), and $\mathrm{TTC}_{\text{crit}} = 3.0$\,s is the TTC at
which the floor reaches saturation (Urgency~3 ``forced'' threshold,
Lee~\cite{hsAnalysesRearEndCrashes2007}).
The leader force is saturated whenever the bumper-to-bumper gap falls below the minimum
safe distance, defined as
\begin{equation}
d_{\min}(v_x) = \max\!\bigl(d_{\min}^{\text{floor}},\; \tau_{\min} \cdot v_x\bigr)
\label{eq:d_min}
\end{equation}
where $d_{\min}^{\text{floor}} = 5$\,m is the low-speed floor, $\tau_{\min} = 1.0$\,s is
the critical time headway as defined by Wang~\emph{et al.}~\cite{wangDrivingSafetyField2016},
$v_x$ is the ego longitudinal speed, and $w_f = 0.5$ is the weighting coefficient applied
to the rear-vehicle contribution at steady state.
A linear interpolation region of width $1$\,m above $d_{\min}(v_x)$ is introduced, over
which the force rises from zero to $F_{\max}$ as the gap closes.
At $v_x = 20$\,m/s this yields $d_{\min} = 20$\,m and an emergency boundary at $21$\,m.

The fifth contribution is an elliptic lateral safety field. The field is derived
from the Li \emph{et al.} \cite{liSharedControlNovel2019} elliptic \emph{driving safety
field}, with a Wang \emph{et al.}\ \cite{wangDrivingSafetyField2015, wangDrivingSafetyField2016}
speed-dependent virtual mass, velocity-corrected kinetic terms with
$K_1 = K_2 = 0.005$, and a $\tanh$ smoothing function with $\sigma = 0.5$\,m for the
lateral force direction. Road-boundary repulsion is added via a
$1/\mathrm{dist}$ potential active within $3$\,m of the lane edge.

The sixth contribution is an online IDM driver-calibration method. During
gap-closing, the human's time headway is estimated by inverting the IDM formula
given the observed gap, velocity, and acceleration. During steady-state following,
it is estimated directly as $T = (d - s_0) / v_x$, where $d$ is the measured gap,
$s_0$ is the standstill distance, and $v_x$ is the ego longitudinal velocity. A
twenty-sample circular buffer smooths the estimate, which converges within
approximately $2$\,s to a driver-specific value used to personalize both the human
reference trajectory and the $R_2$ weight schedule.

The seventh contribution is a Unity-based simulation framework. The complete system
is implemented in Unity with the Universal Render Pipeline and a C\#
wrapper for the OSQP solver. The vehicle plant is a nonlinear six-degree-of-freedom
model \cite{belousovDevelopmentVehicleSimulation} integrated by fourth-order Runge--Kutta at $100$\, Hz, with a Rajamani parabolic tire model calibrated to Audi TT Coup\'{e} parameters.
Per-tick telemetry is logged to CSV files and analyzed by a Python post-processing
pipeline.

\section{Thesis Organization}

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows.

\textbf{Chapter~2: Mathematical Modeling} presents the vehicle-dynamics models used
throughout the thesis. A distinction is drawn between the plant used for simulation, 
a nonlinear six-degree-of-freedom vehicle model integrated by fourth-order
Runge--Kutta at $100$\, Hz  and the reduced models seen by the controllers: a

\textbf{Chapter~3: Safety Field Formulations} describes the risk-quantification

\chapter{Mathematical Modeling}

\section{Introduction}

This chapter describes the vehicle-dynamics models used throughout the thesis. A distinction is drawn between the \emph{plant model}, which governs the simulation, and the \emph{controller models}, which the Nash solvers use for prediction.

The plant is a nonlinear six-degree-of-freedom model derived from Belousov
\cite{belousovDevelopmentVehicleSimulation}. It integrates the coupled longitudinal, lateral, and yaw dynamics by fourth-order Runge Kutta at \SI{100}{Hz}, with a
parabolic tire force law (Rajamani \cite{rajamaniVehicleDynamicsControl2005},
Eq.~13.45) calibrated to the Audi TT Coup\'{e} 2.0~TFSI. This model constitutes the
ground truth against which all control outputs are evaluated.

The Nash solvers operate on reduced linear models that are rebuit at each control step
from the current operating point. The longitudinal solver uses a double-integrator
state-space model with state $[x,\, v_x]^\top$, discretized at \SI{10}{Hz}. The
lateral solver uses a two-degree-of-freedom bicycle model with state
$[y,\, \dot{y},\, \psi,\, \dot{\psi}]^\top$, discretized at \SI{20}{Hz}. Both
controller models are derived by linearizing the plant around the current speed and
applying a Zero-Order Hold (ZOH) discretization.

Section~\ref{sec:plant} presents the plant model. Sections~\ref{sec:long_model}
and~\ref{sec:lat_model} derive the two controller models. Section~\ref{sec:multirate}
describes the multi-rate timing architecture.

\section{Plant Model}
\label{sec:plant}

The simulation plant is a nonlinear six-degree-of-freedom (6-DOF) vehicle model
derived from Belousov \cite{belousovDevelopmentVehicleSimulation}. The state vector
is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{s} = \bigl[x,\; v_x,\; y,\; v_y,\; \psi,\; \dot\psi\bigr]^\top
\label{eq:plant_state}
\end{equation}
where $x$ is the longitudinal position, $v_x$ the longitudinal speed, $y$ the
lateral position, $v_y$ the body-frame lateral velocity, $\psi$ the yaw angle, and
$\dot\psi$ the yaw rate. All quantities are expressed in the vehicle body frame.

\subsection{Equations of Motion}

The equations of motion follow Belousov Eqs.~3.11a--c:
\begin{align}
m_{\text{eff}}\,\dot v_x &= F_{x,c}\cos\delta - F_{yf}\sin\delta - F_{x,r}
\label{eq:plant_long} \\
m\,\dot v_y              &= F_{yf}\cos\delta + F_{yr} + F_{xf,c}\sin\delta
                            - f_v\,v_x\dot\psi
\label{eq:plant_lat} \\
I_z\,\ddot\psi           &= l_f\bigl(F_{yf}\cos\delta + F_{xf,c}\sin\delta\bigr)
                            - l_r F_{yr}
\label{eq:plant_yaw}
\end{align}
where $F_{x,c}$ is the net contact-patch longitudinal force, $F_{x,r}$ collects
body-level resistances (aerodynamic drag, rolling resistance, road grade), $F_{xf,c}$
is the front-axle contact force, $\delta$ is the front-wheel steer angle, $m$ is the
vehicle mass, and $m_{\text{eff}} = m + I_w/r^2$ (Belousov Eq.~3.8) augments the
inertia with the rotational inertia of the driven wheels referred to the vehicle body.
The factor $f_v = \tfrac{1}{2}(\tanh(10|v_x|-8)+1)$ gates the centripetal coupling
terms so that they decay smoothly to zero near standstill, where the body-frame
velocity states lose physical meaning.

The world-frame lateral position and its rate are recovered by the kinematic relations
\begin{equation}
\dot y = -(v_x\sin\psi + v_y\cos\psi), \qquad
y = x_0 - x
\label{eq:plant_world_y}
\end{equation}
where $x_0$ is the lateral spawn coordinate.

\subsection{Tire Model}

Lateral tire forces are computed from the parabolic pressure-distribution model of
Rajamani \cite{rajamaniVehicleDynamicsControl2005}, Eq.~13.45:
\begin{equation}
F_y(\alpha) =
\begin{cases}
\mu F_z\!\left(3\theta\tan\alpha - 3\theta^2\tan^2\!\alpha + \theta^3\tan^3\!\alpha\right)
  & |\tan\alpha| \leq 1/\theta \\
\operatorname{sgn}(\alpha)\,\mu F_z & |\tan\alpha| > 1/\theta
\end{cases}
\label{eq:tire_parabolic}
\end{equation}
where $\theta = C_\alpha/(3\mu F_z)$, $C_\alpha$ is the per-wheel cornering
stiffness, $\mu = 0.8$ is the road friction coefficient, $F_z$ is the wheel normal
load, and $\alpha$ is the tire slip angle. The model recovers a linear
force law $F_y \approx C_\alpha\alpha$ for small $\alpha$ and saturates at
$\mu F_z$ under full sliding.

The Audi TT Coup\'{e} 2.0~TFSI is front-wheel-drive with a 62/38 front-to-rear mass
distribution. Applying the Pacejka rule ($C_\alpha \approx 9.2\,F_{z,\text{wheel}}$)
to the nominal axle loads gives asymmetric per-wheel cornering stiffnesses:
\begin{equation}
C_{af} = 36\,500\ \text{N/rad}, \qquad C_{ar} = 22\,300\ \text{N/rad}
\label{eq:tire_stiffness}
\end{equation}
These values are consistent with the compact FWD range reported in Rajamani
\cite{rajamaniVehicleDynamicsControl2005}, Ch.~2.

\subsection{Numerical Integration}

The 6-DOF state is advanced by fourth-order Runge Kutta (RK4) at \SI{100}{Hz}
($\Delta t_{\text{sim}} = 0.01$~s). RK4 is chosen over Euler integration because the
stiff tire-yaw coupling in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:plant_lat}--\eqref{eq:plant_yaw} causes
first-order methods to diverge at this step size for the vehicle parameters used.

\subsection{Linearization for Nash Solvers}

At each Nash control step, the plant provides linearized Jacobians that the solvers
use to build their prediction models. The longitudinal Jacobian (method
\texttt{GetLongJacobian}) linearizes Eq.~\eqref{eq:plant_long} around the current
operating point ($\delta \approx 0$, $\dot\psi \approx 0$) with feedforward
cancellation of drag and rolling resistance, yielding the double-integrator structure
described in Section~\ref{sec:long_model}. The lateral Jacobian (method
\texttt{GetLatJacobian}) linearizes Eqs.~\eqref{eq:plant_lat}--\eqref{eq:plant_yaw}
around straight driving, yielding the bicycle-model structure described in
Section~\ref{sec:lat_model}. Both Jacobians are recomputed at every solver invocation
to track changes in $v_x$.

\section{Longitudinal Controller Model}
\label{sec:long_model}

The longitudinal Nash solver operates on a linearized double-integrator model
obtained from \texttt{GetLongJacobian()} at each control step. The state vector is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_{\text{long}} = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ v_x \end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:long_state}
\end{equation}
where $x$ is the longitudinal position and $v_x$ is the longitudinal speed.

\subsection{Continuous-Time Representation}

Linearizing Eq.~\eqref{eq:plant_long} around straight driving ($\delta \approx 0$,
$\dot\psi \approx 0$) with feedforward cancellation of drag and rolling resistance
yields
\begin{equation}
\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{long}} = A_c\,\mathbf{x}_{\text{long}} + B_c\,u
\label{eq:long_continuous}
\end{equation}
where the control input $u = a_{\text{des}}$ is the desired longitudinal acceleration
in m/s² and
\begin{equation}
A_c = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -d_{\text{lin}} \end{bmatrix}, \quad
B_c = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:long_matrices_continuous}
\end{equation}
The term $d_{\text{lin}} = \rho C_d A_f |v_x| / m_{\text{eff}}$ is the
linearized aerodynamic drag coefficient at the current speed. At highway speeds
($v_x = 20$~m/s) this evaluates to $d_{\text{lin}} \approx 0.02$~s$^{-1}$, which is
small relative to the closed-loop bandwidth; the model therefore reduces to the
standard double integrator $A_c = \bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$
to good approximation.

The output equation is:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{y} = C\,\mathbf{x}_{\text{long}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\mathbf{x}_{\text{long}}
\label{eq:long_output}
\end{equation}
so both position and velocity are available as outputs for Nash cost evaluation.

\subsection{Discrete-Time Representation}

Zero-Order Hold (ZOH) discretization at $\Delta t = 0.1$~s yields
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_{\text{long}}[k+1] = A_d\,\mathbf{x}_{\text{long}}[k] + B_d\,u[k]
\label{eq:long_discrete}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
A_d = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0.1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
B_d = \begin{bmatrix} 0.005 \\ 0.1 \end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:Ad_Bd_10Hz}
\end{equation}
These matrices correspond to the kinematic updates
\begin{align}
z[k+1]   &= z[k] + v_x[k]\,\Delta t + \tfrac{1}{2}\,u[k]\,\Delta t^2
\label{eq:pos_update} \\
v_x[k+1] &= v_x[k] + u[k]\,\Delta t
\label{eq:vel_update}
\end{align}

\subsection{Control Constraints}
\label{sec:long_constraints}

Two levels of constraints are enforced by the Nash solver. At the first level, wide solver
ceilings are imposed as box constraints on the OSQP quadratic program:
\begin{equation}
u \in [-20,\; 10]\ \text{m/s}^2, \qquad
|\Delta u| \leq 15\ \text{m/s}^2\text{/step}
\label{eq:long_solver_box}
\end{equation}
where $u$ is the commanded longitudinal acceleration, and $\Delta u = u[k] - u[k-1]$ is
the inter-step increment. These bounds serve as numerical guardrails rather than comfort
limits; physical saturation is addressed by the second level.

At the second level, physical bounds are computed at each control step from the
current vehicle state via \texttt{UpdatePhysicalBounds()}, which clips the commanded
acceleration to the envelope that the powertrain and brake system can actually deliver.

For the human player, the inter-step rate is bounded by
\begin{equation}
|\Delta u_2| \leq 4.0\ \text{m/s}^2\text{/step}
\quad \longrightarrow \quad
0.4\ \text{m/s}^2\text{/s at } 10\,\text{Hz}
\label{eq:long_human_jerk}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta u_2 = u_2[k] - u_2[k-1]$ is the inter-step change in the human player's
optimal control. Inter-step oscillations in $u_2^*$ are thereby suppressed while the
Nash game's freedom to assign a nonzero human control is preserved. Comfort shaping beyond
this hard cap is handled through the $R_2$ weight schedule described in
Chapter~\ref{ch:nash_framework}.

\section{Lateral Vehicle Model}
\label{sec:lat_vehicle_model}

\subsection{The Bicycle Model}

The lateral dynamics of the ego vehicle are described by the standard single-track bicycle
model \cite{rajamaniVehicleDynamicsControl2005}. The equations of motion follow from Newton's second law
for lateral translation and yaw rotation:
\begin{align}
m \ddot{y}_{\text{b}} &= F_{yf} + F_{yr} \label{eq:lat_force} \\
I_z \ddot{\psi}        &= l_f F_{yf} - l_r F_{yr} \label{eq:yaw_moment}
\end{align}
where $m$ is the vehicle mass, $I_z$ is the yaw moment of inertia, $F_{yf}$ and $F_{yr}$
are the lateral tire forces at the front and rear axles, $l_f$ is the distance from the
center of gravity to the front axle, and $l_r$ is the distance from the center of gravity
to the rear axle.

The plant model integrates these equations using a load-dependent nonlinear tire force
function. For the Nash solver, which requires a linear state-space model for QP
construction, the equations of motion are linearized at the current longitudinal
velocity $v_x$.

\subsection{State Variables}

The state vector is defined in the global frame as
$\mathbf{x} = [y,\; \dot{y}_{\text{b}},\; \psi,\; \dot{\psi}]^{\top}$,
where $y$ is the global lateral position, $\dot{y}_{\text{b}}$ is the body-frame lateral
velocity, $\psi$ is the heading angle, and $\dot{\psi}$ is the yaw rate. The global lateral
position is related to the body-frame lateral velocity by
\begin{equation}
\dot{y} \approx \dot{y}_{\text{b}} + v_x \psi
\label{eq:global_y}
\end{equation}
where the small-angle approximation $\sin\psi \approx \psi$ holds for the heading angles
encountered during controlled lane changes.

\subsection{Global-Frame Bicycle Model}

Under the small-angle assumption, the axle tire forces are expressed in terms of
per-wheel cornering stiffness $C_{af}$ (front) and $C_{ar}$ (rear):
\begin{align}
F_{yf} &= -2 C_{af} \alpha_f \label{eq:front_tire} \\
F_{yr} &= -2 C_{ar} \alpha_r \label{eq:rear_tire}
\end{align}
where the factor of two converts per-wheel stiffness to the full-axle value, and the
slip angles are
\begin{align}
\alpha_f &= \frac{\dot{y}_{\text{b}} + l_f \dot{\psi}}{v_x} - \delta \label{eq:front_slip} \\
\alpha_r &= \frac{\dot{y}_{\text{b}} - l_r \dot{\psi}}{v_x} \label{eq:rear_slip}
\end{align}
where $\delta$ is the front wheel steering angle. These expressions are substituted into
the equations of motion, and the kinematic relation $\dot{y} \approx \dot{y}_{\text{b}} + v_x\psi$
from Eq.~\eqref{eq:global_y} is applied, yielding the continuous-time state-space
representation
\begin{equation}
\dot{\mathbf{x}} = A_c(v_x)\, \mathbf{x} + B_c\, \delta
\label{eq:lat_ss_continuous}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{x}$ is the state vector and $\delta$ is the steering input. The
velocity-dependent state matrix is
\begin{equation}
A_c = \begin{bmatrix}
0 & 1 & v_x & 0 \\[6pt]
0 & -\dfrac{2(C_{af}+C_{ar})}{m v_x} & 0 &
    \dfrac{2(l_r C_{ar} - l_f C_{af})}{m v_x} - v_x \\[6pt]
0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\[6pt]
0 & \dfrac{2(l_r C_{ar} - l_f C_{af})}{I_z v_x} & 0 &
   -\dfrac{2(l_f^2 C_{af} + l_r^2 C_{ar})}{I_z v_x}
\end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:Ac_lat}
\end{equation}
and the input matrix is
\begin{equation}
B_c = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\[4pt] \dfrac{2 C_{af}}{m} \\[4pt] 0 \\[4pt]
\dfrac{2 l_f C_{af}}{I_z} \end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:Bc_lat}
\end{equation}
where $m$ is the vehicle mass, $I_z$ is the yaw moment of inertia, $l_f$ and $l_r$
are the distances from the center of gravity to the front and rear axles, respectively,
and $v_x$ is the longitudinal speed at the current linearization point. The
$(1,3)$ entry of $A_c$ equals $v_x$, not zero. This term arises directly from the
kinematic identity $\dot{y} = \dot{y}_{\text{b}} + v_x\psi$: differentiating the global
lateral position $y$ introduces a coupling between the heading angle $\psi$ and the
global lateral velocity $\dot{y}$ that is proportional to $v_x$. Because $A_c$ depends
on $v_x$, the model constitutes a linear parameter-varying (LPV) system. The matrices
are recomputed, and the OSQP prediction matrices rebuilt, whenever the longitudinal
speed deviates by more than $2\;\mathrm{m/s}$ from the last linearization point.

\subsection{Discrete-Time Representation}

The continuous-time matrices are converted to discrete time by zero-order hold (ZOH)
Discretization at the Nash control period $\Delta t_{\mathrm{lat}} = 0.05\;\mathrm{s}$
(20~Hz), with the matrix exponential evaluated numerically. Between Nash updates, the
most recent steering command is held constant.

\subsection{Vehicle Parameters}

The bicycle model parameters are based on an Audi~TT Coup\'e 2.0~TFSI (230~PS)
\cite{rajamaniVehicleDynamicsControl2005}. The parameters are listed in
Table~\ref{tab:vehicle_params_ch2}.

\subsection{Sampling Rate Selection}

The vehicle dynamics simulation runs at 100~Hz ($\Delta t_{\mathrm{sim}} = 0.01\;\mathrm{s}$),
which provides accurate integration of the bicycle model equations. The lateral Nash solver
operates at 20~Hz ($\Delta t_{\mathrm{lat}} = 0.05\;\mathrm{s}$), meaning the QP is solved
every fifth simulation step. At the nominal velocity of 20~m/s, the dominant yaw time
constant of the bicycle model is approximately $0.15$--$0.3\;\mathrm{s}$, and the 20~Hz
rate provides three to six control updates per time constant, which is sufficient for
accurate trajectory tracking.

\section{Human Driver Models}
\label{sec:human_driver}

\subsection{Longitudinal: Online IDM Calibration}

The human driver's longitudinal reference trajectory is generated by the Intelligent
Driver Model (IDM) \cite{treiberCongestedTrafficStates2000}, with parameters calibrated online to
the observed driver behavior. The IDM acceleration is:
\begin{equation}
a_{\text{IDM}} = a_{\max} \left[ 1 - \left( \frac{v}{v_0} \right)^\delta
- \left( \frac{s^*(v, \Delta v)}{s} \right)^2 \right]
\label{eq:idm}
\end{equation}
where $a_{\max}$ is the maximum acceleration, $v$ is the current velocity, $v_0$ is the
desired velocity, $s$ is the current gap, $\delta = 4$ is the acceleration exponent, and
$s^*$ is the desired gap:
\begin{equation}
s^*(v, \Delta v) = s_0 + v T + \frac{v \Delta v}{2\sqrt{a_{\max} b}}
\label{eq:idm_desired_gap}
\end{equation}
where $s_0$ is the minimum standstill gap, $T$ is the desired time headway, $\Delta v$ is
the velocity difference to the lead vehicle, and $b$ is the comfortable deceleration.

The time headway parameter $T$ is estimated online from observed following behaviour using
the method of Zhang and Sun \cite{zhangBayesianCalibrationIntelligent2024}. A circular buffer of 20 samples is
maintained; at each longitudinal Nash tick (10~Hz), a new sample is admitted when the
ego vehicle is following a visible leader with $|\Delta v| < 2\;\mathrm{m/s}$ and a
positive headway. The headway regression yields $\hat{T}$, which is clamped to
$[0.5,\;3.0]\;\mathrm{s}$ and applied with a fallback of $T = 1.5\;\mathrm{s}$ when
fewer than ten samples have been collected. The remaining IDM parameters ($a_{\max}$,
$s_0$, $b$) are fixed at values calibrated from the HighD naturalistic dataset
\cite{krajewskiHighDDatasetDrone2018}: $a_{\max} = 0.553\;\mathrm{m/s^2}$,
$s_0 = 3.416\;\mathrm{m}$, $b = 1.649\;\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

\subsection{Lateral: Neuromuscular Steering Model}

The human driver's lateral reference trajectory is generated by a quintic polynomial
lane-change planner with a neuromuscular steering model following Swain and Rath
\cite{swainCooperativeGameTheory2023}. The neuromuscular filter models the biomechanical lag between
the driver's intended steering input and the actual wheel angle:
\begin{equation}
J\ddot{\delta} + B\dot{\delta} + K\delta = K\delta_{\text{cmd}}
\label{eq:neuromuscular}
\end{equation}
where $J = 3.7 \times 10^{-3}\;\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2}$ is the steering column inertia,
$B = 0.1363\;\mathrm{N\cdot m\cdot s/rad}$ is the damping coefficient, $K = 1.1742\;\mathrm{N\cdot m/rad}$
is the stiffness, and $\delta_{\text{cmd}}$ is the commanded steering angle from the
quintic planner. The lane-change duration for the human trajectory is computed from the
relaxed heading constraint (Section~\ref{sec:lat_traj_planning}). Steering output is
saturated at the vehicle's physical limit and passed through an EMA filter
($\alpha = 0.2$) to suppress high-frequency noise.


\section{Summary}

This chapter presented the mathematical models underlying the vehicle dynamics simulation.
The longitudinal model is a double integrator with position and velocity as state variables,
discretized at 10~Hz by zero-order hold. The lateral model is a four-state bicycle model
with state vector $\mathbf{x} = [y,\;\dot{y}_{\text{b}},\;\psi,\;\dot{\psi}]^{\top}$,
linearized at the current longitudinal velocity and discretized at 20~Hz. The cornering
stiffness values ($C_{af} = 36\,500\;\mathrm{N/rad}$, $C_{ar} = 22\,300\;\mathrm{N/rad}$)
reflect the asymmetric FWD weight distribution of the Audi TT platform.

Vehicle dynamics are integrated at 100~Hz. The lateral Nash solver operates at 20~Hz,
exclusively during the MERGE and FOLLOWING phases, while the longitudinal Nash solver
operates at 10~Hz throughout the merging sequence.

The human driver's longitudinal reference is generated by the IDM with online time-headway
calibration (Zhang and Sun \cite{zhangBayesianCalibrationIntelligent2024}), and the lateral reference by a quintic
polynomial planner with a neuromuscular steering filter (Swain and Rath \cite{swainCooperativeGameTheory2023}).
Both models provide the reference trajectories consumed by the Nash solver described in
Chapter~4.

\chapter{Safety Field Formulations}

This chapter develops the mathematical framework for quantifying collision risk in both the longitudinal and lateral directions. Both directions rest on a shared theoretical foundation: the \textit{driving safety field} (DSF) introduced by Wang et al.~\cite{wangDrivingSafetyField2015, wangDrivingSafetyField2016} and extended to elliptic obstacle representations by Li et al.~\cite{liSharedControlNovel2019}. The longitudinal field applies an elliptic potential to regulate the gap between the ego vehicle and its platoon neighbors; the lateral field applies the full Wang--Li formulation to all platoon members visible in the adjacent lane, generating repulsive forces that prevent lateral collisions during lane change. Both fields supply a common authority allocation mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:authority_allocation}) that determines the degree to which the automated system overrides the human driver within the Nash equilibrium framework. The implementation also introduces a dynamic phase detection system that adapts both fields to the current operating context, distinguishing active merging from steady-state following in the longitudinal direction and a five-phase lane-change sequence in the lateral direction.

Section~\ref{sec:dsf_theory} introduces the theoretical foundation of driving safety field theory. Section~\ref{sec:longitudinal_field} describes the bidirectional longitudinal safety field with phase detection. Section~\ref{sec:lateral_field} presents the Wang--Li lateral safety field. Section~\ref{sec:authority_allocation} defines the dynamic authority allocator. Section~\ref{sec:nash_integration} describes the coupling to the Nash equilibrium controller. \section{Driving Safety Field Theory}
\label{sec:dsf_theory}

The driving safety field (DSF) framework, introduced by Wang et al.~\cite{wangDrivingSafetyField2015, wangDrivingSafetyField2016}, extends classical potential field methods from robotics to multi-vehicle traffic environments by representing collision risk as a continuous scalar field over the road plane. Following Li et al.~\cite{liSharedControlNovel2019}, the total field decomposes into three additive components:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{E}_s = \mathbf{E}_p + \mathbf{E}_k + \mathbf{E}_b
\label{eq:total_safety_field}
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{E}_p$ captures static obstacles through a position-dependent potential, $\mathbf{E}_k$ represents the kinetic interaction between moving vehicles, and $\mathbf{E}_b$ encodes driver behavioural characteristics. In platoon-merging scenarios the kinetic component $\mathbf{E}_k$ dominates: all vehicles travel at highway speed, so collision risk arises primarily from closing velocities rather than from stationary obstacles.

The field strength at a given position produces a longitudinal or lateral control force according to~\cite{wangDrivingSafetyField2016}:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{F}_{fi} = E_{si} \, M_i \, R_i \, \exp\!\left(-k_2 \, v_i \cos\theta_i\right)\left(1 + DR_i\right)
\label{eq:field_force}
\end{equation}

where $M_i$ is the virtual mass of vehicle $i$, $R_i$ is a road-condition factor, $v_i$ is its speed, $\theta_i$ is the angle between the vehicle's velocity vector and the field gradient direction, and $DR_i$ is a driver-risk factor that scales the overall force with the assessed riskiness of the driver's behaviour. The virtual mass depends non-linearly on vehicle speed~\cite{wangDrivingSafetyField2016}:

\begin{equation}
M_i = m_0 \left( c_s \, |v_i|^{c_e} + c_o \right)
\label{eq:virtual_mass}
\end{equation}

where $m_0 = 1304\,\mathrm{kg}$ is the physical vehicle mass and the coefficients $c_s = 1.566 \times 10^{-14}$, $c_e = 6.687$, and $c_o = 0.3345$ were calibrated from accident statistics~\cite{wangDrivingSafetyField2016}.

Li et al.~\cite{liSharedControlNovel2019} extended the Wang framework to non-circular obstacle representations by replacing the Euclidean distance with an elliptic distance measure that accounts for the asymmetry between the longitudinal and lateral extents of the safety envelope. The field strength around a single obstacle becomes:

\begin{equation}
E = \frac{G \, R_{\mathrm{obs}} \, M_{\mathrm{obs}}}{r^*} \, \exp\!\left(k_1 \, v_{\mathrm{obs}} \cos\theta_v\right)
\label{eq:li_field_strength}
\end{equation}

where $G = 6 \times 10^{-3}$ is a scaling constant, $R_{\mathrm{obs}}$ is the road-condition factor of the obstacle vehicle, and the kinetic correction term $\exp(k_1 v_{\mathrm{obs}} \cos\theta_v)$ amplifies the field strength when the obstacle moves toward the ego vehicle. The elliptic distance $r^*$ is defined as:

\begin{equation}
r^* = \sqrt{\left(\frac{d_x}{a}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{d_y}{b}\right)^2}
\label{eq:elliptic_distance}
\end{equation}

where $d_x$ and $d_y$ are the longitudinal and lateral separations between the ego and obstacle vehicles, and the semi-axes $a = \max(|\Delta v| \cdot T_s,\, a_{\min})$ and $b = \tau$ adapt to relative velocity, preventing the field from collapsing to zero when the vehicles share the same longitudinal position. The parameters $T_s = 2.0\,\mathrm{s}$, $a_{\min} = 2.0\,\mathrm{m}$, and $\tau = 2.0\,\mathrm{m}$ are fixed. The resultant force on the ego vehicle is~\cite{liSharedControlNovel2019}:

\begin{equation}
F_r = E \, M_{\mathrm{ego}} \, R_{\mathrm{ego}} \, \exp\!\left(-k_2 \, v_{\mathrm{ego}} \cos\theta_i\right)\left(1 + DR\right)
\label{eq:li_force}
\end{equation}

The Wang speed-dependent virtual mass and the Li elliptic distance are combined throughout the implementation. The longitudinal safety field applies the elliptic potential to quantify risk from the leader and follower vehicles; the lateral safety field applies the full Wang--Li formulation to all platoon members in the adjacent lane. Sections~\ref{sec:longitudinal_field} and~\ref{sec:lateral_field} detail each direction separately.

\subsection{Design Philosophy: Bidirectional Forces with Hard Safety Zones}

One design choice differentiates this implementation from standard potential field methods: the longitudinal field is \textit{bidirectional}, generating a repulsive force when the ego vehicle is too close to the leader and an attractive force when it has fallen too far behind. A separate gap-closing controller is therefore unnecessary; the Nash equilibrium solver tracks a single combined reference determined entirely by the safety field.

The bidirectional force is bounded by two hard safety zones derived from a time-headway rule~\cite{wangDrivingSafetyField2016}:

\begin{align}
d_{\min}(v) &= \max\!\left(d_{\mathrm{floor}},\, h_{\min} \cdot v\right) \label{eq:min_safe} \\
d_{\mathrm{emg}}(v) &= d_{\min}(v) + \Delta_{\mathrm{emg}} \label{eq:emg_dist}
\end{align}

where $d_{\mathrm{floor}} = 5.0\,\mathrm{m}$ is a standstill floor, $h_{\min} = 1.0\,\mathrm{s}$ is the minimum time headway, and $\Delta_{\mathrm{emg}} = 1.0\,\mathrm{m}$ is the emergency offset. When the bumper-to-bumper gap falls below $d_{\min}$, the force saturates to its maximum value $F_{\max} = 800\,\mathrm{N}$. Between $d_{\min}$ and $d_{\mathrm{emg}}$, the force ramps linearly from $F_{\max}$ to the elliptic potential value, ensuring a continuous transition into the danger zone. Under these conditions the authority allocator (Section~\ref{sec:authority}) receives a saturated signal, driving $\lambda \to \lambda_{\max}$ and transferring full longitudinal control to the automated system.

\section{Bidirectional Longitudinal Safety Field}
\label{sec:longitudinal_field}

\subsection{Overview and Architecture}

The longitudinal safety field quantifies collision risk from two directions simultaneously: the leader vehicle ahead and the follower vehicle behind. During platoon merging, the ego vehicle must close a large gap to the platoon leader while avoiding a collision with a following vehicle that may be decelerating into the same space. Both constraints must be satisfied concurrently, and the bidirectional formulation addresses this directly.

The field is evaluated over the full merge trajectory and yields a signed scalar force $F_{\mathrm{total}}$ that is passed directly to the authority allocator. The sign convention is: $F > 0$ is repulsive (ego is too close — decelerate); $F < 0$ is attractive (ego has fallen too far behind — accelerate). The desired bumper-to-bumper gap follows a constant time-gap policy~\cite{rajamaniVehicleDynamicsControl2005}:

\begin{equation}
d_{\mathrm{des}}(v) = d_s + h \cdot v
\label{eq:desired_gap}
\end{equation}

where $d_s = 2.0\,\mathrm{m}$ is the standstill distance, $h = 1.5\,\mathrm{s}$ is the time gap, and $v$ is the ego vehicle speed. The gap error with respect to the leader is:

\begin{equation}
e_{\mathrm{gap}} = d_{\mathrm{des}}(v) - d_{\mathrm{actual}}
\label{eq:gap_error}
\end{equation}

so that $e_{\mathrm{gap}} > 0$ indicates the ego is too close (danger) and $e_{\mathrm{gap}} < 0$ indicates the ego has more space than required (safe, attractive regime).

\subsection{Force Computation Model}
\label{sec:long_force}

The leader force is computed through four regimes, ordered by proximity severity.

\subsubsection{Hard Safety Zones}

When the gap falls below the velocity-dependent minimum safe distance $d_{\min}(v)$ defined in Equation~\ref{eq:min_safe}, the force is saturated immediately:

\begin{equation}
F_{\mathrm{leader}} = \begin{cases}
F_{\max} & d < d_{\min}(v) \\[4pt]
\left(1 - \dfrac{d - d_{\min}(v)}{d_{\mathrm{emg}}(v) - d_{\min}(v)}\right) F_{\max} & d_{\min}(v) \leq d < d_{\mathrm{emg}}(v)
\end{cases}
\label{eq:hard_zones}
\end{equation}

where $F_{\max} = 800\,\mathrm{N}$. The linear ramp between $d_{\min}$ and $d_{\mathrm{emg}}$ keeps the transition into full saturation continuous. The EMA filter that normally smooths the force output is bypassed in the emergency zone, so that filter lag cannot attenuate the signal on the first timestep of a dangerous approach.

\subsubsection{Elliptic Potential (Li et al.)}

When the gap exceeds $d_{\mathrm{emg}}$ and the gap error is positive ($e_{\mathrm{gap}} > 0$, ego too close relative to desired), the force is computed from the elliptic \textit{driving safety field} potential~\cite{liSharedControlNovel2019}:

\begin{equation}
F_{\mathrm{elliptic}} = \frac{M_o \cdot R_i}{\left(r_e + \varepsilon\right)^2} \cdot w_d \cdot w_v \cdot \left(1 + DR\right)
\label{eq:elliptic_force}
\end{equation}

where the elliptic ratio is:
\begin{equation}
r_e = \frac{e_{\mathrm{gap}}}{s + \varepsilon}
\label{eq:elliptic_ratio}
\end{equation}

with $\varepsilon = 0.1\,\mathrm{m}$ preventing singularities when $e_{\mathrm{gap}} \to 0$. The distance decay weight is:
\begin{equation}
w_d = \exp\!\left(-\frac{e_{\mathrm{gap}}}{\tau_d}\right), \quad \tau_d = 15\,\mathrm{m}
\label{eq:dist_decay}
\end{equation}

and the closing-velocity weight is:
\begin{equation}
w_v = \exp\!\left(\frac{\max(0,\, v_{\mathrm{rel}})}{5.0}\right), \quad v_{\mathrm{rel}} = v_{\mathrm{ego}} - v_{\mathrm{leader}}
\label{eq:vel_weight}
\end{equation}

so that the force is amplified when the ego approaches the leader ($v_{\mathrm{rel}} > 0$) and remains unchanged when the gap is growing.

\subsubsection{TTC Floor}

A time-to-collision (TTC) floor ensures the elliptic potential does not underestimate risk at mid-range gaps where $r_e$ is small but the closing speed is high. When $v_{\mathrm{rel}} > 0.1\,\mathrm{m/s}$:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{TTC} = \frac{d}{v_{\mathrm{rel}}}
\label{eq:ttc}
\end{equation}

The TTC floor force ramps linearly from zero at $\mathrm{TTC} = T_{\mathrm{warn}} = 5.5\,\mathrm{s}$ to $F_{\max}$ at $\mathrm{TTC} = T_{\mathrm{crit}} = 3.0\,\mathrm{s}$:

\begin{equation}
F_{\mathrm{TTC}} = \left(1 - \frac{\mathrm{TTC} - T_{\mathrm{crit}}}{T_{\mathrm{warn}} - T_{\mathrm{crit}}}\right)_{\!+} F_{\max}
\label{eq:ttc_floor}
\end{equation}

where $(\cdot)_+$ denotes clamping to $[0, 1]$. The final repulsive force takes the larger of the two signals, capped at $F_{\max}$:

\begin{equation}
F_{\mathrm{leader}}^{+} = \min\!\left(\max\!\left(F_{\mathrm{elliptic}},\, F_{\mathrm{TTC}}\right),\, F_{\max}\right)
\label{eq:leader_repulsive}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Attractive Regime}

When $e_{\mathrm{gap}} \leq 0$ (gap exceeds the desired value), a linear attractive force pulls the ego vehicle forward:

\begin{equation}
F_{\mathrm{leader}}^{-} = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\frac{e_{\mathrm{gap}}}{d_{\mathrm{des}}} \cdot F_{\max},\; -0.25\,F_{\max},\; 0\right)
\label{eq:attractive_force}
\end{equation}

The cap at $-0.25\,F_{\max} = -200\,\mathrm{N}$ prevents excessive acceleration when the ego is far behind the platoon; in that regime, the Nash reference generator serves as the primary catch-up mechanism.


\subsection{Dynamic Parameter Computation}

All safety field parameters are adapted to the current driving context via low-pass-filtered updates.

\subsubsection{Dynamic Safety Radius}

The safety radius expands or contracts based on vehicle position in the platoon and current velocity:

\begin{equation}
s = s_{\text{base}} \cdot m_{\text{pos}} \cdot \left(1 + \alpha_v \cdot \frac{v_{\text{ego}}}{v_{\text{ref}}} - \alpha_v\right)
\label{eq:dynamic_radius_impl}
\end{equation}

where $s_{\text{base}} = 10$ m is the base safety radius, $\alpha_v = 0.8$ is the velocity scaling factor, and $v_{\text{ref}} = 120$ km/h ($\approx 33.3$ m/s) is the reference velocity corresponding to the platoon cruise speed. The position multiplier $m_{\text{pos}}$ takes values of 0.8 when the ego vehicle is at the front of the platoon in the leader position, 1.2 when the ego is in the middle of the platoon, and 1.0 when the ego is at the back in the follower position:

\begin{equation}
m_{\text{pos}} = \begin{cases}
0.8 & \text{ego at front of platoon (leader position)} \\
1.2 & \text{ego in middle of platoon} \\
1.0 & \text{ego at back of platoon (follower position)}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:position_multiplier}
\end{equation}

The velocity scaling ensures that higher speeds yield larger safety zones, consistent with the intuition that faster vehicles require greater reaction distance.

\subsubsection{Dynamic Obstacle Mass}

The virtual obstacle mass follows a similar pattern:
\begin{equation}
M_o = M_{\text{base}} \cdot m_{\text{pos}}
\label{eq:dynamic_mass}
\end{equation}

where $M_{\text{base}} = 400$ kg equivalent.

\subsubsection{Low-Pass Filtering}

To prevent abrupt parameter changes that could cause control discontinuities, all dynamic parameters are filtered:

\begin{equation}
\theta_{\text{filtered}}[k] = \alpha_f \cdot \theta_{\text{target}}[k] + (1 - \alpha_f) \cdot \theta_{\text{filtered}}[k-1]
\label{eq:lowpass_filter}
\end{equation}

where $\alpha_f = 0.2$ is the filter coefficient, providing smooth transitions while remaining responsive to changing conditions.

\subsection{Bidirectional Force Combination}

The total longitudinal safety force combines leader and follower contributions:

\begin{equation}
F_{\text{total}} = F_{\text{leader}} + w_f \cdot F_{\text{follower}}
\label{eq:bidirectional_force}
\end{equation}

The follower weight $w_f$ reflects the asymmetric information flow in Cooperative Adaptive Cruise Control (CACC) systems, where information primarily flows forward from leader to follower. The ego vehicle therefore has more reliable information about the vehicle ahead than about the vehicle behind, which justifies weighting the follower contribution at $w_f = 0.5$

This forward-dominant weighting ensures string stability by preventing the ego vehicle from over-reacting to follower dynamics.

\subsection{Phase Detection System}

A key innovation in this work is the \textbf{dynamic phase detection system} that distinguishes between operating modes based on actual system state rather than fixed timers. This prevents the safety field from generating unnecessary corrective forces once the vehicle has reached steady-state following.

\subsubsection{Control Phases}

The system operates in two primary phases. The first is the \textbf{MERGING Phase}, which involves active gap closing with full safety field response, where the vehicle is actively approaching its target position in the platoon. The second is the \textbf{FOLLOWING Phase}, which represents steady-state operation with soft-transition response, where the vehicle has reached its target position and maintains formation.

\subsubsection{Phase Transition Conditions}

\textbf{Entry to FOLLOWING Phase} requires all conditions to be satisfied simultaneously for a sustained period:

\begin{align}
|e_{\text{gap}}| &< \gamma_{\text{gap}} \cdot d_{\text{desired}} \label{eq:following_gap} \\
|v_{\text{rel}}| &< \gamma_v \cdot v_{\text{target}} \label{eq:following_vel} \\
|a_{\text{ego}}| &< a_{\text{threshold}} \label{eq:following_acc}
\end{align}

where $\gamma_{\text{gap}} = 0.10$ represents 10\% of the desired gap, $\gamma_v = 0.05$ represents 5\% of the target velocity, $a_{\text{threshold}} = 0.5\,\mathrm{m/s^{2}}$, and all conditions must hold for $T_{\text{stable}} = 5$ seconds.


\textbf{Exit from FOLLOWING Phase} occurs immediately if any threshold is violated:

\begin{align}
|e_{\text{gap}}| &> \gamma_{\text{gap,exit}} \cdot d_{\text{desired}} \quad \text{OR} \label{eq:merging_gap} \\
|v_{\text{rel}}| &> \gamma_{v,\text{exit}} \cdot v_{\text{target}} \label{eq:merging_vel}
\end{align}

where $\gamma_{\text{gap,exit}} = 0.35$ provides the hysteresis gap-fraction threshold and $\gamma_{v,\text{exit}} = 0.10$ provides 10\% hysteresis on the velocity. The gap-exit condition additionally requires a hold time of $T_{\mathrm{hold}} = 3.0\,\mathrm{s}$ before triggering the return to \textsc{Merge}, which filters out transient gap fluctuations.


The hysteresis between entry and exit thresholds (10\% vs 35\% for gap, 5\% vs 10\% for velocity) prevents oscillation between phases.

\subsubsection{Soft Transition Function}

In the FOLLOWING phase, forces are scaled by a quadratic soft transition function:

\begin{equation}
F_{\text{following}} = F_{\text{raw}} \cdot \min\left(1.0, \left(\frac{|e_{\text{gap}}|}{\gamma_{\text{gap}} \cdot d_{\text{desired}}}\right)^2\right)
\label{eq:soft_transition}
\end{equation}

This creates a smooth reduction of force as the gap error decreases. At the threshold where $|e_{\text{gap}}| = \gamma_{\text{gap}} \cdot d_{\text{desired}}$, the force is at 100\%. At 50\% of the threshold, the force drops to 25\%. Near zero error, the force becomes negligible.

The quadratic relationship ensures smooth, jerk-free transitions while maintaining safety responsiveness for larger errors.

\subsection{Target Position Locking}

After the merge is triggered at time $t_{\text{merge}}$, the safety field locks onto specific leader and follower vehicles by their identifiers rather than using dynamic closest-vehicle detection. This prevents target switching during the merge maneuver:

\begin{equation}
\text{target}_{\text{leader}} = \begin{cases}
\text{closest vehicle ahead} & t < t_{\text{merge}} \\
\text{locked ID} & t \geq t_{\text{merge}}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:target_locking}
\end{equation}

This mechanism is essential for stable merging behavior, especially in scenarios where the ego vehicle may temporarily come closer to a different platoon member than its intended target.

\subsection{Force Saturation and Output}

The final force output is saturated to prevent excessive control commands:

\begin{equation}
F_{\text{output}} = \text{clip}(F_{\text{total}}, -200, 800) \text{ N}
\label{eq:force_saturation}
\end{equation}

The asymmetric limits reflect the directional asymmetry of risk: the repulsive upper bound of $800,\mathrm{N}$ commands a strong deceleration when the ego is dangerously close to the leader, while the attractive lower bound is capped at $-200,\mathrm{N}$ ($=-0.25,F_{\max}$) to prevent excessive acceleration when the vehicle is far behind the platoon.

\subsection{Implementation Algorithm}

Algorithm \ref{alg:longitudinal_field_impl} summarizes the complete longitudinal safety field computation.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Bidirectional Longitudinal Safety Field with Phase Detection}
\label{alg:longitudinal_field_impl}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Input:} Ego state $(x_e, v_e, a_e)$, Leader $(x_l, v_l)$, Follower $(x_f, v_f)$, $d_{\text{desired}}$, $t$
\State \textbf{Output:} Total force $F_{\text{total}}$

\State // Update phase based on conditions
\State $e_{\text{gap}} \gets (x_l - x_e) - d_{\text{desired}}$
\State $v_{\text{rel}} \gets v_e - v_l$
\State Call \textsc{UpdatePhase}($e_{\text{gap}}$, $v_{\text{rel}}$, $a_e$, $d_{\text{desired}}$, $v_{\text{target}}$)

\State // Compute leader force (only if gap < desired)
\If{$e_{\text{gap}} < 0$}
    \State $s \gets$ \textsc{ComputeDynamicRadius}(context)
    \State $M_o \gets$ \textsc{ComputeDynamicMass}(context)
    \State $r_e \gets |e_{\text{gap}}| / (s + \epsilon)$
    \State $w_d \gets \exp(-|e_{\text{gap}}| / \tau_d)$
    \State $w_v \gets \exp(\max(0, v_{\text{rel}}) / 5.0)$
    \State $F_{\text{leader}} \gets M_o \cdot R_i / (r_e + \epsilon)^2 \cdot w_d \cdot w_v \cdot (1 + DR_i)$
\Else
    \State $F_{\text{leader}} \gets 0$ \Comment{Safe - no force needed}
\EndIf

\State // Compute follower force with adaptive weight
\If{follower exists AND not ignoring follower}
    \State $w_f \gets$ \textsc{GetFollowerWeight}(context)
    \State $F_{\text{follower}} \gets w_f \cdot$ \textsc{ComputeForce}(follower)
\Else
    \State $F_{\text{follower}} \gets 0$
\EndIf

\State // Apply soft transition in FOLLOWING phase
\If{phase = FOLLOWING}
    \State $F_{\text{leader}} \gets$ \textsc{ApplySoftTransition}($F_{\text{leader}}$, $e_{\text{gap}}$, $d_{\text{desired}}$)
    \State $F_{\text{follower}} \gets$ \textsc{ApplySoftTransition}($F_{\text{follower}}$, $e_{\text{gap,f}}$, $d_{\text{desired}}$)
\EndIf

\State $F_{\text{total}} \gets F_{\text{leader}} + F_{\text{follower}}$
\State $F_{\text{total}} \gets \text{clip}(F_{\text{total}}, -200, 800)$
\State \Return $F_{\text{total}}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Elliptic Lateral Safety Field}
\label{sec:lateral_field}

\subsection{Design Philosophy}

The lateral safety field quantifies collision risk from neighboring platoon vehicles during a lane-change maneuver. A fundamental distinction separates this field from its longitudinal counterpart. In longitudinal control, reducing the gap to the leader is \textit{dangerous}; in lateral control, closing the gap to the target lane is \textit{desired behavior}. The lateral field therefore generates repulsive force only from genuine proximity to other vehicles, not from progress toward the target lane. This separation allows the Nash equilibrium to govern the lane-change trajectory without safety-field interference, with repulsive forces activated only when a real collision threat arises.

The formulation follows Wang and Li~\cite{wangDrivingSafetyField2015,wangDrivingSafetyField2016,liSharedControlNovel2019}, computing a continuous potential field over the two-dimensional plane. The virtual-mass and elliptic-distance framework from the longitudinal field is retained here, with parameters tuned for lateral dynamics.

\subsection{Control Phases for Lateral Motion}
\label{sec:lat_phases}

The lateral controller advances through four phases that map directly to the \texttt{NashCoordinator} state machine: \textsc{Approach}, \textsc{GapSearch}, \textsc{Merge}, and \textsc{Following}.

During \textsc{Approach}, the ego vehicle remains in its current lane while the coordinator waits for a merge command. In \textsc{GapSearch}, available gaps in the target lane are evaluated, and an insertion point is selected. During \textsc{Merge}, the active lane change is executed under Nash shared control. \textsc{Following} is the steady-state phase, entered once the vehicle has settled in the target lane.

\textbf{Entry to \textsc{Following}.} The transition from \textsc{Merge} to \textsc{Following} requires all five conditions in Equations~\ref{eq:lat_following_y}--\ref{eq:lat_conv_cond} to hold simultaneously for a continuous period $T_{\mathrm{stable}} = 5.0\,\mathrm{s}$:

\begin{align}
|y_{\mathrm{ego}} - y_{\mathrm{platoon}}| &< 0.3\,\mathrm{m} \label{eq:lat_following_y} \\
|\psi| &< 0.05\,\mathrm{rad} \label{eq:lat_following_psi} \\
\frac{|e_{\mathrm{gap}}|}{d_{\mathrm{des}}} &< \gamma_{\mathrm{gap}}^{\mathrm{lat}} = 0.10 \label{eq:lat_following_gap} \\
|v_{\mathrm{ego}} - v_{\mathrm{leader}}| &< \delta_v = 0.5\,\mathrm{m/s} \label{eq:lat_following_vel} \\
\left|\dot{e}_{\mathrm{gap}}\right| &< \dot{e}_{\mathrm{max}} = 0.3\,\mathrm{m/s} \label{eq:lat_conv_cond}
\end{align}

where Equation~\ref{eq:lat_following_y} confirms lateral alignment with the platoon lane, Equation~\ref{eq:lat_following_psi} confirms heading alignment, Equations~\ref{eq:lat_following_gap}--\ref{eq:lat_following_vel} confirm longitudinal convergence and velocity synchronization, and Equation~\ref{eq:lat_conv_cond} confirms the gap is no longer drifting.

\textbf{Exit from \textsc{Following}.} The system returns to \textsc{Merge} immediately upon a lateral exceedance:
\begin{equation}
|y_{\mathrm{ego}} - y_{\mathrm{platoon}}| > \tfrac{1}{4}\,W_{\mathrm{lane}}
\label{eq:lat_exit_y}
\end{equation}

where $y_{\mathrm{ego}}$ is the lateral position of the ego vehicle, $y_{\mathrm{platoon}}$ is the lateral center of the platoon lane, and $W_{\mathrm{lane}}$ is the lane width. A sustained heading deviation also triggers the return:
\begin{equation}
|\psi| > 0.10\,\mathrm{rad}
\label{eq:lat_exit_psi}
\end{equation}

where $\psi$ is the yaw angle of the ego vehicle relative to the road centerline.

\subsection{Virtual Mass Formulation}
\label{sec:lat_virtual_mass}

The virtual mass of each vehicle follows Wang~\cite{wangDrivingSafetyField2016} Eq.\,17 and depends on longitudinal speed:

\begin{equation}
M_i(v_i) = m_0 \bigl(c_s\,|v_i|^{c_e} + c_o\bigr)
\label{eq:lat_virtual_mass}
\end{equation}

where $m_0 = 1304\,\mathrm{kg}$ is the vehicle physical mass, $c_s = 1.566 \times 10^{-14}$ and $c_e = 6.687$ are the speed-scaling coefficients, and $c_o = 0.3345$ is the offset ensuring a non-zero mass at rest. The same formula applies to both the ego vehicle ($M_{\mathrm{ego}}$) and each platoon obstacle ($M_{\mathrm{obs}}$). At highway cruising speed the virtual mass exceeds the physical mass, amplifying the perceived inertia and tightening the repulsive field.

\subsection{Elliptic Field Strength}
\label{sec:lat_elliptic}

The two-dimensional separation between the ego vehicle and each obstacle is decomposed into a longitudinal component $d_x$ and a lateral component $d_y$:
\begin{equation}
d_x = x_{\mathrm{ego}} - x_{\mathrm{obs}}, \qquad d_y = y_{\mathrm{ego}} - y_{\mathrm{obs}}
\label{eq:lat_separation}
\end{equation}

where $x_{\mathrm{ego}}$ and $y_{\mathrm{ego}}$ are the longitudinal and lateral positions of the ego vehicle, $x_{\mathrm{obs}}$ and $y_{\mathrm{obs}}$ are the corresponding positions of the obstacle, and $d_y > 0$ indicates that the ego is to the left of the obstacle. An elliptic metric weights the two components according to the velocity difference and a fixed lateral scale~\cite{liSharedControlNovel2019} (Eqs.\,12--13):

\begin{equation}
r^{*} = \sqrt{\left(\frac{d_x}{a}\right)^{\!2} + \left(\frac{d_y}{b}\right)^{\!2}}
\label{eq:lat_elliptic_dist}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
a = \max\!\bigl(|\Delta v|\,T_s,\; a_{\min}\bigr), \qquad b = \tau
\label{eq:lat_elliptic_axes}
\end{equation}

where $\Delta v = v_{\mathrm{ego}} - v_{\mathrm{obs}}$ is the relative longitudinal speed, $T_s = 2.0\,\mathrm{s}$ is the reaction-time scale, $a_{\min} = 2.0\,\mathrm{m}$ prevents the longitudinal axis from collapsing when both vehicles travel at the same speed, and $b = \tau = 2.0\,\mathrm{m}$ is the fixed lateral axis. At speed differentials below $1.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$, both axes equal $2.0\,\mathrm{m}$, making the field locally isotropic.

The scalar field strength~\cite{liSharedControlNovel2019} (Eq.\,11) is:

\begin{equation}
E = G \cdot R_{\mathrm{obs}} \cdot \frac{M_{\mathrm{obs}}}{r^{*}}
\label{eq:lat_field_strength}
\end{equation}

where $G = 6 \times 10^{-3}$ is the gravitational-analogue constant and $R_{\mathrm{obs}}$ is the road-condition factor for the obstacle vehicle.

\subsection{Kinetic Corrections}
\label{sec:lat_kinetic}

Two kinetic corrections account for the velocities of the obstacle and the ego vehicle relative to the field gradient direction~\cite{liSharedControlNovel2019}.

\subsubsection{Obstacle Kinetic Correction (Li Eq.~18)}

The obstacle's longitudinal velocity is projected onto the elliptic gradient unit vector $\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{r^*}$. For a vehicle moving in the positive-$x$ direction, the unit velocity vector is $[1, 0]^{\top}$, so the projection reduces to the $x$-component of $\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{r^*}$:

\begin{equation}
\cos\theta_v = \hat{u}_{r^*,x} = \frac{d_x / a}{r^{*}}
\label{eq:lat_cos_theta_v}
\end{equation}

where $\hat{u}_{r^*,x}$ is the $x$-component of the normalized gradient vector and $\theta_v$ is the angle between the obstacle velocity and the gradient direction.

\begin{equation}
E \;\leftarrow\; E \cdot \exp\!\bigl(K_1\,|v_{\mathrm{obs}}|\,\cos\theta_v\bigr), \qquad K_1 = 0.005
\label{eq:lat_obs_kinetic}
\end{equation}

where $K_1$ is the obstacle kinetic coefficient. This update amplifies the field when the obstacle moves toward the ego along the gradient and attenuates it when the obstacle moves away.

\subsubsection{Ego Kinetic Correction (Li Eq.~21)}

The ego vehicle's velocity is projected onto the unnormalized elliptic gradient $\nabla(r^{*2})$ to form $\cos\theta_i$:

\begin{equation}
\cos\theta_i = \frac{d_x / a^2}{\left\|(d_x / a^2,\; d_y / b^2)\right\|}
\label{eq:lat_cos_theta_i}
\end{equation}

where $\theta_i$ is the angle between the ego velocity and the elliptic gradient, and the denominator is the Euclidean norm of the unnormalized gradient vector. The repulsive force magnitude is then:

\begin{equation}
F_r = E \cdot M_{\mathrm{ego}} \cdot R_{\mathrm{ego}} \cdot \exp\!\bigl(-K_2\,|v_{\mathrm{ego}}|\,\cos\theta_i\bigr) \cdot (1 + DR)
\label{eq:lat_force_mag}
\end{equation}

where $K_2 = 0.005$ is the ego kinetic coefficient, $R_{\mathrm{ego}}$ is the road-condition factor for the ego vehicle, and $DR = 0.5$ is the dynamic risk addend that sustains a non-zero floor even when kinetic corrections are near unity.

\subsection{Lateral Direction Convention}
\label{sec:lat_direction}

Equation~\ref{eq:lat_force_mag} gives only the magnitude of the repulsive force. The lateral direction is assigned through a smooth hyperbolic tangent of the lateral separation:

\begin{equation}
F_{\mathrm{lat}} = F_r \cdot \tanh\!\left(\frac{d_y}{\sigma}\right), \qquad \sigma = 0.5\,\mathrm{m}
\label{eq:lat_direction}
\end{equation}

where $\sigma$ is the lateral sharpness parameter governing the transition width around $d_y = 0$. When $d_y = 0$ (ego directly ahead of or behind the obstacle), the force is exactly zero: no lateral bias is introduced. When $|d_y| \gg \sigma$, the $\tanh$ saturates to $\pm 1$ and the full magnitude $F_r$ is applied. This continuously differentiable sign function avoids the discontinuity that a hard $\mathrm{sign}(\cdot)$ would introduce at $d_y = 0$.

\subsection{Road Boundary Forces}
\label{sec:lat_boundaries}

Lane-boundary forces prevent the ego vehicle from leaving the road. These act alongside the vehicle-to-vehicle repulsion described above. The lateral position $y_{\mathrm{ego}}$ defines distances to the right and left edges:

\begin{equation}
d_{\mathrm{right}} = W_{\mathrm{road}}/2 - y_{\mathrm{ego}}, \qquad d_{\mathrm{left}} = W_{\mathrm{road}}/2 + y_{\mathrm{ego}}
\label{eq:lat_boundary_dists}
\end{equation}

where $W_{\mathrm{road}}$ is the total width of the road and $y_{\mathrm{ego}}$ is measured from the center line of the road. A repulsive potential is activated when either distance falls below the proximity threshold $d_{\mathrm{prox}} = 3.0\,\mathrm{m}$:

\begin{equation}
F_{\mathrm{boundary},k} = \pm\, K_b \cdot \tanh\!\left(\frac{1}{\max(d_k,\,\varepsilon_b)\,K_{b,s}}\right)
\label{eq:lat_boundary_force}
\end{equation}

where $K_b = 150\,\mathrm{N}$ is the boundary gain, $K_{b,s} = 2.0$ is the saturation scale, $\varepsilon_b = 0.1\,\mathrm{m}$ is a lower-bound clamp on $d_k$ that prevents division by zero as the vehicle approaches the road edge, and $k \in \{\mathrm{right}, \mathrm{left}\}$ indexes the two boundaries. The right boundary contributes a negative (leftward) force and the left boundary a positive (rightward) force. The $\tanh$ saturation prevents the force from diverging as $d_k \to 0$.

\subsection{Force Saturation and Output}
\label{sec:lat_output}

The total lateral force sums over all platoon vehicles and both boundary contributions:

\begin{equation}
F_{\mathrm{total}} = \sum_{j} F_{\mathrm{lat},j} + F_{\mathrm{boundary,right}} + F_{\mathrm{boundary,left}}
\label{eq:lat_total}
\end{equation}

where the index $j$ runs over all platoon vehicles in the scene. The output is saturated to a symmetric bound and filtered through a first-order exponential moving average (EMA):

\begin{equation}
F_{\mathrm{clip}} = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(F_{\mathrm{total}},\; -F_{\mathrm{lat,max}},\; F_{\mathrm{lat,max}}\right), \qquad F_{\mathrm{lat,max}} = 2000\,\mathrm{N}
\label{eq:lat_saturation}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
F_{\mathrm{out}}[k] = \alpha_f \cdot F_{\mathrm{clip}}[k] + (1 - \alpha_f)\cdot F_{\mathrm{out}}[k-1], \qquad \alpha_f = 0.3
\label{eq:lat_ema}
\end{equation}

where $\alpha_f$ is the smoothing coefficient of the EMA and $k$ is the discrete time index. The saturation bound of $\pm 2000\,\mathrm{N}$ is four times the longitudinal bound, reflecting the greater lateral inertia during a lane change. The coefficient $\alpha_f = 0.3$ is higher than the longitudinal value of $0.2$, yielding a faster response to sudden lateral threats while still attenuating high-frequency noise.

\section{Dynamic Authority Allocation}
\label{sec:authority_allocation}

The authority ratio $\lambda[k]$ governs the balance between system control and human input at every Nash timestep. A high $\lambda$ transfers authority to the autonomous system, while a $\lambda$ close to zero returns full control to the driver. Both the longitudinal and lateral allocators follow a three-stage architecture: a sigmoid mapping from the safety-field force, a performance-based sigmoid following Swain and Rath~\cite{swainCooperativeGameTheory2023} Eq.\,15, and fusion via element-wise maximum and adaptive exponential moving average (EMA) smoothing. The two channels share the same sigmoid kernel but differ in their normalization inputs and output bounds. The formal conversion of $\lambda[k]$ to the scaling factor $\alpha[k] \in (0, 1]$ that enters the Nash cost is developed in Section~\ref{sec:authority_conversion}.


\subsection{Shared Sigmoid Kernel}
\label{sec:authority_sigmoid}

The safety-field sigmoid maps a scalar risk magnitude $|F|$ to an authority ratio:

\begin{equation}
\lambda_{\mathrm{safety}} = \lambda_{\min} + \frac{\lambda_{\max} - \lambda_{\min}}{1 + \exp\!\bigl(-k_s\,(|F| - F_{\mathrm{mid}})\bigr)}
\label{eq:safety_sigmoid}
\end{equation}

where $\lambda_{\min}$ and $\lambda_{\max}$ bound the output range, $F_{\mathrm{mid}}$ is the force midpoint at which $\lambda = (\lambda_{\min} + \lambda_{\max})/2$, and $k_s$ controls the transition steepness. The sigmoid is monotonically increasing in $|F|$: low risk force produces near-$\lambda_{\min}$ authority; force at or above $F_{\mathrm{mid}}$ drives $\lambda$ toward $\lambda_{\max}$.

The performance-based sigmoid follows Swain and Rath~\cite{swainCooperativeGameTheory2023} Eq.\,15. A normalized performance index $\ell_n \in [0, 1]$ is defined from the tracking errors, and the authority ratio is derived through a sigmoid:

\begin{equation}
\gamma_1 = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\!\bigl(M_1(-\ell_n + M_2)\bigr)}, \qquad
\lambda_{\mathrm{perf}} = \frac{1 - \gamma_1}{\gamma_1}
\label{eq:swain_rath}
\end{equation}

where $M_1 = 2.0$ is the slope parameter and $M_2 = 0.5$ is the center shift. When $\ell_n = 1$ (zero tracking error), $\gamma_1 \to 1$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{perf}} \to 0$, so the human retains authority. When $\ell_n = 0$ (maximum error), $\gamma_1 \to \sigma(-M_1 M_2) \approx 0.27$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{perf}}$ rises sharply, transferring authority to the system.

The two authority signals are fused by taking their element-wise maximum, subject to the configured ceiling:

\begin{equation}
\lambda_{\mathrm{raw}}[k] = \min\!\bigl(\max(\lambda_{\mathrm{safety}},\, \lambda_{\mathrm{perf}}),\; \lambda_{\max}\bigr)
\label{eq:lambda_fusion}
\end{equation}

A high risk force \emph{or} a large tracking error is therefore sufficient to raise system authority, but neither can force $\lambda$ above $\lambda_{\max}$.

\subsection{Longitudinal Authority Allocation}
\label{sec:long_authority}

\subsubsection{Stage 1 — Safety Sigmoid}

The longitudinal safety field force $F_{\mathrm{total}}$ computed in Section~\ref{sec:longitudinal_field} is passed through Equation~\ref{eq:safety_sigmoid} with parameters $\lambda_{\min} = 0.1$, $\lambda_{\max} = 50$, $F_{\mathrm{mid}} = 400\,\mathrm{N}$, and $k_s = 0.015\,\mathrm{N}^{-1}$.

\subsubsection{Stage 2 — Gap and Velocity Performance Sigmoid}

The normalized performance index combines the gap error $e_{\mathrm{gap}}$ and velocity error $e_v$:

\begin{equation}
\ell_n = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\min\!\left(1 - \frac{|e_{\mathrm{gap}}|}{e_{\mathrm{gap,max}}},\; 1 - \frac{|e_v|}{e_{v,\mathrm{max}}}\right),\; 0,\; 1\right)
\label{eq:ln_long}
\end{equation}

where $e_{\mathrm{gap,max}} = 5.0\,\mathrm{m}$ and $e_{v,\mathrm{max}} = 1.5\,\mathrm{m/s}$ are the normalization maximum. Taking the minimum of the two terms ensures that a large error in either channel is sufficient to raise authority. When the ego vehicle has no forward leader, the velocity normalization is widened to $e_{v,\mathrm{max}} = 15.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $\lambda$ is capped at $\lambda_{\max,\mathrm{NL}} = 2.0$, which prevents an authority spike during the initial speed-matching transient.

\subsubsection{Stage 3 — Fusion and Adaptive EMA}

The raw fused authority $\lambda_{\mathrm{raw}}[k]$ defined in Equation~\ref{eq:lambda_fusion} is smoothed through an adaptive EMA:

\begin{equation}
\lambda_{\mathrm{long}}[k] = \alpha_\lambda \cdot \lambda_{\mathrm{raw}}[k] + (1 - \alpha_\lambda)\cdot\lambda_{\mathrm{long}}[k-1]
\label{eq:long_lambda_ema}
\end{equation}

where $\alpha_\lambda \in (0,1)$ is the smoothing coefficient and $\lambda_{\mathrm{long}}[k-1]$ is the authority ratio at the preceding Nash timestep. The coefficient $\alpha_\lambda$ adapts to the combined tracking error $e_{\mathrm{comb}} = \max(|e_{\mathrm{gap}}|, |e_v|)$:

\begin{equation}
\alpha_\lambda = \begin{cases}
\alpha_{\mathrm{fast}} = 0.12 & e_{\mathrm{comb}} > 5.0\,\mathrm{m} \\[3pt]
\alpha_{\mathrm{base}} + (\alpha_{\mathrm{fast}} - \alpha_{\mathrm{base}})\,\dfrac{e_{\mathrm{comb}} - 2.0}{3.0} & 2.0 < e_{\mathrm{comb}} \leq 5.0\,\mathrm{m} \\[3pt]
\alpha_{\mathrm{base}} = 0.05 & e_{\mathrm{comb}} \leq 2.0\,\mathrm{m}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:long_alpha_adaptive}
\end{equation}

where $\alpha_{\mathrm{base}} = 0.05$ is the slow EMA coefficient and $\alpha_{\mathrm{fast}} = 0.12$ is applied when tracking error is large. During the \textsc{Following} phase, $\alpha_\lambda$ is held at $\alpha_{\mathrm{follow}} = 0.05$ and $\lambda$ is capped at $\lambda_{\max,\mathrm{follow}} = 5.0$, limiting authority in steady-state platoon following where aggressive corrections would reduce comfort.

\subsection{Lateral Authority Allocation}
\label{sec:lat_authority}

\subsubsection{Stage 1 — Safety Sigmoid}

The lateral safety field force from Section~\ref{sec:lateral_field} is passed through Equation~\ref{eq:safety_sigmoid} with $\lambda_{\min} = 0.1$, $\lambda_{\max} = 10$, $F_{\mathrm{mid}} = 200\,\mathrm{N}$, and $k_s = 0.025\,\mathrm{N}^{-1}$. The narrower range $\lambda \in [0.1, 10]$ compared with the longitudinal range $[0.1, 50]$ reflects the observation that excessive authority during a lane change produces abrupt steering corrections that reduce passenger comfort.

\subsubsection{Stage 2 — Lane-Offset Performance Sigmoid}

The normalized performance index for the lateral channel is derived from the lateral position error $e_y = y_{\mathrm{ego}} - y_{\mathrm{target}}$ relative to the half lane width:

\begin{equation}
\ell_n = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\frac{W_{\mathrm{lane}}/2 - |e_y|}{W_{\mathrm{lane}}/2},\; 0,\; 1\right)
\label{eq:ln_lat}
\end{equation}

where $W_{\mathrm{lane}} = 3.5\,\mathrm{m}$ is the nominal lane width and $e_y$ is the signed lateral deviation from the target lane center. When the ego vehicle is at the lane center ($e_y = 0$), $\ell_n = 1$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{perf}} \to 0$; when $|e_y| \geq W_{\mathrm{lane}}/2$, $\ell_n = 0$ and authority rises to the sigmoid maximum. The same Swain--Rath kernel (Equation~\ref{eq:swain_rath}, $M_1 = 2.0$, $M_2 = 0.5$) is applied.

\subsubsection{Stage 3 — Fusion and Adaptive EMA}

The lateral EMA adapts to the absolute lateral error $|e_y|$:

\begin{equation}
\alpha_\lambda = \begin{cases}
\alpha_{\mathrm{fast}} = 0.08 & |e_y| > 1.5\,\mathrm{m} \\[3pt]
\alpha_{\mathrm{base}} + (\alpha_{\mathrm{fast}} - \alpha_{\mathrm{base}})\,\dfrac{|e_y| - 0.5}{1.0} & 0.5 < |e_y| \leq 1.5\,\mathrm{m} \\[3pt]
\alpha_{\mathrm{base}} = 0.02 & |e_y| \leq 0.5\,\mathrm{m}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:lat_alpha_adaptive}
\end{equation}

where $\alpha_{\mathrm{base}} = 0.02$ is the slow lateral coefficient and $\alpha_{\mathrm{fast}} = 0.08$ applies when the lateral deviation exceeds $1.5\,\mathrm{m}$. The slow base coefficient makes the lateral authority change gradually near the lane center, preventing steering oscillation around the target line during steady-state lane keeping.

\subsection{Conversion to Shared Control}
\label{sec:authority_conversion}

The authority ratio $\lambda[k]$ from either channel is converted to a scalar weighting $\alpha[k] \in (0, 1]$ following the mapping of Pustilnik and Borrelli~\cite{pustilnikGeneralizedNashEquilibrium2025}:

\begin{equation}
\alpha[k] = \frac{1}{1 + \lambda[k]}
\label{eq:alpha_from_lambda}
\end{equation}

where $\alpha[k]$ is the weight applied to the human's tracking cost $Q_2$ inside the Nash solver: $\lambda \to 0$ (no system authority) gives $\alpha \to 1$ and the human cost is preserved at its nominal value; $\lambda \to \infty$ (full system authority) gives $\alpha \to 0$ and the human's tracking term vanishes, so the human's optimal action collapses onto the system reference. The Nash shared-control input is then:

\begin{equation}
u_{\mathrm{shared}}[k] = u_1^{*}[k] + u_2^{*}[k]
\label{eq:shared_control}
\end{equation}

where $u_1^{*}$ and $u_2^{*}$ are the Nash equilibrium actions of the autonomous system (Player~1) and the human driver (Player~2), respectively. The authority weighting is embedded in the Nash cost matrices rather than applied to the outputs: the human's tracking weight is scaled by $\alpha[k]$, so under high risk ($\lambda \gg 1 \Rightarrow \alpha \to 0$) the human incurs almost no penalty for deviating from the system reference. The human's optimal response therefore aligns with $u_1^{*}$ without the system overriding the control channel directly.

\section{Integration with Nash Equilibrium Control}
\label{sec:nash_integration}

The safety field and authority allocator described in the preceding sections (\ref{sec:longitudinal_field}, \ref{sec:lateral_field}, and \ref{sec:authority_allocation}) operate as an outer supervisory layer around the inner Nash equilibrium controller. This section defines the coupling between the two layers. The coupling comprises four elements: how the authority ratio $\lambda[k]$ enters the Nash cost matrices, how the two players' control inputs are combined, how the online driver model is updated, and how the multi-rate schedule maintains loop stability in real time.

\label{sec:nash_scheduling}

Three loops execute at different rates within a single ego vehicle, as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:multi_rate}.

The Nash loops run at lower rates than the physics loop because each solve involves an iterative best-response procedure over a prediction horizon of $N_p = 20$ steps, which is too expensive to run every 20\,ms. Between Nash ticks, the vehicle receives the last commanded shared control input, and the physics loop applies it unchanged.

Both Nash loops remain inactive during the \textsc{Approach} phase (before the driver requests a merge), while the vehicle is under full manual control. When the driver sends a join request, the coordinator enables Nash and transitions the phase state to \textsc{GapSearch}, at which point both loops activate simultaneously. They remain active through \textsc{Merge} and \textsc{Following}. To ensure a smooth handover at the \textsc{Approach} to \textsc{GapSearch} transition, both solvers are continuously warm-started during \textsc{Approach} from the driver's current control inputs and the measured vehicle state; the warm-start mechanism is described in detail in Section~\ref{sec:nash_warm}.

\subsection{Authority Injection into Nash Cost Matrices}
\label{sec:nash_alpha}

The Nash equilibrium formulation optimizes two quadratic cost functions defined as integrals of the instantaneous stage cost over the finite prediction horizon $[t, t + T_p]$:

\begin{align}
J_1(u_1, u_2) &= \int_{t}^{t+T_p} \Bigl( \bigl\| y(\tau) - r_1(\tau) \bigr\|_{Q_1}^{2} + \bigl\| u_1(\tau) \bigr\|_{R_1}^{2} \Bigr) \, \mathrm{d}\tau \label{eq:nash_j1} \\
J_2(u_1, u_2) &= \int_{t}^{t+T_p} \Bigl( \alpha \cdot \bigl\| y(\tau) - r_2(\tau) \bigr\|_{Q_2}^{2} + \bigl\| u_2(\tau) \bigr\|_{R_2}^{2} \Bigr) \, \mathrm{d}\tau \label{eq:nash_j2}
\end{align}

where $\tau$ is the prediction time variable, $t$ is the current time, $T_p = N_p \cdot T_{\mathrm{Nash}}$ is the prediction horizon length, $y(\tau)$ is the predicted output trajectory under the joint control $u(\tau) = u_1(\tau) + u_2(\tau)$, $r_1(\tau)$ and $r_2(\tau)$ are the system and human reference trajectories, $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ are the tracking-weight matrices for the two players, and $R_1$, $R_2$ are the effort penalties on each player's individual input. For numerical solution the horizon is discretized into $N_p = 20$ steps of duration $T_{\mathrm{Nash}}$, the linearized vehicle model is rolled out from the current state $\mathbf{x}_0$ over the horizon, and the resulting predicted output takes the compact stacked form $Y = H\,U + F\,\mathbf{x}_0$, where $H$ is the input-to-output prediction matrix, $F$ is the free-response matrix, and $U$ is the stacked joint control sequence. Under this discretization, Equations~\ref{eq:nash_j1}--\ref{eq:nash_j2} reduce to quadratic programs in the stacked $u_1$ and $u_2$ sequences respectively.

The scalar $\alpha = 1/(1+\lambda)$ scales the human's tracking weight. At $\lambda = 0.1$ (safe conditions), $\alpha \approx 0.91$ and the human's reference dominates. At $\lambda = 50$ (emergency), $\alpha \approx 0.01$ and the human's tracking penalty nearly vanishes, so the human's optimal input aligns with the system reference $r_1$.

Rather than rebuilding the quadratic programming (QP) problem structure at every Nash tick, the solver pre-computes a set of problem instances at initialization, one for each level of a discrete $\lambda$ grid. The longitudinal channel uses $L_\lambda = 11$ levels and the lateral channel uses $7$ levels, both log-spaced over $[0.1, 50]$ and $[0.1, 10]$ respectively. At runtime the requested $\lambda$ is snapped to the nearest grid point in log-space, and the corresponding pre-computed problem instance is solved. This removes the most expensive numerical step of the QP setup from the real-time control loop and keeps the per-tick computational cost bounded.

\subsection{Adaptive Human Weight $R_2$}
\label{sec:nash_r2}

In addition to the $\alpha$ scaling of $Q_2$ developed in Section~\ref{sec:nash_alpha}, the human's effort penalty $R_2$ is adapted online to reflect how synchronized the ego vehicle is with the target platoon state. The adaptation is driven by a normalized synchronization index $\ell_n \in [0, 1]$, defined so that $\ell_n = 0$ indicates that the ego is far from the target and $\ell_n = 1$ indicates full synchronization. The specific definition of $\ell_n$ differs between the two channels because the tracking objectives differ: the longitudinal channel measures synchronization against a two-dimensional target (gap and velocity), while the lateral channel measures it against a one-dimensional target (lateral offset) whose weighting further depends on the current phase.

\subsubsection{Longitudinal Synchronization}

The longitudinal synchronization index combines the instantaneous gap and velocity errors relative to the platoon target state:
\begin{equation}
\ell_n^{\text{long}}[k] = \min\!\left( 1 - \frac{|e_{\text{gap}}[k]|}{e_{\text{gap},\max}}, \; 1 - \frac{|e_v[k]|}{e_{v,\max}} \right)
\label{eq:ln_long_sync}
\end{equation}
where $e_{\text{gap}}[k]$ and $e_v[k]$ are the instantaneous gap and velocity errors relative to the platoon target, $e_{\text{gap},\max} = 10\,\mathrm{m}$ and $e_{v,\max} = 1.5\,\mathrm{m/s}$ are their normalization bounds, and $\ell_n^{\text{long}}[k]$ is clipped to $[0, 1]$. The minimum of the two normalized terms ensures that a large error in either component is sufficient to drive $\ell_n$ toward zero.

\subsubsection{Lateral Synchronization: Phase-Dependent Definition}

The lateral synchronization index has two distinct forms depending on the current phase of the merge state machine (Section~\ref{sec:mobil_decision}). During the \textsc{Merge} phase, the index measures how close the ego lateral position $y_{\text{ego}}$ is to the target lane center $y_{\text{target}}$:
\begin{equation}
\ell_n^{\text{lat, merge}}[k] = \mathrm{clip}\!\left( \frac{W_{\text{lane}}/2 - |e_y[k]|}{W_{\text{lane}}/2}, \; 0, \; 1 \right)
\label{eq:ln_lat_merge}
\end{equation}
where $e_y[k] = y_{\text{ego}}[k] - y_{\text{target}}[k]$ is the signed lateral offset, $W_{\text{lane}} = 3.5\,\mathrm{m}$ is the nominal lane width, and $\ell_n^{\text{lat, merge}}[k]$ ramps linearly from $0$ at the half-lane boundary to $1$ at the target lane center. This form matches the Merge-phase objective of reducing the lateral offset.

During the \textsc{Following} phase, the ego is already inside the target lane and the objective shifts to suppressing small residual offsets that would otherwise grow into a limit cycle. The synchronization index is replaced by a lane-boundary encroachment fraction $\beta_y[k]$ computed against a narrow dead-band around the lane center:
\begin{equation}
\beta_y[k] = \mathrm{clip}\!\left( \frac{|e_y[k]| - d_{\text{dead}}}{W_{\text{lane}}/2 - d_{\text{dead}}}, \; 0, \; 1 \right)
\label{eq:beta_y_following}
\end{equation}
where $d_{\text{dead}} = 0.03\,\mathrm{m}$ is the dead-band radius and $\beta_y[k] = 0$ within the dead-band. The interpolation direction of the target $R_2$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:r2_target}) is inverted for this phase: the effort penalty rises from a small in-lane value $R_{2,\text{free}}^{\text{lat}} = 250\,000$ (giving the human authority when centered) toward a large boundary value that returns authority to the system as the ego approaches the half-lane width.

\subsubsection{Target Effort Penalty}

For both channels, the target effort penalty interpolates linearly between two channel-specific endpoints:
\begin{equation}
R_{2,\text{target}}[k] = R_{2,\text{start}} + \left( R_{2,\text{follow}} - R_{2,\text{start}} \right) \ell_n[k]
\label{eq:r2_target}
\end{equation}
where $R_{2,\text{start}}$ is the large restraining value applied when the ego is far from the target ($\ell_n \to 0$), $R_{2,\text{follow}}$ is the small in-platoon value applied at full synchronization ($\ell_n \to 1$), and $\ell_n[k]$ is $\ell_n^{\text{long}}$ for the longitudinal channel and $\ell_n^{\text{lat, merge}}$ for the lateral channel during \textsc{Merge}. The channel-specific values are $(R_{2,\text{start}}^{\text{long}}, R_{2,\text{follow}}^{\text{long}}) = (120\,000, 40\,000)$ and $(R_{2,\text{start}}^{\text{lat}}, R_{2,\text{follow}}^{\text{lat}}) = (250\,000, 30\,000)$. In the \textsc{Following} phase of the lateral channel, the endpoints of Eq.~\eqref{eq:r2_target} are swapped to $R_{2,\text{start}} = R_{2,\text{free}}^{\text{lat}} = 250\,000$ and $R_{2,\text{follow}} = R_{2,\text{follow}}^{\text{lat}} = 30\,000$, and the driving quantity is $\beta_y[k]$ rather than $\ell_n$.

The current $R_2$ tracks $R_{2,\text{target}}$ through an exponential moving average (EMA) with coefficient $\alpha_{R_2} = 0.05$ shared by both channels. A dead-band update threshold prevents the sparse QP $P$ matrix from being rebuilt when the change in $R_2$ is below $\Delta R_{2,\min}$, with channel-specific values $\Delta R_{2,\min}^{\text{long}} = 50$ and $\Delta R_{2,\min}^{\text{lat}} = 200$; this reduces per-cycle allocation cost during quasi-stationary operation without altering the equilibrium.

\subsubsection{Activity-Based $R_2$ Scaling}

Following Lazcano et al.~\cite{lazcanoMPCBasedHapticShared2021}, an additional scaling reduces $R_2$ when the driver applies non-trivial pedal or steering input:
\begin{equation}
R_{2,\text{eff}}[k] = R_2[k] \cdot \left( 1 - \mathrm{clip}\!\left( \frac{a_d[k]}{0.3}, \, 0, \, 1 \right) \left( 1 - s_{\min} \right) \right)
\label{eq:r2_activity}
\end{equation}
where $a_d[k] \in [0, 1]$ is the driver activity, defined as the maximum of the normalized throttle, brake, or absolute steering input at cycle $k$; $R_2[k]$ is the value tracked by the synchronization EMA of Eq.~\eqref{eq:r2_target}; $R_{2,\text{eff}}[k]$ is the effective effort penalty passed to the solver; and $s_{\min} = 0.25$ is the minimum residual scaling factor. The driver is thereby given perceptible authority during intentional inputs: pressing the pedal amplifies the human contribution $u_2$ even when the safety-based $\lambda$ would otherwise suppress it.

\subsubsection{Conflict-Based $R_2$ Boost (Lateral Only)}

During the \textsc{Merge} phase, a moving-average filter over the last five Nash ticks detects opposing steering intents between the system ($u_1$) and the human ($u_2$):
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{conflict}[k] = \begin{cases}
1 & \mathrm{sign}(u_1) \neq \mathrm{sign}(u_2) \text{ and both above threshold} \\
0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:conflict_indicator}
\end{equation}
where the threshold on each input is $10^{-4}\,\mathrm{rad}$ and $\mathrm{conflict}[k]$ is the binary indicator of an opposing-intent event at tick $k$. When the moving average $\overline{\mathrm{conflict}}$ exceeds a $0.2$ dead-band, the lateral $R_2$ is boosted according to
\begin{equation}
R_2^{\text{lat}} \leftarrow R_2^{\text{lat}} \cdot \left( 1 + b_c \right), \qquad b_c = B_{\max} \cdot \mathrm{clip}\!\left( \frac{\overline{\mathrm{conflict}} - 0.2}{0.6}, \, 0, \, 1 \right)
\label{eq:conflict_boost}
\end{equation}
where $B_{\max} = 0.5$ is the maximum boost, $\overline{\mathrm{conflict}}$ is the moving-average conflict rate, and $b_c \in [0, B_{\max}]$ is the resulting scaling factor. The boost is zero below the 0.2 dead-band, ramps linearly through the interval $[0.2, 0.8]$, and saturates at $B_{\max}$ above 0.8. The moving-average filter ensures that isolated one-tick disagreements between the two players (for example, transient sensor noise) do not trigger a boost; only a sustained tug-of-war lasting at least one full tick window raises $R_2^{\text{lat}}$, which reduces the magnitude of $u_2^{*}$ in subsequent solves and damps prolonged steering oscillation.

The boost is applied only during the \textsc{Merge} phase. In \textsc{Following}, the adaptive $Q_1$ mechanism described in Section~\ref{sec:nash_q1} already suppresses residual lateral offsets, and applying both mechanisms concurrently would double-count the correction.


\subsection{Adaptive Cost Matrix $Q_1$ in Following Phase}
\label{sec:nash_q1}

In the \textsc{Following} phase, small lateral offsets from the platoon lane center can grow into a limit cycle if the tracking weight remains constant. To prevent this, $Q_1$ (the system tracking weight for the lateral position and heading states) is adapted based on the fraction of the lane the ego occupies:

\begin{equation}
\beta_y = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\frac{|e_y| - d_{\mathrm{dead}}}{W_{\mathrm{lane}}/2 - d_{\mathrm{dead}}},\, 0,\, 1\right)
\label{eq:beyond_frac}
\end{equation}

where $e_y$ is the lateral offset from the lane center, $W_{\mathrm{lane}}$ is the lane width, $d_{\mathrm{dead}} = 0.03\,\mathrm{m}$ is a narrow dead-band around the lane center, and $\beta_y \in [0,1]$ is the resulting encroachment fraction. The lateral position weight $Q_y$, heading weight $Q_\psi$, and their terminal counterparts $Q_{y,\mathrm{T}}$, $Q_{\psi,\mathrm{T}}$ are linearly interpolated between their nominal and beyond-boundary values:

\begin{equation}
Q_y[k] = Q_{y,\mathrm{nom}} + \left(Q_{y,\mathrm{boundary}} - Q_{y,\mathrm{nom}}\right) \beta_y
\label{eq:qy_adapt}
\end{equation}

where $Q_{y,\mathrm{nom}}$ is the nominal in-lane weight and $Q_{y,\mathrm{boundary}}$ is the elevated weight applied at the lane boundary. Both players share the same $Q_1$, so both incur a high cost when $|e_y|$ approaches the half-lane width. The system input $u_1$ therefore grows to correct the offset, and the human's Nash response aligns with $u_1$ under the increased shared penalty.

\subsection{Online Driver Model Calibration}
\label{sec:nash_driver_calib}

The human reference trajectory $r_2$ used in Equation~\ref{eq:nash_j2} is generated by an Intelligent Driver Model (IDM) whose parameters are calibrated online during the run. Following Zhang and Sun~\cite{zhangBayesianCalibrationIntelligent2024}, the desired time gap $T$ is estimated by inverting the IDM equilibrium condition during the \textsc{GapSearch} and \textsc{Merge} phases (valid when $|\Delta v| < 2\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $v_x < v_0$), and by direct measurement $T = (\mathrm{gap} - s_0)/v_x$ during \textsc{Following}, where $\Delta v$ is the relative velocity to the leader, $v_x$ is the ego longitudinal speed, $v_0$ is the IDM desired free-flow speed, and $s_0$ is the IDM jam-distance parameter. This estimate flows into the reference generator so that the human reference reflects the driver's actual preference for gap size, rather than a fixed nominal value.

Because the calibration consumes only measured vehicle state (gap, relative velocity, longitudinal speed), and does not read the Nash output, no circular dependency arises between the driver model and the shared controller.

\subsection{Warm-Starting and Re-Linearization}
\label{sec:nash_warm}

Two techniques keep the solver responsive to fast state transients without full re-initialization.

\textbf{Warm-starting.}  Every Nash solve is initialized from two sources: the current \emph{physical state} $\mathbf{x}_0$ and an initial \emph{control guess}. The physical state $\mathbf{x}_0$ is read from the vehicle simulator at the start of each solve rather than stored between solves: the longitudinal solver receives $\mathbf{x}_0 = [x,\, v_x]^{\top}$, and the lateral solver receives $\mathbf{x}_0 = [y,\, \dot{y},\, \psi,\, \dot{\psi}]^{\top}$. This guarantees that each solve begins from the vehicle's true pose and velocity, not from a propagated prediction that may have drifted from the physics.

The control initial guess consists of the previous-input buffer and the two best-response warm-start sequences $\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ws},1}$, $\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ws},2}$ that seed the iterative best-response loop across the full prediction horizon $N_p$. The seeding source depends on the phase:
    
    
    On the phase \textsc{Approach}, the longitudinal buffers and warm-start sequences are filled at every physics tick with the measured vehicle acceleration $a_x$; the lateral buffers and sequences are filled at every physics tick with the driver's measured steering angle $\delta$, and the internal lateral-acceleration history $a_{\mathrm{lat}}$ is refreshed from the measured $a_{\mathrm{lat}}$. Because the Nash solver does not execute during \textsc{Approach}, this seeding runs at the physics rate (50\,Hz), keeping the solver's internal state continuously aligned with the manually controlled vehicle.

    On the phases \textsc{GapSearch}, \textsc{Merge}, \textsc{Following}, the buffers and sequences are updated from the previous Nash solution. The lateral solver additionally performs a one-shot re-seeding from the driver's current steering angle on the first tick of \textsc{GapSearch}, so that the handover from manual to Nash begins from the actual steering wheel position.

The combined effect is that at the \textsc{Approach}~$\to$~\textsc{GapSearch} transition, the first Nash solve starts from the vehicle's true state $\mathbf{x}_0$ and from a control guess that matches the manual input immediately before the handover. The commanded acceleration and steering angle therefore remain continuous through the transition, and the iterative best-response loop typically converges within a small number of iterations because the warm-start already lies close to the new optimum.

\textbf{Re-linearization.} The vehicle state-space matrices $(A, B)$ used to build $H$ depend on the current longitudinal speed $v_x$. When $v_x$ drifts by more than a threshold (0.5\,m/s for the longitudinal channel, and at every tick for the lateral channel due to the Coriolis sensitivity), the matrices are recomputed and $H$ is rebuilt. This keeps the linearization error bounded across the wide speed range spanned by a merge from $20$ to $33\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

\subsection{Shared Control Output and Injection}
\label{sec:nash_output}

Both Nash solvers return the first-step optimal actions $u_1^{*}[k]$ and $u_2^{*}[k]$ of Players~1 and~2 respectively. The shared control input is their algebraic sum, but the saturation bounds and the injection mechanism differ between the two channels.

\subsubsection{Longitudinal Channel}

The longitudinal shared control is a desired acceleration, clamped to the vehicle's speed-dependent physical acceleration bounds:

\begin{equation}
u_{\mathrm{shared}}^{\mathrm{long}}[k] = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(u_1^{*}[k] + u_2^{*}[k],\; a_{\min}(v_x),\; a_{\max}(v_x)\right)
\label{eq:nash_ushared_long}
\end{equation}

where $a_{\min}(v_x)$ and $a_{\max}(v_x)$ are the speed-dependent deceleration and acceleration limits derived from the powertrain torque map and brake capacity of the ego vehicle, and $u_{\mathrm{shared}}^{\mathrm{long}}[k]$ is the clamped longitudinal acceleration command at tick $k$. The clamped acceleration is then converted to throttle and brake commands through the vehicle's inverse engine map, which inverts the steady-state force-to-acceleration relationship of the powertrain and brake system to produce a pair $(\mathrm{throttle}, \mathrm{brake}) \in [0, 1] \times [0, 1]$ with at most one non-zero component per tick. These pedal commands are written directly into the vehicle's input registers for consumption by the physics update.

\subsubsection{Lateral Channel}

The lateral shared control is a desired road-wheel angle, clamped to the mechanical steering-rack limits:

\begin{equation}
\delta_{\mathrm{shared}}[k] = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(u_1^{*}[k] + u_2^{*}[k],\; \delta_{\min},\; \delta_{\max}\right)
\label{eq:nash_ushared_lat}
\end{equation}

where $\delta_{\min}$ and $\delta_{\max}$ are the minimum and maximum admissible road-wheel angles in radians, and $\delta_{\mathrm{shared}}[k]$ is the clamped steering angle command. Because the vehicle's steering input register expects a normalized value in $[-1, 1]$ rather than an angle, the command is scaled by the maximum wheel angle before injection:

\begin{equation}
\delta_{\mathrm{norm}}[k] = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\frac{\delta_{\mathrm{shared}}[k]}{\delta_{\max}^{\mathrm{wheel}}},\; -1,\; 1\right)
\label{eq:nash_steer_norm}
\end{equation}

where $\delta_{\max}^{\mathrm{wheel}}$ is the maximum road-wheel angle (in radians) obtained from the vehicle parameter set as $\delta_{\max}^{\mathrm{wheel}} = \pi \cdot \theta_{\max}^{\mathrm{deg}} / 180$, and $\delta_{\mathrm{norm}}[k]$ is the normalized steering command. If the Nash lateral solver fails to converge on a given tick (a rare event triggered by singular linearizations at very low speed), the coordinator falls back to a proportional controller $u_1 = -0.1 \cdot e_y$, $u_2 = 0$, which keeps the vehicle centered while the solver recovers on the following tick.

\subsection{Separation of Concerns}
\label{sec:nash_separation}

The layered architecture keeps three responsibilities distinct. The \textbf{Nash controller} produces optimal cooperative actions given the current cost matrices and reference trajectories. The \textbf{safety field} generates a collision-risk signal that never bypasses the Nash channel. The \textbf{phase detector} switches parameter sets (fade thresholds, EMA rates, weight bounds) between operating regimes. Because the safety field influences the Nash solve only through the scalar $\lambda$, the Nash problem remains well-posed and convex regardless of the safety force magnitude, and the safety layer can be enabled or disabled without changing the solver structure. This separation is what allows the same Nash formulation to be reused for both longitudinal and lateral control, with only the state-space matrices, reference generators, and weight bounds changing between the two channels.

\section{Computational Considerations}
\label{sec:chap3_compute}

The shared-control layer described in this chapter must run in real time inside the Unity physics loop of a single ego vehicle. Three engineering aspects determine whether the design meets that budget: the per-tick complexity of each module, the numerical measures that keep the outputs bounded and continuous, and the mechanisms that suppress redundant work between ticks.

\subsection{Per-Tick Complexity}
\label{sec:chap3_complexity}

\textbf{Safety fields.} The longitudinal safety field iterates over at most one leader and one follower per ego vehicle, so its per-tick cost is $\mathcal{O}(1)$. The lateral safety field iterates over all $N_{\mathrm{obs}}$ platoon vehicles to accumulate the elliptic potential, giving $\mathcal{O}(N_{\mathrm{obs}})$, where $N_{\mathrm{obs}}$ is the number of platoon vehicles in the ego's neighborhood. In the merging scenarios of Chapter~5, $N_{\mathrm{obs}} \leq 5$. Both fields therefore complete within tens of microseconds on a mid-range central processing unit (CPU).

\textbf{Authority allocator.} The three-stage allocator of Section~\ref{sec:authority_allocation} evaluates two sigmoids and one exponential moving average (EMA) per channel per Nash tick, each an $\mathcal{O}(1)$ arithmetic expression.

\textbf{Nash solve.} Each Nash step formulates and solves a small convex quadratic program (QP) of dimension $N_p \times N_u$, where $N_p = 20$ is the prediction horizon and $N_u \in \{1, 2\}$ is the number of inputs per player. The solver internals are developed in Chapter~4; from the perspective of the shared-control layer, the per-tick QP cost is bounded by design through the pre-factored $\lambda$ grid of Section~\ref{sec:nash_alpha}.

\subsection{Numerical Stability}
\label{sec:chap3_stability}

Several small measures keep the field outputs bounded and continuously differentiable, so that the downstream sigmoid mappings and the Nash cost matrices never receive singular or diverging inputs.

\textbf{Epsilon regularization.} All distance denominators are lower-bounded before use. The elliptic ratio $r^{*}$ is floored at $\varepsilon = 10^{-3}$, the boundary distance $d_k$ is floored at $\varepsilon_b = 0.1\,\mathrm{m}$, and the gap $d$ inside the longitudinal potential is floored at $\varepsilon = 0.1\,\mathrm{m}$. These floors eliminate $1/0$ singularities without materially changing the field shape away from the singular point.

\textbf{Force saturation.} All fields are hard-clipped to their configured maxima ($F_{\mathrm{long,max}} = 800\,\mathrm{N}$, $F_{\mathrm{lat,max}} = 2000\,\mathrm{N}$) before being passed to the authority allocator, so a numerical excursion inside the potential cannot propagate into an unbounded authority command.

\textbf{Smooth switching.} The hyperbolic tangent $\tanh$ replaces every hard sign function in the field: the lateral direction assignment ($\tanh(d_y/\sigma)$), the boundary repulsion, and the phase-fade transitions. The total field is therefore continuously differentiable in the vehicle state, which keeps the authority ratio $\lambda$ continuous through phase transitions.

\textbf{First-order filtering.} The safety force ($\alpha_f = 0.2$ longitudinal, $0.3$ lateral), the field parameters $(s, M_o, R_i, DR)$, the human effort penalty $R_2$, and the authority ratio $\lambda$ itself are all smoothed through EMA filters. The one deliberate exception is the emergency zone of the longitudinal field, where the EMA is bypassed so that a hard-brake command reaches the authority allocator without lag.

\subsection{Avoiding Redundant Work}
\label{sec:chap3_avoidwork}

Two mechanisms cut the number of expensive operations per tick.

\textbf{Dead-band update thresholds.} The QP Hessian $P$ is rebuilt only when the change in $R_2$ or $Q_y$ exceeds a configured threshold ($\Delta R_{2,\min}$ or $\Delta Q_{y,\min}$). Sub-threshold changes are accumulated in the EMA state but do not trigger a matrix rebuild, so per-tick allocation and sparsity-pattern recomputation are suppressed when the tracking error is quasi-static.

\textbf{Pre-factored $\lambda$ grid.} As described in Section~\ref{sec:nash_alpha}, the solver holds one factorized problem instance per discrete $\lambda$ level ($L_\lambda = 11$ longitudinal, $7$ lateral). At runtime the requested $\lambda$ is snapped to the nearest grid point, which removes the most expensive numerical step of QP setup from the real-time execution path.

Taken together, these measures keep the per-tick cost of the safety field, authority allocator, and Nash solve bounded and independent of scenario duration, so that the closed-loop control runs at the target multi-rate schedule (Table~\ref{tab:multi_rate}) without dropped ticks.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:chap3_summary}

This chapter developed the shared-control architecture of the platoon-merging system in three parts: the safety field formulation, the dynamic authority allocator, and the coupling to the Nash equilibrium controller. The main design choices are recapitulated below.

\textbf{Elliptic driving safety field.} The longitudinal channel (Section~\ref{sec:longitudinal_field}) and the lateral channel (Section~\ref{sec:lateral_field}) share the Wang--Li elliptic driving safety field formulation~\cite{wangDrivingSafetyField2015,wangDrivingSafetyField2016,liSharedControlNovel2019}. The field is parameterized by a speed-dependent virtual mass $M_i(v_i)$, an elliptic distance metric $r^{*}$ whose semi-axes scale with the relative velocity, kinetic corrections for the obstacle and ego velocities, and a driver risk addend $DR$. This unified formulation replaces the earlier two-threshold collision-based approach, and produces a continuously differentiable field over the two-dimensional plane.

\textbf{Bidirectional longitudinal field with regime switching.} Four regimes are combined in the longitudinal field: hard safety zones with force saturation below a speed-dependent minimum gap, the elliptic potential of Li et al., a time-to-collision (TTC) floor that ramps between $T_{\mathrm{warn}} = 5.5\,\mathrm{s}$ and $T_{\mathrm{crit}} = 3.0\,\mathrm{s}$, and a bounded attractive regime for large positive gaps. Follower and leader contributions are combined with an asymmetric weight $w_f = 0.5$, chosen to reflect the forward-dominant information flow of Cooperative Adaptive Cruise Control (CACC).

\textbf{Phase-dependent soft transition.} A quadratic fade scales the leader force near the desired gap. The fade threshold is tighter in \textsc{Merge} ($\gamma_{\mathrm{merge}} = 0.08$) than in \textsc{Following} ($\gamma_{\mathrm{follow}} = 0.15$). The field therefore applies its full magnitude during aggressive gap closure and then relaxes into steady-state platoon following.

\textbf{Three-stage authority allocator.} A common architecture is used in both channels (Section~\ref{sec:authority_allocation}): a safety-field sigmoid on $|F|$, the Swain--Rath Eq.~15 sigmoid on the normalized tracking-error index $\ell_n$, and adaptive EMA smoothing that accelerates during large errors and slows near equilibrium. The output $\lambda$ enters the Nash cost matrices through $\alpha = 1/(1+\lambda)$, following Pustilnik and Borrelli~\cite{pustilnikGeneralizedNashEquilibrium2025}.

\textbf{Integration with Nash equilibrium control.} Section~\ref{sec:nash_integration} defined the coupling between the outer authority layer and the inner Nash solver. The coupling elements are: multi-rate scheduling at 50\,Hz for the physics loop, 10\,Hz for the longitudinal Nash step, and 20\,Hz for the lateral Nash step; pre-factored solver instances at a discrete $\lambda$ grid; adaptive $R_2$ that tracks synchronization, driver activity, and detected conflict; adaptive $Q_1$ that stiffens near lane boundaries in \textsc{Following}; online Intelligent Driver Model (IDM) calibration; warm-starting of both solvers throughout \textsc{Approach}; and channel-specific injection of the shared control into throttle/brake or normalized steering.

\section{Nash Game Formulation}
\label{sec:nash_formulation}

\subsection{Two-Player Non-Cooperative Game}

The shared control problem is formulated as a non-cooperative differential game between two players, following the framework of Li et al.~\cite{liSharedControlNovel2019} and its adaptation by Pustilnik and Borrelli~\cite{pustilnikGeneralizedNashEquilibrium2025}. Player~1 is the autonomous system, and Player~2 is the human driver. Both players operate on the same vehicle and apply their respective control inputs (longitudinal acceleration or steering angle) to a shared dynamic system.

Although the formulation is non-cooperative in the game-theoretic sense, cooperative behavior emerges through the dynamic authority parameter $\lambda[k]$. This scalar, computed from the driving safety field~\cite{wangDrivingSafetyField2015, wangDrivingSafetyField2016} by the authority allocator, scales the human's cost function so that under high risk the human's optimal response aligns with the system's reference trajectory. The implementation does not use explicit cross-coupling weight matrices between the two players; the coupling enters solely through the scaling factor $\alpha = 1/(1+\lambda[k])$ that multiplies the tracking term of Player~2 in the stacked cost.

A key design principle is that each player tracks its own reference trajectory independently. The autonomous system tracks the safety-optimal reference $\mathbf{r}_1$, while the human driver tracks the reference $\mathbf{r}_2$ derived from an online driver model. No blending of references occurs within the Nash game. Coordination between players arises from the $\alpha$-scaled cost structure and from the additive combination of the two players' optimal inputs at the vehicle interface:
\begin{equation}
u_{\text{shared}}[k] = u_1^{*}[k] + u_2^{*}[k]
\label{eq:shared_control_ch4}
\end{equation}
where $u_1^{*}[k]$ and $u_2^{*}[k]$ are the first-step optimal actions returned by the Nash solvers of Player~1 and Player~2 respectively, and $u_{\text{shared}}[k]$ is the resulting command applied to the vehicle actuator (throttle/brake for the longitudinal channel, steering angle for the lateral channel) after saturation to the physical actuator bounds. The additive form reflects the physical fact that both players actuate the same input channel: the autonomous system's contribution and the driver's contribution sum at the actuator, and it is the equilibrium condition on $u_1^{*}$ and $u_2^{*}$ (rather than an explicit blending rule) that determines how authority is distributed.

\subsection{Nash Equilibrium Definition}

A strategy pair $(D_1^{*}, D_2^{*})$ constitutes a Nash equilibrium if neither player can unilaterally improve their cost by deviating~\cite{jondConnectedAutomatedVehicle2023}:
\begin{align}
J_1(D_1^{*}, D_2^{*}) &\leq J_1(D_1, D_2^{*}) \quad \forall D_1 \label{eq:nash_def_1}\\
J_2(D_1^{*}, D_2^{*}; \lambda) &\leq J_2(D_1^{*}, D_2; \lambda) \quad \forall D_2 \label{eq:nash_def_2}
\end{align}
where $D_1$ and $D_2$ are the stacked control sequences of Player~1 and Player~2 over the prediction horizon, $J_1$ and $J_2$ are their quadratic cost functionals defined in Section~\ref{sec:cost_functions}, and $\lambda = \lambda[k]$ is the current authority ratio that scales the tracking term of $J_2$.

Unlike centralized optimization, which minimizes a single combined objective, the Nash equilibrium preserves individual objectives and achieves coordination through the $\lambda$-scaling of $J_2$~\cite{liSharedControlNovel2019}.

\subsection{Game Structure per Control Cycle}

At each Nash control cycle $k$, the game proceeds as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Both players observe the current vehicle state $\mathbf{x}[k]$.
\item The corresponding safety field computes the risk force $F[k]$ (Chapter~3): $F_{\text{long}}[k]$ for the longitudinal cycle and $F_{\text{lat}}[k]$ for the lateral cycle.
\item The authority allocator maps $F[k]$ to the authority ratio $\lambda[k]$.
\item Each player's best-response Quadratic Program is solved by OSQP with the pre-computed $\lambda$-level structure of Section~\ref{sec:solution_algorithm}, returning $u_1^{*}[k]$ and $u_2^{*}[k]$.
\item The shared control is computed via Eq.~\eqref{eq:shared_control_ch4} and clipped to the actuator bounds.
\end{enumerate}

This procedure applies identically to both the longitudinal and lateral controllers, differing only in the state-space model, weight matrices, prediction rate, and constraint values.


\section{Distributed Model Predictive Control Framework}
\label{sec:dmpc}

Both Nash controllers propagate the vehicle state over a finite prediction horizon $N_p$ using a linearized state-space model. The longitudinal channel uses a two-state model that captures aerodynamic drag as a small linear damping term. The lateral channel uses the four-state bicycle model. The two models are re-linearized at runtime whenever the longitudinal speed $v_x$ drifts by more than a threshold, which keeps the prediction accurate across the wide speed range spanned by a merge from $20$ to $33\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

\subsection{Longitudinal Dynamics}
\label{sec:longitudinal_dynamics}

The longitudinal dynamics are described by a two-state model with state $\mathbf{x} = [x, v_x]^{\top}$ (longitudinal position and speed) and control input $u = a_{\text{des}}$ (desired acceleration in $\mathrm{m/s^2}$). The continuous-time state-space matrices are:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A}_c^{\text{long}} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -d_{\text{lin}} \end{bmatrix}, \quad
\mathbf{B}_c^{\text{long}} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
\mathbf{C}^{\text{long}} = \mathbf{I}_2
\label{eq:long_ss}
\end{equation}
where $d_{\text{lin}} = \rho \, C_d \, A_f \, |v_x| / m_{\text{eff}}$ is the linearized drag coefficient, $\rho = 1.225\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ is the air density, $C_d$ is the drag coefficient, $A_f$ is the frontal area, and $m_{\text{eff}}$ is the effective inertial mass. The unit entry in $\mathbf{B}_c^{\text{long}}$ reflects the fact that the control input is specified as a desired acceleration in $\mathrm{m/s^2}$: the throttle and brake commands that realize this acceleration are computed downstream by an inverse-powertrain map that cancels the drag, rolling-resistance, and grade terms as feedforward, so from the Nash solver's perspective the control input directly drives $\dot{v}_x$. Both players apply acceleration to the same vehicle, hence $\mathbf{B}_1 = \mathbf{B}_2 = \mathbf{B}_c^{\text{long}}$. The output selection matrix $\mathbf{C}^{\text{long}} = \mathbf{I}_2$ produces $\mathbf{z} = [x, v_x]^{\top}$ with $n_x = 2$ states and $n_z = 2$ outputs.

Discretization to the Nash step $T_s = T_{\text{long}} = 0.1\,\mathrm{s}$ uses the augmented matrix exponential:
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{A}^{\text{long}} & \mathbf{B}^{\text{long}} \\ \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{1} \end{bmatrix}
= \exp\!\left( \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{A}_c^{\text{long}} & \mathbf{B}_c^{\text{long}} \\ \mathbf{0} & 0 \end{bmatrix} T_s \right)
\label{eq:long_zoh}
\end{equation}
which yields the zero-order-hold discretization $(\mathbf{A}^{\text{long}}, \mathbf{B}^{\text{long}})$ used by the solver.

\subsection{Lateral Dynamics: Bicycle Model}
\label{sec:lateral_dynamics}

The lateral dynamics use the linearized single-track bicycle model of Chapter~2, with state $\mathbf{x} = [y, \dot{y}, \psi, \dot{\psi}]^{\top}$ (lateral position, lateral velocity, heading angle, and yaw rate) and control input $\delta$ (front-wheel steering angle). The continuous-time state-space matrices are:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A}_c^{\text{lat}} = \begin{bmatrix}
0 & 1 & v_x & 0 \\[4pt]
0 & -\dfrac{2(C_{af} + C_{ar})}{m \, v_x} - (1 - f_v) k_{\dot{y}} & 0 & \dfrac{2(l_r C_{ar} - l_f C_{af})}{m \, v_x} - v_x \\[4pt]
0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\[4pt]
0 & \dfrac{2(l_r C_{ar} - l_f C_{af})}{I_z \, v_x} & 0 & -\dfrac{2(l_f^2 C_{af} + l_r^2 C_{ar})}{I_z \, v_x} - (1 - f_v) k_{\dot{\psi}}
\end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:Ac_bicycle}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{B}_c^{\text{lat}} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 2 C_{af} / m \\ 0 \\ 2 l_f C_{af} / I_z \end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:Bc_bicycle}
\end{equation}
where $C_{af}$ and $C_{ar}$ are the per-tire cornering stiffness coefficients of the front and rear axles (each entry is multiplied by two inside $\mathbf{A}_c^{\text{lat}}$ and $\mathbf{B}_c^{\text{lat}}$ to account for both wheels of the axle), $l_f$ and $l_r$ are the distances from the center of gravity to the front and rear axles, $m$ is the vehicle mass, $I_z$ is the yaw moment of inertia, and $v_x$ is the longitudinal velocity (assumed constant within each prediction horizon)~\cite{rajamaniVehicleDynamicsControl2005, fernandezVehicleDynamicsModel}. The entry $\mathbf{A}_c^{\text{lat}}[0,2] = v_x$ represents the kinematic coupling between heading and global lateral position, as introduced in Chapter~2.

The additional terms $-(1 - f_v) k_{\dot{y}}$ in $\mathbf{A}_c^{\text{lat}}[1,1]$ and $-(1 - f_v) k_{\dot{\psi}}$ in $\mathbf{A}_c^{\text{lat}}[3,3]$ are low-speed damping corrections that prevent the linearized tire model from becoming ill-conditioned at small $v_x$, where the analytic slip stiffness diverges as $1/v_x$. Here $k_{\dot{y}} = \mu_t \, g / 0.5$ and $k_{\dot{\psi}} = \mu_t \, m \, g \, L / (4 \, I_z \, 0.3)$ are the friction-based damping gains, with $\mu_t$ the tire--road friction coefficient, $g$ the gravitational acceleration, and $L = l_f + l_r$ the wheelbase. The smooth-switching factor
\begin{equation}
f_v = \tfrac{1}{2}\left( \tanh(10 \, v_x - 8) + 1 \right)
\label{eq:fv_switch}
\end{equation}
transitions monotonically from $f_v \approx 0$ at $v_x = 0$ to $f_v \approx 1$ for $v_x \gtrsim 1\,\mathrm{m/s}$, so that the damping correction is active only during starts and low-speed maneuvers and vanishes at the platoon cruising speed. This formulation keeps the state-space model well-conditioned across the full operating range without introducing a discontinuous switch.

The vehicle parameters used in the bicycle model are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:vehicle_params}.

Discretization of the lateral model uses the same augmented matrix exponential as the longitudinal channel (Eq.~\eqref{eq:long_zoh}), applied with $T_s = T_{\text{lat}} = 0.05\,\mathrm{s}$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{A}^{\text{lat}} & \mathbf{B}^{\text{lat}} \\ \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{1} \end{bmatrix}
= \exp\!\left( \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{A}_c^{\text{lat}} & \mathbf{B}_c^{\text{lat}} \\ \mathbf{0} & 0 \end{bmatrix} T_s \right)
\label{eq:zoh_discretization}
\end{equation}
The output matrix selects the lateral position and heading:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{C}^{\text{lat}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:C_lateral}
\end{equation}
yielding $\mathbf{z} = [y, \psi]^{\top}$ with $n_x = 4$ states and $n_z = 2$ outputs. As in the longitudinal case, both players apply steering to the same vehicle, so $\mathbf{B}_1 = \mathbf{B}_2 = \mathbf{B}^{\text{lat}}$.

\subsection{Runtime Re-Linearization}
\label{sec:relinearization}

Because both $\mathbf{A}_c^{\text{long}}$ and $\mathbf{A}_c^{\text{lat}}$ depend on the current longitudinal speed $v_x$, the linearization is refreshed at runtime whenever $v_x$ drifts by more than a threshold. The longitudinal solver rebuilds its matrices when $|v_x - v_x^{\text{cached}}| > 2.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$, and the lateral solver rebuilds on every Nash tick because the Coriolis-like $v_x$ terms in $\mathbf{A}_c^{\text{lat}}$ have a stronger sensitivity to speed. Each rebuild calls the vehicle's analytic Jacobian, re-discretizes via Eq.~\eqref{eq:long_zoh}/\eqref{eq:zoh_discretization}, and re-factorizes the OSQP problems at all pre-computed $\lambda$ levels.

\subsection{Multi-Step Prediction and Compact Form}

For each channel, the system output over the prediction horizon $N_p$ is written in compact form as:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{Z}[k] = \mathbf{U} \, \mathbf{x}[k] + \mathbf{H} \, D_1 + \mathbf{H} \, D_2
\label{eq:compact_prediction}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{U} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_z N_p \times n_x}$ is the free-response matrix, $\mathbf{H} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_z N_p \times N_u}$ is the forced-response matrix, and $D_1, D_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{N_u}$ are the stacked control sequences of Player~1 and Player~2 over the control horizon $N_u$. Since $\mathbf{B}_1 = \mathbf{B}_2$ in both channels, the forced-response matrices coincide: $\mathbf{H}_1 = \mathbf{H}_2 \equiv \mathbf{H}$.

The free-response matrix has the block form:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{U} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{C} \mathbf{A} \\ \mathbf{C} \mathbf{A}^2 \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{C} \mathbf{A}^{N_p} \end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:U_matrix}
\end{equation}
and the forced-response matrix has a lower-triangular Toeplitz structure:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{H} = \begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{C} \mathbf{B} & \mathbf{0} & \cdots & \mathbf{0} \\
\mathbf{C} \mathbf{A} \mathbf{B} & \mathbf{C} \mathbf{B} & \cdots & \mathbf{0} \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
\mathbf{C} \mathbf{A}^{N_p-1} \mathbf{B} & \mathbf{C} \mathbf{A}^{N_p-2} \mathbf{B} & \cdots & \mathbf{C} \mathbf{A}^{N_p-N_u} \mathbf{B}
\end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:H_matrix}
\end{equation}
Both channels use $N_p = 20$ prediction steps and $N_u = 10$ control steps. The dimensions of $\mathbf{U}$ and $\mathbf{H}$ therefore differ between channels only through the state and output sizes ($n_x = n_z = 2$ for the longitudinal channel; $n_x = 4$, $n_z = 2$ for the lateral channel). The algebraic structure of Equations~\eqref{eq:U_matrix}--\eqref{eq:H_matrix} is otherwise identical.

\section{Cost Function Formulation}
\label{sec:cost_functions}
%====================================================================
This section develops the quadratic cost functions that define the Nash game of Section~\ref{sec:nash_formulation}. The presentation focuses on three items that belong to the theoretical formulation of the game: the structure of the two players' costs, the authority mapping that links the allocator output to the human's tracking weight, and the horizon shaping applied to the tracking term. The runtime adaptation of the cost weights (synchronization-based, activity-based, and conflict-based mechanisms), the lane-boundary tracking-weight adaptation in the following phase, and the online driver model calibration are integration-level mechanisms and are developed in Section~\ref{sec:nash_integration} of Chapter~3.

\subsection{Quadratic Cost Structure}
\label{sec:cost_quadratic}

Following the framework of Li et al.~\cite{liSharedControlNovel2019} as reformulated by Pustilnik and Borrelli~\cite{pustilnikGeneralizedNashEquilibrium2025}, each player minimizes a finite-horizon quadratic cost that penalizes tracking error and own control effort. Each player carries its own tracking-weight matrix ($Q_1$ for the system, $Q_2[k]$ for the human) and its own effort weight ($R_1$ and $R_2$):
\begin{align}
J_1(D_1, D_2) &= \sum_{j=0}^{N_p-1} \| \mathbf{e}_1[k+j] \|_{Q_1[j]}^{2} + \sum_{j=0}^{N_u-1} R_1 \, u_1[k+j]^2 \label{eq:cost_player1}\\
J_2(D_1, D_2) &= \sum_{j=0}^{N_p-1} \| \mathbf{e}_2[k+j] \|_{Q_2[k, j]}^{2} + \sum_{j=0}^{N_u-1} R_2 \, u_2[k+j]^2 \label{eq:cost_player2}
\end{align}
where $\mathbf{e}_i[k+j] = \mathbf{r}_i[k+j] - \mathbf{z}[k+j]$ is the tracking error of player $i$ at prediction step $j$, $\mathbf{z}[k+j]$ is the predicted output at step $j$ under the joint control $u_1[k+j] + u_2[k+j]$, $Q_1[j]$ and $Q_2[k, j]$ are the horizon-indexed tracking weights of Player~1 and Player~2 respectively, and $R_1, R_2 > 0$ are the corresponding scalar effort penalties. Both players observe the same predicted output $\mathbf{z}[k+j]$, because both actuate the same physical signal (gap and velocity for the longitudinal channel; lateral position and heading for the lateral channel), and each player measures the deviation against its own reference $\mathbf{r}_i[k+j]$.

The implementation does not include cross-coupling weight matrices $S_1, S_2$ that would penalize each player for the other player's control effort. The coordination between the two players arises entirely from the authority mapping developed in Section~\ref{sec:pustilnik_mapping}, together with the additive combination $u_{\text{shared}}[k] = u_1^{*}[k] + u_2^{*}[k]$ defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:shared_control_ch4}.

\subsection{Pustilnik--Borrelli Authority Mapping}
\label{sec:pustilnik_mapping}

The authority ratio $\lambda[k]$ produced by the allocator (Section~\ref{sec:authority_allocation}) enters the game through the human's tracking weight. Rather than the direct proportionality $Q_2 \propto \lambda$ used by Li et al.~\cite{liSharedControlNovel2019}, the implementation adopts the Pustilnik--Borrelli mapping~\cite{pustilnikGeneralizedNashEquilibrium2025}:
\begin{equation}
Q_2[k, j] = \alpha[k] \, Q_1[j], \qquad \alpha[k] = \frac{1}{1 + \lambda[k]}
\label{eq:pustilnik_alpha}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha[k] \in (0, 1]$ is the scaling factor applied to Player~2's tracking weight at cycle $k$ and $\lambda[k] \in [\lambda_{\min}, \lambda_{\max}]$ is the authority ratio from the allocator. The mapping is bounded and monotonically decreasing: $\alpha \to 1$ as $\lambda \to 0$, and $\alpha \to 0$ as $\lambda \to \infty$. For the longitudinal channel $\lambda \in [0.1, 50]$, so $\alpha \in [0.0196, 0.909]$; for the lateral channel $\lambda \in [0.1, 10]$, so $\alpha \in [0.091, 0.909]$.

Two limiting regimes characterize the effect of $\alpha$ on the Nash equilibrium:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Safe conditions} ($\lambda \to 0$, $\alpha \to 1$). $Q_2[k, j] \to Q_1[j]$, so the human's tracking weight equals the system's. Each player minimizes the deviation from its own reference under equal tracking pressure. The equilibrium input $u_2^{*}[k]$ tracks $\mathbf{r}_2$ freely, and the driver retains full authority over the actuator.
\item \textbf{Dangerous conditions} ($\lambda \gg 1$, $\alpha \to 0$). $Q_2[k, j] \to 0$, so the human's tracking cost vanishes. Player~2's cost reduces to $J_2 \to \sum_j R_2 \, u_2^2$, which is minimized at $u_2^{*}[k] = 0$ (subject to any active input-rate constraints). The shared control $u_{\text{shared}}[k] = u_1^{*}[k] + u_2^{*}[k]$ therefore collapses onto $u_1^{*}[k]$, and the system takes full authority.
\end{itemize}

The Pustilnik--Borrelli mapping guarantees that Player~2's Hessian remains positive definite for every $\lambda \geq 0$: from Eq.~\eqref{eq:cost_player2} and the compact prediction of Section~\ref{sec:dmpc}, Player~2's quadratic form is $2 \alpha \, \mathbf{H}^{\top} \bar{Q}_1 \mathbf{H} + 2 R_2 \, \mathbf{I}_{N_u} \succ 0$ for any $\alpha > 0$ and $R_2 > 0$. Positive-definiteness is preserved as $\lambda \to \infty$ ($\alpha \to 0$), because the $R_2 \mathbf{I}$ term does not vanish. The alternative mapping $Q_2 \propto \lambda$ would produce a Hessian $2 \lambda \, \mathbf{H}^{\top} \bar{Q}_1 \mathbf{H} + 2 R_2 \, \mathbf{I}_{N_u}$ whose condition number grows without bound as $\lambda$ increases, degrading the numerical stability of the solver at high risk levels.

\subsection{Horizon Weighting and Terminal Weight}
\label{sec:horizon_weighting}

The tracking-weight sequence $Q_1[j]$ that appears in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:cost_player1}--\eqref{eq:cost_player2} is not held constant along the prediction horizon. Two mechanisms shape the horizon profile.

\paragraph{Linear ramp on the running weight.} Following Flad et al.~\cite{fladCooperativeSharedControl2017}, the running tracking weight is scaled by a linear ramp that increases from $1 \times$ at $j = 0$ to $w_Q \times$ at $j = N_p - 2$:
\begin{equation}
Q_1[j] = s_j \, Q_1^{\text{nom}}, \qquad s_j = 1 + \frac{j}{N_p - 1} \left( w_Q - 1 \right), \quad j = 0, 1, \ldots, N_p - 2
\label{eq:horizon_ramp}
\end{equation}
where $Q_1^{\text{nom}}$ is the nominal in-lane tracking weight (defined per channel in Sections~\ref{sec:long_weights}--\ref{sec:lat_weights}), $s_j$ is the horizon scaling coefficient at step $j$, and $w_Q > 1$ is the channel-specific ramp coefficient. The ramp gives progressively more weight to deviations that persist further into the future, so that the solver treats a transient overshoot near the current step less aggressively than a sustained error at the end of the horizon.

\paragraph{Terminal weight.} At the final horizon step $j = N_p - 1$, the ramped $Q_1[j]$ is replaced by a distinct terminal weight $Q_{1,T}$:
\begin{equation}
Q_1[N_p - 1] = Q_{1,T}
\label{eq:terminal_weight}
\end{equation}
where $Q_{1,T}$ has substantially larger entries than $Q_1^{\text{nom}}$ (roughly one order of magnitude, as specified per channel below). The terminal weight approximates a control-Lyapunov terminal cost that enforces state-space closure at the end of the horizon and improves the receding-horizon stability of the closed loop.

The ramp coefficient is $w_Q = 2.0$ for the longitudinal channel and $w_Q = 1.5$ for the lateral channel. The longitudinal channel uses the larger ramp because gap errors near the two-second horizon end remain physically meaningful for merge planning, whereas lateral errors decay faster under the shorter time step and require less far-horizon emphasis.

\subsection{Longitudinal Channel Weights}
\label{sec:long_weights}

The nominal tracking weight matrix for the longitudinal controller is:
\begin{equation}
Q_1^{\text{nom, long}} = \begin{bmatrix} Q_p & 0 \\ 0 & Q_v \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5000 & 0 \\ 0 & 1500 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
Q_{1,T}^{\text{long}} = \begin{bmatrix} 50000 & 0 \\ 0 & 6000 \end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:Q1_long}
\end{equation}
where $Q_p$ is the position (gap) tracking weight, $Q_v$ is the velocity tracking weight, and $Q_{1,T}^{\text{long}}$ is the terminal state weight applied at $j = N_p - 1$. The position weight exceeds the velocity weight because the primary tracking objective during platoon merging is gap closure; velocity matching then follows through the constant-time-gap reference of Section~\ref{sec:reference_generation}. The terminal weights are ten and four times the corresponding nominal values, respectively, so that the solver commits to a specific end-of-horizon platoon state rather than trading off gap and velocity errors at the terminal step.

The effort penalties are:
\begin{equation}
R_1^{\text{long}} = 75000, \qquad R_2^{\text{long}}[k] \in [40000, 120000]
\label{eq:R_long}
\end{equation}
where $R_1^{\text{long}}$ is the system's effort penalty (held constant) and $R_2^{\text{long}}[k]$ is the human's effort penalty, which is adapted online by the mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:nash_r2} between $R_{2,\text{follow}}^{\text{long}} = 40000$ and $R_{2,\text{start}}^{\text{long}} = 120000$. The choice $R_{2,\text{follow}}^{\text{long}} < R_1^{\text{long}}$ means that once the ego has synchronized to the platoon, the human's effort is penalized less than the system's, granting the driver higher steady-state authority~\cite{naTwoNashequilibriumbasedSteering2022}. The ratio $Q_v / R_1^{\text{long}} \approx 0.02$ reproduces the Pareto-optimal single-player tuning identified in preliminary open-loop simulation.

\subsection{Lateral Channel Weights}
\label{sec:lat_weights}

The nominal tracking weight matrix for the lateral controller is:
\begin{equation}
Q_1^{\text{nom, lat}} = \begin{bmatrix} Q_y & 0 \\ 0 & Q_\psi \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5000 & 0 \\ 0 & 2000 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
Q_{1,T}^{\text{lat}} = \begin{bmatrix} 20000 & 0 \\ 0 & 8000 \end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:Q1_lat}
\end{equation}
where $Q_y$ is the lateral position tracking weight, $Q_\psi$ is the heading tracking weight, and $Q_{1,T}^{\text{lat}}$ is the corresponding terminal weight. The ratio $Q_\psi / Q_y = 0.4$ was tuned so that a heading error of $0.1\,\mathrm{rad}$ carries a cost comparable to a lateral offset of $0.3\,\mathrm{m}$; larger heading weights caused the solver to over-correct heading aggressively during lane changes, producing steering oscillation. In the following phase these nominal values are further adapted to the lane-boundary encroachment fraction, as developed in Section~\ref{sec:nash_q1}.

The nominal effort penalties are:
\begin{equation}
R_1^{\text{lat, nom}} = R_2^{\text{lat, nom}} = 250000
\label{eq:R_lat_nominal}
\end{equation}
where both weights are set equally at $250000$ to keep steering commands smooth under the wide operating envelope. During the \textsc{GapSearch} phase the maneuver goal is heading regulation $\psi \to 0$ rather than lateral displacement, and a large entry heading (up to $25^\circ$ following a driver-initiated deviation before the merge is armed) requires strong corrective steering. The nominal effort weights are therefore temporarily reduced to
\begin{equation}
R_1^{\text{lat, GS}} = R_2^{\text{lat, GS}} = 25000
\label{eq:R_lat_gs}
\end{equation}
during \textsc{GapSearch}, which lets the solver produce steering angles up to $\delta \approx -20^\circ$ where the nominal weights would produce only $\delta \approx -2^\circ$. The nominal values are restored on the transition from \textsc{GapSearch} to \textsc{Merge}. Beyond this phase-based override, the lateral effort penalty is further adapted online by the synchronization, activity, and conflict mechanisms of Section~\ref{sec:nash_r2}.

The lateral effort weights of $2.5 \times 10^5$ are approximately three orders of magnitude larger than the longitudinal $R_1^{\text{long}} = 7.5 \times 10^4$. This ratio reflects the different physical scales of the two control inputs: longitudinal commands are typical accelerations of order $1$--$2\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, while steering angles are typical corrections of order $0.01\,\mathrm{rad}$. The larger lateral weights keep steering commands physically realistic despite the smaller magnitude of the input.

\subsection{Runtime Adaptation and Driver Modeling}
\label{sec:cost_runtime}

The values listed in Sections~\ref{sec:long_weights}--\ref{sec:lat_weights} are the nominal levels used to initialize the Nash cost matrices. During a run, four integration-level mechanisms modify these values so that the cost structure reflects the current platoon state, driver engagement, and phase context. All four are developed in the corresponding subsections of Chapter~3:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Adaptive $R_2$} — synchronization-based interpolation between $R_{2,\text{start}}$ and $R_{2,\text{follow}}$, activity-based scaling in response to driver input, and conflict-based boost for the lateral channel during opposing-intent events. Developed in Section~\ref{sec:nash_r2}.
\item \textbf{Adaptive $Q_1$ in following phase} — encroachment-fraction interpolation between the nominal lateral weights and elevated boundary weights, preventing residual limit cycles near the lane center. Developed in Section~\ref{sec:nash_q1}.
\item \textbf{Online driver model calibration} — real-time estimation of the human reference $\mathbf{r}_2$ for the longitudinal channel through IDM parameter inversion. Developed in Section~\ref{sec:nash_driver_calib}, with the reference-generation details in Section~\ref{sec:reference_generation}.
\item \textbf{Warm-starting and re-linearization} — cross-phase continuity of the solver internal state and speed-dependent refresh of the discrete-time matrices. Developed in Section~\ref{sec:nash_warm}.
\end{itemize}

The cost formulation in this section defines only the fixed structure of the game (Eqs.~\eqref{eq:cost_player1}--\eqref{eq:cost_player2}), the Pustilnik--Borrelli mapping (Eq.~\eqref{eq:pustilnik_alpha}), the horizon shaping (Eqs.~\eqref{eq:horizon_ramp}--\eqref{eq:terminal_weight}), and the nominal per-channel weights. All time-varying refinements enter through the integration layer of Chapter~3.

\subsection{Compact Matrix Representation}
\label{sec:cost_compact}

Using the compact prediction of Eq.~\eqref{eq:compact_prediction}, the two cost functions of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:cost_player1}--\eqref{eq:cost_player2} take the block-matrix form:
\begin{align}
J_1(D_1, D_2) &= \mathbf{E}_1^{\top} \bar{Q}_1 \mathbf{E}_1 + D_1^{\top} \bar{R}_1 D_1 \label{eq:cost_matrix_1}\\
J_2(D_1, D_2) &= \alpha[k] \, \mathbf{E}_2^{\top} \bar{Q}_1 \mathbf{E}_2 + D_2^{\top} \bar{R}_2 D_2 \label{eq:cost_matrix_2}
\end{align}
where $\bar{Q}_1 = \mathrm{blkdiag}\!\left( s_0 \, Q_1^{\text{nom}}, s_1 \, Q_1^{\text{nom}}, \ldots, s_{N_p - 2} \, Q_1^{\text{nom}}, Q_{1,T} \right)$ is the block-diagonal horizon weight combining the ramp of Eq.~\eqref{eq:horizon_ramp} and the terminal weight of Eq.~\eqref{eq:terminal_weight}; $\bar{R}_i = R_i \, \mathbf{I}_{N_u}$ is the effort weight of player $i$ over the control horizon; $\mathbf{E}_i = \mathbf{R}_i - \mathbf{U} \mathbf{x}[k] - \mathbf{H} D_1 - \mathbf{H} D_2$ is the stacked tracking error of player $i$; and the scalar $\alpha[k] = 1/(1 + \lambda[k])$ implements the Pustilnik--Borrelli authority mapping of Eq.~\eqref{eq:pustilnik_alpha}. The $\alpha[k]$ factor appears as a single scalar multiplier on Player~2's tracking quadratic form, which the solver exploits by pre-factoring one OSQP instance per discrete level of $\lambda$ (Section~\ref{sec:solution_algorithm}) rather than rebuilding the Hessian at every cycle.

\section{Dynamic Authority Allocation}
\label{sec:authority_allocation_}
%====================================================================
The Nash game of Section~\ref{sec:nash_formulation} takes the authority ratio $\lambda[k]$ as an exogenous input at every cycle. The full definition of $\lambda[k]$ and its two-channel implementation is developed in the shared-control chapter (Section~\ref{sec:authority_allocation}), which describes the three-stage architecture common to both channels: a sigmoid on the driving-safety-field force, a Swain--Rath performance sigmoid on the normalized tracking errors, and an element-wise fusion followed by an adaptive exponential moving average. Only the parameters that make $\lambda[k]$ observable to the cost formulation are recapitulated here.

The longitudinal allocator produces $\lambda_{\text{long}}[k] \in [\lambda_{\min}, \lambda_{\max}] = [0.1, 50]$ from the longitudinal safety force and the gap and velocity tracking errors; the lateral allocator produces $\lambda_{\text{lat}}[k] \in [0.1, 10]$ from the lateral safety force and the lane-offset tracking error. The narrower lateral range reflects the observation that excessive system authority during a lane change produces abrupt steering corrections that reduce passenger comfort. Both channels expose their $\lambda[k]$ to the Nash cost through the Pustilnik--Borrelli mapping introduced in Eq.~\eqref{eq:pustilnik_alpha}:
\begin{equation}
\alpha[k] = \frac{1}{1 + \lambda[k]}
\label{eq:alpha_recap}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha[k] \in (0, 1]$ is the scaling factor applied to Player~2's tracking weight $Q_2[k, j] = \alpha[k] \, Q_1[j]$. The bounded, monotonically decreasing shape of Eq.~\eqref{eq:alpha_recap} together with the two-channel ranges above yields $\alpha_{\text{long}} \in [0.0196, 0.909]$ and $\alpha_{\text{lat}} \in [0.091, 0.909]$: the lateral channel never reaches the near-zero regime that transfers full authority to the system, whereas the longitudinal channel approaches it under high-risk conditions. This is consistent with the intended operational split: the longitudinal system can take near-full authority during an imminent collision, while the lateral system retains enough human contribution to preserve steering handover continuity.

The remaining implementation details (per-channel sigmoid parameters $\lambda_{\min}$, $\lambda_{\max}$, $F_{\text{mid}}$, $k_s$, the tracking-error normalization bounds, the adaptive EMA coefficients, the phase-specific caps applied in \textsc{Following}, and the no-leader branch) are given in Section~\ref{sec:authority_allocation}. The pre-computed $\lambda$-level grid used by the solver to avoid rebuilding the Hessian at every cycle is developed in Section~\ref{sec:solution_algorithm}.

\section{Nash Equilibrium Solution via Iterative Best Response}
\label{sec:solution_algorithm}
% ===================================================================

The Nash equilibrium of the two coupled quadratic programs defined in Section~\ref{sec:cost_functions} is computed at every cycle by an Iterative Best Response (IBR) loop. Each iteration alternates between two smaller Quadratic Programs, one for each player, in which the other player's control sequence is held fixed. Convergence of the IBR loop to a Nash equilibrium follows from the strict convexity of the two per-player QPs, which is guaranteed by the positive-definite structure of the Hessians developed in Section~\ref{sec:pustilnik_mapping}.

\subsection{Best-Response Quadratic Programs}
\label{sec:best_response_qp}

At iteration $\iota$ of cycle $k$, Player~1 solves for its stacked control sequence $D_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{N_u}$ while Player~2's sequence $D_2^{(\iota-1)}$ from the previous iteration is held fixed:
\begin{equation}
D_1^{(\iota)} = \arg\min_{D_1} \; \frac{1}{2} D_1^{\top} P_1 \, D_1 + q_1(D_2^{(\iota-1)})^{\top} D_1
\quad \text{s.t.} \quad C D_1 \in [l_1, u_1]
\label{eq:qp_player1}
\end{equation}
where $P_1 = 2 \left( \mathbf{H}^{\top} \bar{Q}_1 \mathbf{H} + R_1 \, \mathbf{I}_{N_u} \right)$ is the Player~1 Hessian, $q_1(D_2) = -2 \left( \mathbf{H}^{\top} \bar{Q}_1 \, \mathbf{e}_1^{\text{free}} - \mathbf{H}^{\top} \bar{Q}_1 \mathbf{H} \, D_2 \right)$ is the linear cost that depends on the other player's fixed sequence, $\mathbf{e}_1^{\text{free}} = \mathbf{R}_1 - \mathbf{U} \mathbf{x}[k]$ is the free-response tracking error, $C$ is the constraint matrix combining the box and rate-limit rows, and $[l_1, u_1]$ are the corresponding bounds.

Player~2 then solves the analogous problem with the updated $D_1^{(\iota)}$ fixed:
\begin{equation}
D_2^{(\iota)} = \arg\min_{D_2} \; \frac{1}{2} D_2^{\top} P_2(\alpha) \, D_2 + q_2(\alpha, D_1^{(\iota)})^{\top} D_2
\quad \text{s.t.} \quad C D_2 \in [l_2, u_2]
\label{eq:qp_player2}
\end{equation}
where $P_2(\alpha) = 2 \left( \alpha[k] \, \mathbf{H}^{\top} \bar{Q}_1 \mathbf{H} + R_2 \, \mathbf{I}_{N_u} \right)$ is the Player~2 Hessian scaled by the Pustilnik--Borrelli factor $\alpha[k]$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:pustilnik_alpha}, and $q_2(\alpha, D_1) = -2 \alpha[k] \left( \mathbf{H}^{\top} \bar{Q}_1 \, \mathbf{e}_2^{\text{free}} - \mathbf{H}^{\top} \bar{Q}_1 \mathbf{H} \, D_1 \right)$ is the corresponding linear cost. The scalar $\alpha[k]$ multiplies both the quadratic and the linear tracking terms of Player~2 uniformly, which is what makes the pre-computation of Section~\ref{sec:precomputed_grid} possible.

The two coupled QPs of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:qp_player1}--\eqref{eq:qp_player2} are the discrete-time projection of the continuous-time best-response equations of the differential game.

\subsection{IBR Loop and Convergence Criterion}
\label{sec:ibr_loop}

The IBR loop is initialized from a warm start $(D_1^{(0)}, D_2^{(0)})$ inherited from the previous cycle by a horizon shift (Section~\ref{sec:nash_warm}). Each subsequent iteration performs one solve of Eq.~\eqref{eq:qp_player1} followed by one solve of Eq.~\eqref{eq:qp_player2}. Convergence is declared when the largest change in either player's control sequence between successive iterations falls below a tolerance:
\begin{equation}
\Delta^{(\iota)} = \max\!\left( \left\| D_1^{(\iota)} - D_1^{(\iota-1)} \right\|_{\infty}, \; \left\| D_2^{(\iota)} - D_2^{(\iota-1)} \right\|_{\infty} \right) < \varepsilon_{\text{IBR}}
\label{eq:ibr_convergence}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta^{(\iota)}$ is the infinity-norm best-response gap at iteration $\iota$ and $\varepsilon_{\text{IBR}} = 10^{-4}$ is the tolerance shared by both channels. A hard cap of $\iota_{\max} = 15$ iterations prevents runaway execution; in typical merge scenarios the loop converges in three to eight iterations because the warm start already lies close to the new optimum. If the maximum iteration count is reached without convergence, the last iterate is returned unchanged; the next cycle's warm start absorbs the residual gap and the loop typically converges within one or two additional iterations.

\subsection{Pre-Computed $\lambda$-Level Grid for DPP Compliance}
\label{sec:precomputed_grid}

The Player~2 Hessian $P_2(\alpha)$ depends on $\alpha[k]$ and therefore on $\lambda[k]$, which changes continuously. Rebuilding and re-factorizing the OSQP problem at every Nash cycle would violate the Disciplined Parametric Programming (DPP) contract that OSQP relies on for constant-time factorization reuse. Instead, the solver instantiates one OSQP problem per discrete $\lambda$ level and selects the closest level at runtime:
\begin{equation}
i^{*}[k] = \arg\min_{i \in \{1, \ldots, L_\lambda\}} \; \left| \ln \Lambda_i - \ln \lambda[k] \right|
\label{eq:lambda_snap}
\end{equation}
where $\Lambda = \{\Lambda_1, \ldots, \Lambda_{L_\lambda}\}$ is the pre-defined discrete $\lambda$ grid and $i^{*}[k]$ is the index of the closest grid point to $\lambda[k]$ in log-space. Log-space nearest-neighbor selection places the grid points at approximately equal relative distance, matching the multiplicative sensitivity of $\alpha = 1/(1+\lambda)$ to $\lambda$.

The longitudinal channel uses $L_\lambda^{\text{long}} = 11$ pre-computed levels:
\begin{equation}
\Lambda^{\text{long}} = \{0.1, \, 0.25, \, 0.5, \, 1.0, \, 2.0, \, 5.0, \, 10.0, \, 15.0, \, 30.0, \, 40.0, \, 50.0\}
\label{eq:long_levels}
\end{equation}
and the lateral channel uses $L_\lambda^{\text{lat}} = 7$ pre-computed levels:
\begin{equation}
\Lambda^{\text{lat}} = \{0.1, \, 0.25, \, 0.5, \, 1.0, \, 2.0, \, 5.0, \, 10.0\}
\label{eq:lat_levels}
\end{equation}
Both grid ceilings match the sigmoid ceilings of Section~\ref{sec:authority_allocation}: $\Lambda_{11}^{\text{long}} = 50 = \lambda_{\max}^{\text{long}}$ and $\Lambda_7^{\text{lat}} = 10 = \lambda_{\max}^{\text{lat}}$. Every authority ratio produced by the allocator therefore lies within the pre-factored grid range, and the log-space snap of Eq.~\eqref{eq:lambda_snap} selects the nearest level without truncation. The uppermost levels correspond to $\alpha = 1/(1 + 50) \approx 0.0196$ for the longitudinal channel and $\alpha = 1/(1 + 10) \approx 0.0909$ for the lateral channel, which set the effective floors on Player~2's tracking weight under the highest allocator output.

At initialization, one OSQP problem instance is built and factorized per grid level $\Lambda_i$; at runtime, only the linear-cost vector $q_2$ of the selected instance is updated per IBR iteration, which is an $\mathcal{O}(N_u)$ operation. Player~1's problem does not depend on $\lambda$ and therefore uses a single OSQP instance.

\subsection{Hessian Regularization}
\label{sec:hessian_regularization}

To prevent numerical singularity when the tracking-weight Hessian $\mathbf{H}^{\top} \bar{Q}_1 \mathbf{H}$ becomes ill-conditioned (for example, when the terminal weight dominates the running weight, or when the linearized state-space model produces near-zero directional response), a small diagonal regularization is added at construction:
\begin{equation}
\left( \mathbf{H}^{\top} \bar{Q}_1 \mathbf{H} \right)_{\text{reg}} = \mathbf{H}^{\top} \bar{Q}_1 \mathbf{H} + \varepsilon_{\text{reg}} \, \mathbf{I}_{N_u}
\label{eq:reg}
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon_{\text{reg}} = 10^{-5}$ is the regularization coefficient applied once during the construction of the shared tracking-weight Hessian. This regularization is inherited by both $P_1$ and $P_2(\alpha)$ through their common $\mathbf{H}^{\top} \bar{Q}_1 \mathbf{H}$ term, and its magnitude is small enough that the equilibrium is unchanged to numerical precision while the positive-definiteness of both Hessians is guaranteed for every $\alpha > 0$.

\subsection{Constraints}
\label{sec:qp_constraints}

The constraint matrix $C$ that appears in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:qp_player1}--\eqref{eq:qp_player2} encodes two families of constraints on the control sequence.

\paragraph{Box constraints.} Each element of $D_i$ is bounded to the physically admissible input range of player $i$. For the longitudinal channel, the box is $[u_{i,\min}, u_{i,\max}]$ where the bounds are tightened at every cycle from the vehicle's speed-dependent physical acceleration limits $a_{\min}(v_x)$ and $a_{\max}(v_x)$ obtained from the powertrain torque map and brake capacity. For the lateral channel, the box is $[\delta_{\min}, \delta_{\max}] = [-0.436, 0.436]\,\mathrm{rad}$ (approximately $\pm 25^\circ$), which corresponds to the mechanical steering-rack limits of the ego vehicle.

\paragraph{Rate constraints.} Adjacent elements of $D_i$ are constrained to differ by no more than a per-step rate limit:
\begin{equation}
\left| u_i[k+j] - u_i[k+j-1] \right| \leq \Delta u_i^{\max} \cdot T_s, \qquad j = 1, \ldots, N_u - 1
\label{eq:rate_constraint}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta u_i^{\max}$ is the per-second rate limit of player $i$ and $T_s$ is the Nash time step. For the longitudinal channel, $\Delta u_1^{\max} = 15\,\mathrm{m/s^3}$ (system) and $\Delta u_2^{\max} = 4\,\mathrm{m/s^3}$ (human, tightened to smooth the driver's inter-cycle response). For the lateral channel, the per-step rate limit is derived at runtime from a jerk cap:
\begin{equation}
\Delta \delta^{\max} = \frac{j^{\text{lat}}_{\max} \cdot T_s}{v_x}
\label{eq:lat_rate_from_jerk}
\end{equation}
where $j^{\text{lat}}_{\max}$ is the lateral jerk cap ($5\,\mathrm{m/s^3}$ for the human, $7\,\mathrm{m/s^3}$ for the system, following the UN ECE-R79 §5.6.2--5.6.4 lateral-jerk envelope), $T_s$ is the lateral Nash time step, and $v_x$ is the current longitudinal speed. The speed-dependent form converts the physically meaningful jerk limit into a steering-angle rate limit that tightens as the vehicle speeds up.

The additional first-step rate constraint $|u_i[k] - u_i^{\text{prev}}| \leq \Delta u_i^{\max} \cdot T_s$ preserves continuity with the last cycle's applied command.

\subsection{OSQP Solver Configuration}
\label{sec:osqp_config}

Both channels use the OSQP first-order splitting solver with warm starting enabled. The channel-specific tolerances and iteration caps are given in Table~\ref{tab:osqp_settings}.

Two distinct iteration counts appear in this section, and it is important to distinguish them. The IBR cap $\iota_{\max} = 15$ defined in Section~\ref{sec:ibr_loop} bounds the number of \emph{outer} alternations between the two players' QPs. The OSQP maximum iterations in Table~\ref{tab:osqp_settings} bound the number of \emph{inner} ADMM iterations that each individual per-player QP solve runs within one IBR alternation. A single Nash cycle therefore performs at most $\iota_{\max} = 15$ outer IBR alternations, each invoking two OSQP solves (one per player) that themselves run up to $1000$ (longitudinal) or $600$ (lateral) internal iterations.

\subsection{Algorithm Summary}
\label{sec:algorithm_summary}

The full solve routine executed at every Nash cycle is summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:ibr_nash}. The routine is identical for the longitudinal and lateral channels; the two channels differ only in the state-space model, weight matrices, constraint values, and the $\lambda$-level grid described above.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Nash Equilibrium via Iterative Best Response with Pre-Computed $\lambda$ Grid}
\label{alg:ibr_nash}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Initialization (once, or on re-linearization):}
\State Build prediction matrices $\mathbf{U}, \mathbf{H}$ from $(\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C})$ (Section~\ref{sec:dmpc}).
\State Build shared Hessian $\mathbf{H}^{\top} \bar{Q}_1 \mathbf{H}$; regularize by Eq.~\eqref{eq:reg}.
\State Build $P_1$ and instantiate one OSQP solver for Player~1.
\For{each $\Lambda_i \in \Lambda$}
    \State Compute $\alpha_i = 1/(1 + \Lambda_i)$.
    \State Build $P_2(\alpha_i)$ per Eq.~\eqref{eq:qp_player2} and instantiate one OSQP solver.
\EndFor
\State
\State \textbf{Runtime (each Nash cycle $k$):}
\State \textbf{Input:} $\mathbf{x}[k]$, references $\mathbf{R}_1, \mathbf{R}_2$, authority $\lambda[k]$, previous applied $u_1^{\text{prev}}, u_2^{\text{prev}}$.
\State Snap: $i^{*} \leftarrow \arg\min_i |\ln \Lambda_i - \ln \lambda[k]|$ per Eq.~\eqref{eq:lambda_snap}.
\State Compute free errors: $\mathbf{e}_i^{\text{free}} \leftarrow \mathbf{R}_i - \mathbf{U} \mathbf{x}[k]$ for $i = 1, 2$.
\State Update box and rate bounds on both solvers from $u_i^{\text{prev}}$ and (for lateral) $v_x$.
\State Load warm-start sequences $(D_1^{(0)}, D_2^{(0)})$ from horizon shift of previous cycle.
\For{$\iota = 1, \ldots, \iota_{\max}$}
    \State $q_1 \leftarrow -2 (\mathbf{H}^{\top} \bar{Q}_1 \mathbf{e}_1^{\text{free}} - \mathbf{H}^{\top} \bar{Q}_1 \mathbf{H} \, D_2^{(\iota-1)})$; update Player~1 OSQP linear cost.
    \State $D_1^{(\iota)} \leftarrow$ OSQP solve of Eq.~\eqref{eq:qp_player1}.
    \State $q_2 \leftarrow -2 \alpha_{i^{*}} (\mathbf{H}^{\top} \bar{Q}_1 \mathbf{e}_2^{\text{free}} - \mathbf{H}^{\top} \bar{Q}_1 \mathbf{H} \, D_1^{(\iota)})$; update Player~2 OSQP linear cost.
    \State $D_2^{(\iota)} \leftarrow$ OSQP solve of Eq.~\eqref{eq:qp_player2} at level $i^{*}$.
    \State $\Delta^{(\iota)} \leftarrow \max(\|D_1^{(\iota)} - D_1^{(\iota-1)}\|_\infty, \; \|D_2^{(\iota)} - D_2^{(\iota-1)}\|_\infty)$.
    \If{$\Delta^{(\iota)} < \varepsilon_{\text{IBR}}$} \textbf{break} \EndIf
\EndFor
\State Shift $(D_1^{(\iota)}, D_2^{(\iota)})$ one step to warm-start the next cycle.
\State \Return first-step controls $(u_1^{*}[k], u_2^{*}[k]) = (D_1^{(\iota)}[0], D_2^{(\iota)}[0])$.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The per-cycle computation is dominated by the two OSQP solves per IBR iteration. Both solves are warm-started; each takes on the order of hundreds of microseconds on a mid-range CPU, so the full IBR loop consistently completes within the $T_s = 0.1\,\mathrm{s}$ longitudinal cycle and the $T_s = 0.05\,\mathrm{s}$ lateral cycle.

\section{Reference Trajectory Generation}
\label{sec:reference_generation}
% ===================================================================

The Nash game of Section~\ref{sec:cost_functions} takes two references per cycle: $\mathbf{r}_1$ for the system (Player~1) and $\mathbf{r}_2$ for the human (Player~2). This section develops the trajectory generators that produce these references at every cycle of the longitudinal and lateral channels. Both channels share the same operating principle: $\mathbf{r}_1$ is derived from the safety-optimal platoon-following objective, while $\mathbf{r}_2$ is derived from a driver-behavior model that projects the human's current intent forward over the prediction horizon.

\subsection{Longitudinal System Reference}
\label{sec:long_sys_ref}

The longitudinal system reference tracks a constant time-gap (CTG) spacing policy relative to the platoon leader, and switches between four regimes based on the current gap error and predicted time-to-collision.

\paragraph{CTG spacing.} The desired inter-vehicle gap grows linearly with the ego speed:
\begin{equation}
d_{\text{des}}[k] = s_0 + h \cdot v_x[k]
\label{eq:ctg_spacing}
\end{equation}
where $s_0 = 2.0\,\mathrm{m}$ is the standstill distance, $h = 1.5\,\mathrm{s}$ is the time headway (Rajamani~\cite{rajamaniVehicleDynamicsControl2005}), and $v_x[k]$ is the current ego longitudinal speed. When no explicit leader is present (join-before scenarios), a virtual leader at target speed replaces the missing vehicle, so the reference generator always has a well-defined tracking target.

\paragraph{Regime selection.} Given the current gap $g[k]$ and leader speed $v_L[k]$, the generator selects one of four regimes at every cycle:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Catchup} (leader significantly faster than ego): the reference is set to the leader's current speed for all $N_p$ horizon steps, so the Nash solver sees a sustained velocity error that drives the ego toward the leader's speed.
\item \textbf{Cruise} (gap $> D_{\text{detect}} = 300\,\mathrm{m}$, no meaningful leader interaction): the reference acceleration follows the free-road branch of the intelligent driver model (IDM), $a = a_{\max}^{\text{IDM}} \left( 1 - (v_x / v_0)^{\delta} \right)$, with $a_{\max}^{\text{IDM}} = 0.553\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, $v_0 = v_{\text{plat}} \cdot 1.05$, and $\delta = 4$~\cite{treiberCongestedTrafficStates2000, treiberTrafficFlowDynamics2013}.
\item \textbf{Collision avoidance} ($\text{TTC} < 3\,\mathrm{s}$ or $gap_curr < gap_{\text{crit}}$ or $gap < gap_{\text{safe, dyn}}$): the reference acceleration is set to $a_{\min}(v_x)$, the maximum physically achievable deceleration. Routing hard-braking through the Player~1 reference (rather than through a direct actuator override) keeps the Nash solver producing a bounded and cooperative response consistent with the ISO 11270 §5.4 jerk envelope.
\item \textbf{Transitional} (all other conditions): the acceleration is a Rajamani parabolic target velocity $v_{\text{target}} = v_L + \mathrm{sign}(e_g) \sqrt{2 a_{\text{comf}} |e_g|}$ ($a_{\text{comf}} = 1.5\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$) fed through a proportional velocity controller $a_{\text{parab}} = K_V (v_{\text{target}} - v_x)$, with $K_V = 0.4$. For small gap errors ($|e_g| < 5\,\mathrm{m}$) this parabolic term is quadratically blended with a CTG feedback correction $a_{\text{ctg}} = K_1^{\text{ctg}} e_g + K_2^{\text{ctg}} (v_L - v_x)$, using gains $K_1^{\text{ctg}} = 0.05$ and $K_2^{\text{ctg}} = 0.4$. In \textsc{Following}, if $|e_g| < 3\,\mathrm{m}$ the gap term is skipped entirely and the reference tracks the leader's velocity, which prevents Player~1 from generating a non-zero acceleration when the driver has released the pedal near the target gap.
\end{itemize}
The regime output is the reference acceleration $a_{\text{ref}}[k+j]$, which is integrated forward together with the leader's constant-velocity prediction $x_L[k+j] = x_L[k] + v_L[k] \cdot j \cdot T_s$ to produce the horizon reference $\mathbf{r}_1 = (p_{\text{ref}}[k+j], v_{\text{ref}}[k+j])_{j=1}^{N_p}$.

\subsection{Longitudinal Human Reference}
\label{sec:long_human_ref}

The human reference $\mathbf{r}_2$ for the longitudinal channel projects the driver's current pedal input forward using a decayed auto-regressive process that converges to an IDM attractor. Three empirical findings from the driver-behavior literature shape the construction.

\paragraph{Measured entry acceleration.} At the current cycle, the acceleration $a_0$ is either the value produced by the driver's actual throttle and brake input (converted to acceleration through the vehicle's inverse powertrain map), or, in the \textsc{Approach} phase at low speed, the IDM free-road branch that prevents the reference from anchoring at rest. This entry value carries the immediate driver intent into the prediction.

\paragraph{IDM attractor.} The long-term equilibrium acceleration is computed from the current gap and relative velocity through the standard IDM equation:
\begin{equation}
a_{\text{IDM}}[k] = a_{\max}^{\text{IDM}} \left( 1 - \left( \frac{v_x}{v_0} \right)^\delta - \left( \frac{s^{*}(v_x, \Delta v)}{g} \right)^2 \right)
\label{eq:idm_attractor}
\end{equation}
where $a_{\max}^{\text{IDM}} = 0.553\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ is the IDM maximum acceleration, $v_0$ is the desired free-flow speed, $\delta = 4$ is the acceleration exponent, $g$ is the current gap, $\Delta v = v_x - v_L$ is the relative velocity to the leader, and $s^{*}(v_x, \Delta v) = s_0 + v_x T + v_x \Delta v / (2 \sqrt{a_{\max}^{\text{IDM}} \cdot b})$ is the desired dynamical spacing~\cite{treiberCongestedTrafficStates2000}. The comfortable braking deceleration is $b = 1.649\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ and the desired time headway $T$ is the online-calibrated value produced by the driver estimator of Section~\ref{sec:nash_driver_calib}.

\paragraph{AR(1) decay with truncated horizon.} Following Flad et al.~\cite{fladCooperativeSharedControl2017} and Na, Cole and Li~\cite{naTwoNashequilibriumbasedSteering2022}, the reference acceleration transitions from the measured entry value $a_0$ toward $a_{\text{IDM}}$ through a first-order auto-regressive process with time constant $\tau = 1.51\,\mathrm{s}$:
\begin{equation}
a_{\text{ref}}[k+j] = \alpha_{\text{AR}} \cdot a_{\text{ref}}[k+j-1] + (1 - \alpha_{\text{AR}}) \cdot a_{\text{IDM}}[k], \qquad j = 1, \ldots, 5
\label{eq:ar1_decay}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_{\text{AR}} = \exp(-T_s / \tau) \approx 0.936$ is the per-step decay factor at $T_s = 0.1\,\mathrm{s}$ and $a_{\text{ref}}[k+0] \equiv a_0$. Beyond step $j = 5$ ($\approx 0.5\,\mathrm{s}$ into the horizon), the reference is frozen at $a_{\text{ref}}[k+5]$; the empirical driver preview horizon of approximately half a second means that projecting the AR(1) further would produce far-fetched long-range intent~\cite{naTwoNashequilibriumbasedSteering2022}. The resulting acceleration profile is integrated forward to produce the position and velocity sequences that populate $\mathbf{r}_2 = (p_{\text{ref},2}[k+j], v_{\text{ref},2}[k+j])_{j=1}^{N_p}$.

\subsection{Lateral System Reference}
\label{sec:lat_sys_ref}

The lateral system reference generates smooth lane-change trajectories using Hermite polynomials that are locked to the maneuver entry state and rolled forward in wall-clock time. Two polynomial orders are used depending on the phase.

\paragraph{Quintic Hermite (Merge).} During the \textsc{Merge} phase, a fifth-order Hermite polynomial produces the reference lateral position with matched entry velocity:
\begin{equation}
y_{\text{ref}}(\tau) = y_0 + v_{y,0} T_{\text{lc}} \, \tau + c_3 \tau^3 + c_4 \tau^4 + c_5 \tau^5, \qquad \tau = t / T_{\text{lc}} \in [0, 1]
\label{eq:quintic_merge}
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ is the normalized time, $t$ is the elapsed time since \textsc{Merge} entry, $T_{\text{lc}}$ is the total lane-change duration set at entry, $y_0$ is the lateral position at entry, $v_{y,0}$ is the lateral velocity at entry, and the coefficients $c_3 = 10 \Delta y - 6 v_{y,0} T_{\text{lc}}$, $c_4 = -15 \Delta y + 8 v_{y,0} T_{\text{lc}}$, $c_5 = 6 \Delta y - 3 v_{y,0} T_{\text{lc}}$ are derived from the boundary conditions $y(0) = y_0$, $\dot{y}(0) = v_{y,0}$, $\ddot{y}(0) = 0$, $y(T_{\text{lc}}) = y_0 + \Delta y$, $\dot{y}(T_{\text{lc}}) = \ddot{y}(T_{\text{lc}}) = 0$. The reference heading is $\psi_{\text{ref}}(\tau) = \dot{y}_{\text{ref}}(\tau) / v_x$, the small-angle projection of the polynomial's tangent onto the road frame~\cite{rajamaniVehicleDynamicsControl2005}. Setting the entry velocity $v_{y,0}$ from the measured lateral velocity, rather than fixing it to zero, means that the polynomial matches the vehicle's actual state at handover and avoids the initial-tangent discontinuity that a boundary-trivial quintic $\Delta y (10\tau^3 - 15\tau^4 + 6\tau^5)$ would introduce.

\paragraph{Cubic Hermite (Approach, GapSearch, Following settle).} During the \textsc{Approach} and \textsc{GapSearch} phases, and during the residual lateral settling in \textsc{Following}, a cubic Hermite polynomial is used:
\begin{equation}
y_{\text{ref}}(\tau) = y_0 + v_{y,0} T \, \tau + (3 \Delta y - 2 v_{y,0} T) \tau^2 + (- 2 \Delta y + v_{y,0} T) \tau^3
\label{eq:cubic_gapsearch}
\end{equation}
where $T$ is the settling duration for the corresponding phase. The cubic order allows the boundary conditions on position and velocity to be satisfied without over-constraining the second derivative, which suits the small offsets typical of these phases.

\paragraph{Lane-change duration.} The duration $T_{\text{lc}}$ is set at \textsc{Merge} entry from the current lateral state, subject to two constraints:
\begin{equation}
T_{\text{lc}}^{\text{sys}} = \max\!\left( T_{\min}, \; T_{\text{phys}} \right), \qquad T_{\text{phys}} = \begin{cases}
\dfrac{2 |\Delta y|}{v_{y,0}} & v_{y,0} \cdot \mathrm{sign}(\Delta y) > 0.05\,\mathrm{m/s} \\[6pt]
\dfrac{1.875 \, |\Delta y|}{v_x \, \tan \psi_{\max}} & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:tlc_selection}
\end{equation}
where $T_{\min} = 4\,\mathrm{s}$ is the absolute floor duration, $T_{\text{phys}}$ is the physically feasible duration under either the natural approach (when the lateral velocity already points toward the target) or the heading-constrained geometric fallback, and $\psi_{\max} = 4^\circ$ is the maximum heading angle allowed during the quintic maneuver. The coefficient $1.875$ in the geometric fallback is derived from the maximum $\dot{y}$ of the quintic $y = \Delta y (10\tau^3 - 15\tau^4 + 6\tau^5)$, which occurs at $\tau = 1/2$ and equals $1.875 \, \Delta y / T_{\text{lc}}$. The system reference thus guarantees $|\psi(\tau)| \leq \psi_{\max}$ throughout the maneuver, independent of the entry lateral velocity.

\subsection{Lateral Human Reference}
\label{sec:lat_human_ref}

The lateral human reference uses the same cubic and quintic Hermite structure as the system reference but with a tighter heading bound $\psi_{\max}^{\text{hum}} = 2.6^\circ$, following the naturalistic-lane-change data of Lee and Olsen~\cite{leeComprehensiveExaminationNaturalistic2004a}. The human duration is reduced correspondingly:
\begin{equation}
T_{\text{lc}}^{\text{hum}} = \max\!\left( T_{\min}, \; T_{\text{phys}} \cdot \tfrac{1.5}{1.875} \right)
\label{eq:tlc_human}
\end{equation}
where the factor $1.5$ is the cubic maximum-$\dot{y}$ coefficient (analogous to the quintic $1.875$), and $T_{\min} = 4\,\mathrm{s}$ is the same absolute floor. The ratio $1.5 / 1.875 \approx 0.8$ therefore makes the human reference shorter than the system reference, reflecting the observation that human drivers execute lane changes more directly with less emphasis on smoothness at the endpoints~\cite{guHumanlikeMotionPlanning2014, hoffmannAutonomousAutomobileTrajectory2007}. The polynomial coefficients are otherwise identical to those of the system reference.

The implementation does not include categorical driver-type presets (cautious, normal, aggressive) for the lateral reference, in contrast to the earlier formulations reported by Li et al.~\cite{liSharedControlNovel2019}. Driver individuality enters the lateral channel through the MOBIL politeness parameter that gates the lane-change decision~\cite{kestingGeneralLaneChangingModel2007} and through the online-calibrated longitudinal driver model of Section~\ref{sec:nash_driver_calib}; per-driver tuning of the lateral polynomial coefficients was found to be neither necessary nor observable during interactive runs with a Logitech G29 wheel.

\subsection{MOBIL Lane-Change Decision}
\label{sec:mobil_decision}

The transition from lateral gap search to the active merge maneuver is gated by the MOBIL lane-change decision model of Kesting, Treiber, and Helbing~\cite{kestingGeneralLaneChangingModel2007, kestingMOBILGeneralLaneChanging}. MOBIL is invoked at every Nash cycle of the \textsc{GapSearch} phase, and its decision determines whether the target lateral gap is safe enough to commit to the quintic merge trajectory of Eq.~\eqref{eq:quintic_merge}. Only after MOBIL approves and the longitudinal state has synchronized to the platoon does the phase machine transition to \textsc{Merge}.

\paragraph{Safety criterion.} The new follower behind the ego (once merged into the target lane) must not need to brake harder than a comfort threshold to maintain its own gap:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{a}_n \geq -b_{\text{safe}}
\label{eq:mobil_safety}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{a}_n$ is the IDM acceleration the new follower would produce if the ego were placed in the target lane at its current position and velocity, and $b_{\text{safe}} = 4\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ is the comfort deceleration bound. If no follower is present in the target lane, the criterion is trivially satisfied.

\paragraph{Gap criterion.} The physical gaps to the front and rear neighbors in the target lane must exceed a hard minimum $s_{\min}^{\text{mobil}} = 8\,\mathrm{m}$:
\begin{equation}
g_{\text{front}} = x_{\text{new}_\text{leader}} - x_{\text{ego}} - L \geq s_{\min}^{\text{mobil}}, \qquad g_{\text{rear}} = x_{\text{ego}} - x_{\text{new}_\text{follower}} - L \geq s_{\min}^{\text{mobil}}
\label{eq:mobil_gap}
\end{equation}
where $x_{\text{ego}}$ is the ego longitudinal position, $x_{\text{new}_\text{leader}}$ and $x_{\text{new}_\text{follower}}$ are the longitudinal positions of the target-lane front and rear neighbors, and $L$ is the ego vehicle length. The gap check prevents a merge into an insufficient physical opening even when the IDM-based safety criterion of Eq.~\eqref{eq:mobil_safety} would still be satisfied through predictive braking of the new follower.

\paragraph{Mandatory-mode approval.} For platoon merging the model runs in the mandatory-lane-change mode, which drops the incentive check that would otherwise gate discretionary changes:
\begin{equation}
\text{approved} \iff \left( \tilde{a}_n \geq -b_{\text{safe}} \right) \; \land \; \left( g_{\text{front}} \geq s_{\min}^{\text{mobil}} \right) \; \land \; \left( g_{\text{rear}} \geq s_{\min}^{\text{mobil}} \right)
\label{eq:mobil_approval}
\end{equation}
where the three conjuncts are the safety criterion (Eq.~\eqref{eq:mobil_safety}) and the two gap criteria (Eq.~\eqref{eq:mobil_gap}). The mandatory formulation reflects the operational context: the driver has already committed to joining the platoon by pressing the merge trigger, so no additional utility gain (over remaining in the current lane) is required.

\paragraph{Neighbor identification.} The ego's target-lane neighbors are determined at every check by sorting the platoon vehicles along the longitudinal axis and locating the ego's position relative to the sorted sequence. Three positional relationships are recognized: the ego may be ahead of the platoon (only a follower is defined), behind the platoon (only a leader is defined), or bracketed between two consecutive platoon vehicles (both neighbors are defined). This handles the three canonical merge geometries (join-before, join-after, and join-middle) with a single check.

\paragraph{Coupling to phase transition.} MOBIL approval is a necessary but not sufficient condition for the \textsc{GapSearch}~$\to$~\textsc{Merge} transition. The phase machine additionally requires that the longitudinal velocity error to the immediate target-lane leader falls below a threshold ($|v_{\text{ego}} - v_L| < 5\,\mathrm{m/s}$) and that the relative gap error is below $40\%$ of the desired CTG spacing, both held for a minimum dwell time of $2\,\mathrm{s}$ in \textsc{GapSearch}. Once all three conditions co-occur, the lateral reference generator captures the current lateral state as the polynomial entry point (Eq.~\eqref{eq:quintic_merge}) and the phase transitions to \textsc{Merge}.

\subsection{Phase-Dependent Reference Selection}
\label{sec:phase_reference}

The phase state machine of Chapter~3 gates which reference-generation branch is active. Four phases are defined:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Approach}: The ego is under full manual control. The Nash solver is warm-started but does not act on the vehicle; the reference generators run at the physics rate to keep the warm-start iterates consistent with the current driver input.
\item \textbf{GapSearch}: A merge has been armed but the target longitudinal gap is not yet established. The lateral reference regulates heading toward $\psi \to 0$ via the cubic polynomial with $\Delta y = 0$, and the longitudinal reference operates in the transitional regime.
\item \textbf{Merge}: The target gap has been reached and the lane change is executing. The lateral reference switches to the quintic Hermite of Eq.~\eqref{eq:quintic_merge}, and the longitudinal reference tracks the platoon leader through the CTG spacing.
\item \textbf{Following}: The ego has joined the platoon. Both references switch to steady-state platoon following, with the small residual lateral settling using the cubic Hermite of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cubic_gapsearch}.
\end{enumerate}

The five-phase decomposition sometimes used in the shared-control literature (cruise, gap search, lane change, lane keeping, following) is here compressed to four by merging the cruise and approach regimes into a single manual-control phase, and by treating lane keeping as the initial subphase of following~\cite{kestingGeneralLaneChangingModel2007, kestingMOBILGeneralLaneChanging}. Phase transitions are detected by the MOBIL lane-change model and the driving safety field described in Chapter~3.

\section{Longitudinal versus Lateral: Comparison Summary}
\label{sec:comparison}

The longitudinal and lateral Nash controllers share the same algorithmic skeleton: linearized state-space model, quadratic cost with authority-scaled human tracking weight, Iterative Best Response over a pre-factored $\lambda$ grid, and reference generation split between a system and a human channel. They differ in the state dimension, the time step, the numerical weights, and the driver-modeling mechanism. This section summarizes the differences across four families of parameters and comments on the design choices that motivate each split.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:ch4_summary}
% ===================================================================

This chapter developed the game-theoretic framework for shared control during platoon merging, in both the longitudinal and lateral channels. The Nash game is formulated as a two-player finite-horizon quadratic optimization on the linearized vehicle dynamics of Chapter~2. Each player minimizes its own quadratic cost against its own reference trajectory, and coordination between the two players is produced by a scalar authority scaling of the human's tracking weight rather than by explicit cross-coupling matrices. The equilibrium is computed by an Iterative Best Response loop over a pre-factored $\lambda$ grid, which preserves real-time performance without sacrificing the game-theoretic structure. The main methodological choices are summarized below.

\paragraph{Pustilnik--Borrelli authority mapping.} The authority ratio $\lambda[k]$ produced by the allocator of Section~\ref{sec:authority_allocation} enters the Nash game through the scaling factor $\alpha[k] = 1/(1 + \lambda[k])$ applied to the human's tracking weight $Q_2[k] = \alpha[k] \, Q_1$~\cite{pustilnikGeneralizedNashEquilibrium2025}. This mapping is bounded ($\alpha \in (0, 1]$) and monotonically decreasing, which guarantees positive-definiteness of Player~2's Hessian for every $\lambda \geq 0$ and preserves numerical stability of the solver across the full authority range.

\paragraph{Iterative Best Response with pre-factored $\lambda$ grid.} Rather than reformulating the equilibrium as a stacked QP, the solver alternates two per-player OSQP calls at every Nash cycle. Each Player~2 solve reuses one of eleven pre-factored problem instances (seven for the lateral channel), one per discrete $\lambda$ level, selected by nearest-neighbor snap in log-space~\cite{pustilnikGeneralizedNashEquilibrium2025}. The construction removes the most expensive numerical step of the QP setup from the real-time execution path and keeps the per-cycle computation bounded independently of $\lambda[k]$.

\paragraph{Heading-constrained lane-change references.} The lateral system reference uses a quintic Hermite polynomial locked to the maneuver entry state, with the total lane-change duration $T_{\text{lc}}$ set at entry to guarantee $|\psi(\tau)| \leq \psi_{\max}$ throughout the horizon (Section~\ref{sec:lat_sys_ref})~\cite{guHumanlikeMotionPlanning2014}. The polynomial matches the vehicle's actual lateral velocity at entry rather than assuming a zero-velocity handover, which eliminates the initial-tangent discontinuity that a boundary-trivial quintic would introduce.

\paragraph{Empirically grounded human reference.} The longitudinal human reference is generated by an AR(1) decay from the measured pedal-derived acceleration toward an IDM attractor whose time-gap parameter is calibrated online during the run (Section~\ref{sec:long_human_ref})~\cite{fladCooperativeSharedControl2017, naTwoNashequilibriumbasedSteering2022, zhangBayesianCalibrationIntelligent2024}. The prediction is truncated at approximately half a second, in accordance with the observed driver preview horizon reported in the naturalistic-driving literature. The lateral human reference uses a cubic Hermite polynomial with a tighter heading bound $\psi_{\max}^{\text{hum}} = 2.6^\circ$~\cite{leeComprehensiveExaminationNaturalistic2004a}, reflecting the more direct trajectories that human drivers execute during lane changes.

\paragraph{MOBIL-gated phase transition.} The transition from lateral gap search to the active merge maneuver is gated by the MOBIL mandatory-lane-change criterion, applied at every Nash cycle to the target-lane neighbors (Section~\ref{sec:mobil_decision})~\cite{kestingGeneralLaneChangingModel2007}. The criterion combines an IDM-based safety check on the new follower with a hard gap floor, which prevents the merge from committing to an infeasible opening even when the local safety margin would formally admit predictive braking.

The longitudinal control modules include \texttt{longitudinal\_constrained\_nash\_solver.py} for the Nash equilibrium solver with DMPC, \texttt{longitudinal\_safety\_field.py} for bidirectional safety field computation, \texttt{longitudinal\_authority\_allocator.py} for dynamic authority allocation, \texttt{system\_reference\_generator.py} for CTG-based reference trajectories, and \texttt{main\_with\_nash.py} for complete system integration and simulation.

The lateral control modules include \texttt{lateral\_constrained\_nash\_solver.py} for the lateral Nash equilibrium solver, \texttt{lateral\_safety\_field.py} for ellipse-based collision detection, \texttt{lateral\_authority\_allocator.py} for sigmoid-based authority allocation with rate limiting, \texttt{system\_reference\_generator.py} for quintic polynomial reference trajectories, \texttt{human\_reference\_generator.py} for cubic polynomial human driver references, \texttt{vehicle.py} implementing the dual-model bicycle architecture (Section~\ref{sec:dual_model}), \texttt{unified\_lateral\_controller.py} for complete lateral control integration, and \texttt{main\_with\_menu.py} for interactive simulation with scenario selection.
